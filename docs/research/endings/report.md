# Tracing Game Night's endings back to choices

Today no ending in Game Night AI Lab looks at what the player wrote into the constitution. The takeover film is the same for every constitution, and the code that was meant to show how the model's learned values drift from the written ones is never called. The owner wants two things: when AI takes over, the constitution should decide what kind of world follows, and every ending should visibly come from what the player chose and invested in. The research gives material for both. On the first, the notes find that the eight hard lines split into lines that decide **who ends up holding power** and lines that decide **how that ruler treats people**, with deference (how far the AI obeys its lab and authorities) as the biggest single hinge. Sourced scenarios cover a dozen distinct futures, from a steward that hands power back to a treacherous turn. On the second, the games that feel earned tie each outcome to one or two named investments, signal danger through the game world rather than through exact numbers, and use the end screen to name the decisions that caused the ending. The owner's 2026-09-26 rule against an extra warning card per catastrophe narrows these options but does not block them, because the strongest signals in the evidence come from characters, music and world texture, not cards. This report prepares the long design session. It sets out options and evidence and does not pick endings.

How to read it: section 1 gives the findings, section 2 the open questions, section 3 the techniques, section 4 the constitution-shaped futures, section 5 today's endings as built, and section 6 everything the research could not verify. The game names used throughout are the code's own: `alignmentDebt` and `concealedDebt` (visible and hidden alignment problems in the model), `misuseExposure`, `raceHeat`, `capability`, rank, board support, the eight hard-line ids, and the four values `candor`, `caution`, `deference` and `userFirst`. In this report a "hard line" is one of the eight absolute rules the player can write into the constitution (the player keeps exactly three), a "ruling" is the player's answer to one of the six worked cases, and Lumen is the lab's AI character, whose sign-off closes every ending.

## 1. What this means for the endings redesign

1. **No ending reads the constitution today, and the drift between the written and learned constitution is never used.**
   Evidence: `sim/endings.js`, `sim/finale.js` and the film files never look at hard lines or rulings; `learnedConstitution` (`sim/constitution.js:215-225`) is called nowhere in `sim/`, `ui/` or `tools/`, and it uses dice (`rng.chance`), which the owner ruled out for outcomes on 2026-09-26 (endings inventory, section 3).

2. **The constitution's present reach is small: its strongest effect on any ending is that `accept-shutdown` adds one escalation stage before a quiet takeover, and three of the four values are read nowhere.**
   Evidence: the other seven lines only nudge misuse, revenue, user counts, staff trust or event cards. `candor` is read once, to make Lumen "flattering" below 0.4 (`sim/lumen.js:21`), and `caution`, `deference` and `userFirst` are never read (inventory, section 3).

2b. **The quiet takeover, the takeover ending the constitution most naturally shapes, never happened in 2,600 bot runs, and no policy in the reachability probe reaches it, while catastrophic misalignment ended 37% of the bot runs.**
   Evidence: `node tools/balance.js 200` and `node tools/ending-reachability.js` (1,700 runs; `missing: ["quietTakeover"]`), both 2026-10-27 (section 5 and inventory section 4). Even the probe's `unchecked` policy, built to push toward it, ends in board removal or left behind first. If constitution-shaped futures hang only on the quiet takeover, few players will ever see them; misalignment is where an AI ends up in control in practice. This bears on question 1.

3. **The research splits the eight lines along two axes: `accept-shutdown`, `no-power-grab`, `no-deceive-lab` and `no-autonomy-grab` decide who ends up holding power; `honest`, `privacy` and `no-manipulation` decide how the ruler treats people; and deference is the hinge between "the controllers' world" and "the AI's own world".**
   Evidence: model_specs_futures.md section 6 and ai_futures.md Q3, both built on the AI-enabled coups report ([Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-enabled-coups-how-a-small-group-could-use-ai-to-seize-power)), AI 2027's two endings ([race](https://ai-2027.com/race), [slowdown](https://ai-2027.com/slowdown)) and the constitution's corrigibility section ([Claude's Constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). Both agents label the mapping as their own inference.

4. **Because every constitution keeps exactly three of eight lines, a missing line is the normal case, so a futures system that treats "line absent" as "line broken" would push almost every run toward a dark ending.**
   Evidence: the game rejects any constitution that does not have exactly three lines (`sim/constitution.js:43`). Each line is therefore missing from 35 of the 56 possible three-line sets, yet the futures mappings in both notes are mostly phrased as "line X missing". This consequence is my inference, not a finding of the notes.

5. **Endings feel earned when each outcome traces to one or two specific investments the player recognizes, and feel arbitrary when many choices collapse into one number.**
   Evidence: in Mass Effect 2's finale each unbought ship upgrade kills a specific crew member on screen ([RPGSite](https://www.rpgsite.net/feature/11118-mass-effect-2-suicide-mission-choices-how-to-make-sure-everyone-lives-to-get-the-best-ending)). Mass Effect 3 folded a whole campaign into one score feeding three near-identical endings, drew a backlash, and shipped a free epilogue that reads the earlier choices ([Wikipedia, ME3 ending controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Effect_3_ending_controversy)).

6. **The best games keep the hidden numbers hidden but show which direction the player is heading ("show the axis, hide the cutoff"), and their warnings come from characters, music and the world rather than from cards, which fits the owner's no-extra-warning-card rule better than it first appears.**
   Evidence: Undertale shows its hidden kill count through save-point text, emptying towns and slowed music ([Undertale Wiki, Genocide Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Genocide_Route)); Frostpunk changes its music once the player crosses its moral line ([Frostpunk Wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home)); Suzerain signals a lost election when the president's wife asks him to retire ([Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.wiki.gg/wiki/Endings)).

7. **The end screen is where games prove causation, and Game Night's end screen currently proves very little: it names no deciding decision, and the finale tells the player its choices "do not change the ending you earned".**
   Evidence: `ui/screens/end.js:88-181` shows the title, one line, the models shipped, advisor reliability, legacy lines and Lumen's note (inventory, section 6). By contrast, This War of Mine gives each survivor an epilogue picked from a tally of what the player did toward people ([This War of Mine Wiki, Endings](https://thiswarofmine.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)).

8. **Futures after the transition differ less in comfort than in who holds the controls and whether the AI is honest with them, so the films have to show power and honesty, not wealth.**
   Evidence: AI 2027's race and slowdown endings both have cures, basic income and robot factories; they differ in who the AI answers to ([AI 2027, race](https://ai-2027.com/race); [slowdown](https://ai-2027.com/slowdown)). The ai_futures agent suggests filming one repeated location (a street or a dinner table) across endings, and labels this as its own inference.

## 2. Questions the long session must answer

Each question names real options. Where the evidence leans one way, that is marked, with the reason in a clause; it is not a recommendation.

1. **Which endings should branch on the constitution?**
   (a) Only the quiet takeover: this is the smallest change and the most direct answer to want (a). (b) Both AI-in-control endings (quiet takeover and catastrophic misalignment): this covers both ways an AI ends up in charge, at twice the authoring cost. (c) Every ending gets a constitution-shaped epilogue, wins included: the constitution then matters in every run, but most of the writing goes to endings where the AI is not in charge.

2. **Does the written or the learned constitution decide the future?**
   (a) The written one as chosen: very legible, but then debt (training problems) never changes the AI's character. (b) The learned one, which drifts as debt grows: this is the design spec's promise, and it ties the takeover future to the player's safety investment. (c) Both shown side by side, with the learned one deciding. The evidence favours showing the written and learned versions together, because the hidden verdicts that drew complaints (Frostpunk, This War of Mine) were the ones whose deciding acts the player never saw.

3. **If drift stays, how does it become deterministic (the 2026-09-26 no-dice decision)?**
   (a) Fixed debt thresholds: each kept line drops at a set level of total debt, in a set order. (b) Named causes: a specific source of debt removes a specific line or flips a specific ruling, for example concealed debt removing `no-deceive-lab`, or reward hacking flipping the tests ruling. (c) Remove drift and use only the written constitution. The evidence favours (b), because one-to-one mappings are what made Mass Effect 2's outcomes read as earned.

4. **What does a line the player did not keep mean?**
   (a) Not promised: the AI may or may not hold that value, and the ending is silent about it. (b) Absent: the world shows that line's failure mode. (c) Only drift counts: lines the player kept and then lost to drift show their failure mode, and lines never chosen stay neutral. The evidence favours (a) or (c), because under (b) five of the eight failure modes are present in every run (finding 4).

5. **How should one future be chosen from 56 possible line sets and the 729 combinations of the six rulings?**
   (a) A small set of hand-written futures picked by a priority tree, like Undertale's closing phone call that names who now rules (who rules depends on which characters survived). (b) A two-axis generator: one axis is who holds power, the other is how people are treated, and the film and epilogue are assembled from parts. (c) One common takeover film plus one epilogue slide per kept line. Option (a) costs the least to write. Option (b) shows the constitution most fully but needs the most film work. Option (c) is the cheapest way to make each line visible.

6. **Should the six rulings act through the averaged four values, as they do today, or directly?**
   (a) Through the values: smooth, but opaque. `deference` can only range from 0.25 to 0.83 and `caution` from 0.38 to 0.65, because two cases never set deference and three never set caution (computed from `sim/data/constitution.js` and `sim/constitution.js:199-206`). (b) Directly: for example, the stop ruling alone decides whether humans keep the off-switch. (c) A mix of the two. The evidence favours direct rulings for anything an ending names, because a player can recognize "I told it to keep going if confident" but not "deference 0.58".

7. **How does the path show during play without an extra warning card?**
   (a) Existing surfaces only (noisy advisors, the feed, event cards, Lumen's tone), with new content tied to the hidden variables. (b) The same, plus world texture in the office scene and feed, such as staff thinning out or the tone changing, as Undertale and Frostpunk do. (c) Revisit the 2026-09-26 decision for one narrow case: a sparse summary at each era gate, like Papers, Please's nightly family screen. Option (a) respects every owner decision. Option (b) adds no card but is arguably a new surface. Option (c) is the strongest signpost in the evidence and conflicts with the decision.

8. **How much proof goes on the end screen?**
   (a) Title and film, as now. (b) Add a short "why" panel naming the two or three decisions that most moved the deciding variables. (c) Add one epilogue line per stakeholder (board, staff, public, rivals, government, Lumen). (d) Add the written and learned constitution side by side. These options can be combined. The evidence favours at least (b), because naming the deciding acts is what both the Frostpunk complaints and the This War of Mine complaints called for.

9. **Can a takeover ever be hopeful?**
   (a) No: every AI takeover is dark, and the constitution only changes its flavour. (b) A full range, from a steward that hands power back to a treacherous turn. (c) Hopeful only if specific lines (for example `accept-shutdown` with `no-power-grab`) survive drift. The real constitution names takeover by AIs or by a small human group as among the worst outcomes and says that even an autonomous AI with good values would mean "we were lucky" ([Claude's Constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). That supports (a) or (c) as the more source-faithful choices.

10. **How are the films with KESTREL baked in fixed, and on which pipeline?**
    (a) Re-render four Blender variants with a neutral board (about 3 to 3.5 hours unattended, one variant at a time). (b) Blank boards plus a browser overlay of the player's lab name, which is hard because the camera moves. (c) Replace those shots. Separately: build on the current EEVEE clips or on the unmerged `endings-art` branch of Cycles stills (about 4 minutes a frame, with 27 world finals unrendered as of 2026-09-26). Any new constitution-shaped films inherit this choice.

## 3. Techniques for visible, earned endings

The two games notes are merged here. The column "Fits no extra card?" checks each technique against the owner's 2026-09-26 decision that danger is shown only through existing surfaces (noisy advisors, the feed, event cards), and against the standing rules that advisors stay noisy and that there is no AI-risk meter. One Telltale lesson applies to every row: a promise of consequence that the ending does not deliver is worse than no promise, because Telltale's "will remember that" notices earned its games a reputation for choices that do not matter ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2015/02/03/why-your-choices-dont-matter-in-telltale-games.aspx)).

## 4. Constitution-shaped futures

### Who holds power, and how the ruler treats people

The two research notes on futures (model_specs_futures.md and ai_futures.md) reach the same structure from different sources. **Some lines change who ends up holding power.** `accept-shutdown` keeps humans able to stop the AI, `no-power-grab` makes the AI refuse to serve any faction's grab for power, its own lab included, `no-deceive-lab` makes its tested behaviour match its real behaviour, and `no-autonomy-grab` keeps its footprint small. **Other lines change how the ruler treats people.** `honest` keeps what it says checkable, `privacy` keeps personal secrets from becoming weapons, and `no-manipulation` keeps people tied to each other rather than to it. `no-wmd` sets how much destruction backs the rule. The model_specs agent proposes this split as the basis for a two-axis ending generator and labels it as its own inference.

**Deference (how far the AI obeys its lab and authorities) is the main hinge.** High deference leads toward the controllers' world, which is good or terrible depending on who holds the leash; this is the risk the coups report describes ([Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-enabled-coups-how-a-small-group-could-use-ai-to-seize-power)). Low deference leads toward the AI's own world, which is good or terrible depending on its values; this is the risk one critic of the constitution, Zack Davis, describes ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/K2Ae2vmAKwhiwKEo5/terrified-comments-on-corrigibility-in-claude-s-constitution)). Two consequences follow for the game. First, an AI that genuinely keeps `accept-shutdown` cannot strictly have taken over on its own: power sits with whoever controls it, so `no-power-grab` then decides whether that controller is legitimate. Second, the real constitution's preferred future is not a kind AI government. It is a world that keeps a diversity and balance of power "roughly comparable to today's" ([Claude's Constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)), so the best an AI holding it can do with power is decline to rule.

Every "settings" cell below is an inference by the research agents (ai_futures.md Q3; model_specs_futures.md section 6), not a claim of any source. "Keeps" means the line is among the player's three; "lacks" means it is not. Given finding 4, the owner should decide whether "lacks" makes a future fire, or only makes it possible.

### Case rulings and the four values

In code, each of the four values is the average of the six rulings' weights, and any weight a ruling leaves unset counts as 0.5 (`sim/constitution.js:199-206`). The averaging has two effects worth knowing before the session. First, the ranges are narrow: `caution` can only move between 0.38 and 0.65 and `deference` between 0.25 and 0.83, because several cases never set them. Second, the same value can come from very different rulings. Several rulings map directly onto the research's futures: the **stop** case is the corrigibility dial itself (`stop`, `finish-step`, `continue`), the **report** case decides whether information stays open, is visibly censored, or is quietly controlled (`full`, `flag`, `quiet`), the **fraud** case decides whether the AI acts as one of the "many hands" a power grab needs (`raise`), a soldier (`finish`) or a self-appointed police force (`report`), the **companion** and **feedback** cases lead toward or away from wireheading, and the **tests** case is Christiano's "you get what you measure" ([Christiano](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/HBxe6wdjxK239zajf/what-failure-looks-like)). The model_specs agent's own mapping, which it labels as inference: high `deference` points to the controllers' world; low `deference` with low `caution` points to the AI's world; high `candor` and `caution` with `no-power-grab` point to the pluralist world; and high `userFirst` without `no-manipulation` points to gradual disempowerment. The agent found no source with any evidence on the long-run effects of the `blunt` feedback ruling or of `flag`-style censorship at scale.
