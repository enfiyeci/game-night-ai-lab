# How narrative and choice-driven games make endings follow visibly from earlier choices

Scope: the narrative-game half of the endings evidence for Game Night AI Lab (a 20-minute browser management game, five eras, 11 endings today). Each section asks, for one game: what state does the ending read, how is that state signposted during play, how does the ending prove causation, what did designers and critics say. A cross-cutting section covers legibility versus surprise, then a summary table and a coverage statement.

**How sources were read.** Every page cited below was opened with a fetch tool that passes the whole page text to a summarising model and returns its summary; I did not see raw HTML. When the tool reported that it read only part of a page, the claim carries ⚠️. Claims that rest only on a search-engine snippet (the page itself was never opened or never loaded) also carry ⚠️ and say so. Items I know from general knowledge but could not confirm from a source this session are put in Gaps, marked ⚠️, and never stated as findings.

## Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, 2019): thoughts as identity, a debrief as judge

### Takeaway
Disco Elysium makes the player's identity a set of visible, named objects (Thoughts, including four Ideologies and four Copotypes) that the player chooses to "internalize", and then closes with a debrief where superiors weigh Harry's conduct. The causal link is carried by named labels the player committed to, plus characters who comment on what was done, rather than by a numeric score.

### Cited Findings
- The Thought Cabinet was pitched by lead designer Robert Kurvitz as an "inventory for thoughts" that stands in for perks, traits, class, reputation and alignment all at once. — [GameBanshee, Thought Cabinet dev blog relay, 2019-10-01](http://www.gamebanshee.com/39hc9)
- There are 53 thoughts; a typical character internalizes about 16 per playthrough, so no run sees them all. Each starts face down, showing only its name and some initial text; the full effect is revealed only after internalizing (30 in-world minutes to 3 in-world days). Kurvitz compared the reveal to finishing a wonder in Civilization. — [GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/39hc9)
- Thoughts come from conversation, and skills can generate them from the character's own repeated behaviour (for example repeatedly saying artsy things). Slots are finite; a skill point opens a slot or forgets a thought. Bonuses are often paired with drawbacks. — [GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/39hc9)
- Alignment is handled by the four Ideologies and four Copotypes, "the most powerful thoughts". Ideologies are the closest thing to an alignment system; Copotypes describe how the detective sees himself as a police officer. Combining them yields named personas, e.g. a "socialist Superstar Cop". External reputation is tags that other characters remember; internal reputation is the skills' opinions of the player. — [GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/39hc9)
- Kurvitz wanted deep choices and outcomes, but practical limits pushed the team towards "microreactivity", where small acts ripple through events; skill dialogue was meant to show small decisions having larger effects. Failure is a deliberate design pillar (pass/fail dice; a failing-cop protagonist). The writers acknowledge the central case has no real solution. — ⚠️ [Wikipedia, Disco Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium) (fetch read only the first 100,000 of about 155,000 characters; the unread tail is mostly the reference list, but I cannot confirm that)
- Skill checks roll two six-sided dice plus the skill; high skills also carry quirks that penalize single-minded builds (high Drama detects lies but causes paranoia). — ⚠️ [Wikipedia, Disco Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium) (same partial read)
- Ending: on the island, the squad (Jean Vicquemare, with Judit and Trant Heidelstam) confronts Harry about his actions, including whether he solved the case and how he handled the mercenaries. In the best outcome they invite him, plus Kim or Cuno, to a special RCM unit. Ruby's fate, how many Hardie Boys and mercenaries die, and whether Cuno replaces a hospitalised Kim all vary with play. — ⚠️ [Wikipedia, Disco Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium) (same partial read)
- The debrief starts automatically; Jean is the final decision-maker on whether Harry is cleared to work. The ending animation differs by outcome: Jean helps Harry into the car, or lifts Cuno in, or turns away and leaves Harry in the rain. A final Esprit de Corps passage records other officers' yes/no verdicts. Showing Jean the photo of the Insulindian Phasmid (the cryptid side quest) softens him. — [Disco Elysium Wiki (wiki.gg), Jean Vicquemare](https://discoelysium.wiki.gg/wiki/Jean_Vicquemare) (page read to its end; it covers the debrief only briefly)
- Kim gives an honest, unsoftened assessment of Harry's behaviour in the debrief and reportedly comments on political ideology, copotype and karaoke. — ⚠️ search-engine snippet only, from [GamersDecide, Key moments that affect Disco Elysium endings](https://gamersdecide.com/articles/disco-elysium-endings); the page itself was not opened.
- Critics split on choice: Eurogamer criticised limited role-playing choice; PC Gamer praised depth and freedom. — ⚠️ [Wikipedia, Disco Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium) (same partial read)

### Inferences
- The transferable move is **naming the player's accumulated stance and letting them hold it**: an Ideology or Copotype is an object the player chose to equip, so when the ending (or a character) references it, the causation is self-evident. For Game Night, lab "doctrines" (for example "Safety-first lab", "Race-to-the-frontier lab") that the player explicitly adopts in an era, and that the ending names back, would give the same effect cheaply.
- **Face-down until committed** is Disco Elysium's answer to legibility versus surprise: the player sees the name (the direction) but not the payoff until they have invested. That is a pattern a management game can reuse for era-defining investments.
- The debrief works as a **tribunal of characters who saw what you did**; it proves causation by having people with grudges or sympathies cite specific acts. A board or regulator hearing at the end of era 5 is the natural Game Night analogue.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ Political "vision quests" (the long ideology-specific quest lines) and the exact debrief topics list could not be sourced: the Fandom wiki returned HTTP 402 and the wiki.gg "THE RETURN" page returned 404. GameSpot's article on the Thought Cabinet nearly sinking the project returned HTTP 403.
- ⚠️ Not verified this session (general knowledge only): that the UI shows a success percentage before each check, and that "red" checks are one-shot while "white" checks can be retried after raising a skill. If used, these need a source.

## Suzerain (Torpor Games, 2020; Kingdom of Rizia DLC 2024): faction pressure, many named endings, a compass

### Takeaway
Suzerain's endings read a broad state of faction relations, approval, economy, war outcome, corruption and family ties, and the results screen then places the player on an in-universe political compass with statistics. The endings themselves are many and specific (nine main, about twenty sub-endings in the base game per the fan wiki), each tied to identifiable earlier acts.

### Cited Findings
- The game ends with a results screen showing an in-universe political compass and statistics; it has multiple endings and high replay value. Play is reading ministers' advice and choosing among options on constitutional reform, economy, security and welfare. — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game))
- The compass has four quadrants named for in-world ideologies: Malenyevist (economic left), Sordish Reformism (libertarian), Arcasian Capitalism (economic right), Sollism (authoritarian). — [Suzerain Wiki (wiki.gg), Endings](https://suzerain.wiki.gg/wiki/Endings)
- Base-game endings and what triggers them, per the fan wiki: Reelection (high approval, recovering economy, balanced tensions; beating Rumburg in war secures it); Assassination (angering the Oligarchs or the Bluds; avoided by Serge's lucky charm or by creating the secret police); Lost Election (signalled when Monica asks you to retire); Impeached (sub-variants for the Old Guard's emergency powers, corruption, or a botched move against Tusk); Early Retirement (govern cleanly, avoid war and corruption; destination text depends on investments); Path to Exile (cede territory and flee; variants depend on whether you kept your marriage and your vice-president); Coup (military dissolves government; cutting military spending or disrespecting the general triggers it; ceding territory guarantees it); Hail the Queen (lose the war); World at War (ally with a superpower and attack Rumburg, causing nuclear war). — [Suzerain Wiki (wiki.gg), Endings](https://suzerain.wiki.gg/wiki/Endings)
- The fan wiki notes one explicit in-fiction warning sign: Monica asking Rayne to retire signals the Lost Election path. — [Suzerain Wiki (wiki.gg), Endings](https://suzerain.wiki.gg/wiki/Endings)
- Rizia endings (Status Quo, Coup with house-specific variants, Reform, Revolution, Exile, Abdication, Civil War) are listed only as a draft; the coup is triggered by failing to appease the noble houses and Reform by failing to appease the people. — [Suzerain Wiki (wiki.gg), Endings](https://suzerain.wiki.gg/wiki/Endings) (page read to its end; its Rizia section is mostly "TBA")
- Rizia casts the player as King Romus Toras balancing three royal houses; the May 2025 "Sovereign" 3.1.0 update significantly reworked the Rizia storyline after player feedback. — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game))
- Lead developer Ata Sergey Nowak: the team wrote the story first and then added numbers to track resources, opinion and ideology; quantifying ideology was the hardest problem; they wanted players to follow gut feeling or roleplay rather than play a numbers game. Each minister argues for a biased vision, so the player acts on partial information. Voter groups of different sizes mean expanding rights may not pay off in a conservative-majority country. — [AV Club interview, Suzerain](https://www.avclub.com/suzerain-interview)
- Nowak called the game "an empathy machine for lots of types of leaders"; some players who lost the constitutional vote vowed revenge, which he linked to real leaders doubling down. The driver Serge became the stand-in for the ordinary citizen. — [AV Club interview](https://www.avclub.com/suzerain-interview)
- Players report coups that seemed to come "from nowhere". — ⚠️ search-engine snippet of Steam discussions only; not opened, anecdotal.

### Inferences
- **Write narrative first, quantify second** (Nowak) is a direct counter to "endings as threshold readouts": every number exists to track a story-level relationship. For Game Night, each ending should map to a named relationship (board, regulators, rival labs, public, your own researchers) rather than to a raw meter.
- **Faction-specific failure endings** (assassination by the faction you angered, coup by the military you starved) make causation obvious because the ending's agent is the faction the player neglected. Game Night's "removed by the board" is already this shape; "quiet takeover" and "left behind" could each be given an agent the player had a relationship with all game.
- **In-fiction warning beats** (Monica asking you to retire) are cheap, diegetic signposts that a threshold is near.
- The compass screen is a **reflective summary of stance**, separate from the ending itself: it tells the player who they were, even when the ending tells them what happened.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ I could not source the per-faction or per-region epilogue cards, or how the game states the result of the constitutional referendum: neither Wikipedia nor the wiki.gg Endings page describes them. A search-snippet from GamingTrend said the Sovereign update brought "deeper epilogues" (snippet only, not opened).
- No designer statement on endings or epilogues was found; the AV Club interview does not discuss them.

## Papers, Please (Lucas Pope, 2013): many tiny decisions, a nightly ledger, 20 endings

### Takeaway
Papers, Please accumulates hundreds of minute decisions into a few hard state variables (money, family health, EZIC involvement, arrests), surfaces the family's condition every night on a deliberately sparse screen, and ends in one of 20 endings. Pope's design goal was that the sum of ambiguous small choices feel like a personal path, neither wholly good nor bad.

### Cited Findings
- Story mode has twenty endings based on the player's actions; decisions about admitting or denying EZIC agents (a coup conspiracy) affect the ending; the player can flee to Obristan with or without family; going into debt ends the game. — [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Daily loop: protocol violations bring citations by fax shortly after the entrant leaves, reducing pay; bribes add income but risk imprisonment; at day's end the player budgets rent, food, heat and medicine for the family. — [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Pope kept the end-of-day family screen simple so it would not sway players' feelings, and aimed for a non-judgmental narrative. — [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- The nightly family status is a few dots and some text, deliberately unspecified so players project their own family; if the player affords crayons for the son's birthday, the son draws his father as a hero. — [Game Developer, "Designing the bleak genius of Papers, Please" (Leigh Alexander, 2013)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Pope designed encounters that can resolve several ways and do not read clearly as good or bad; when enough add up, the player feels they took a personal path. He wanted to stay "vague and non-judgemental". — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Interdependent scheduling made the game fragile: adding one encounter or rule could disrupt the rest; Pope stopped introducing new rules on days when someone talks to the player first because playtesters ignored the bulletin. Technical simplicity (no voice, minimal 3D) let him afford more branches. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Yahtzee Croshaw: constant moral choices that make it "really hard to be a good person". — [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)

### Inferences
- **The nightly ledger** is the key signposting device: once per day the player sees money in, money out and which family members are cold, hungry or sick. Each ending (family dead, arrested, fled) is then the continuation of a trend the player has watched for days. Game Night's era transitions are the natural place for an equivalent "end-of-era ledger" showing where the lab's investments went and which risk lines moved.
- **Sparse is enough**: Pope's dots-and-text family screen shows that the signpost does not need to be detailed; it needs to be regular and tied to the thing that will end the game.
- **Small ambiguous choices accumulating into a path** is the opposite of a big final choice. It makes endings feel earned, but Pope's own warning applies: interdependent content is fragile, so a 20-minute game should keep the number of interacting ending conditions small.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ I could not verify the exact list of the 20 endings, their numbered ending screens, or a shared "ending N of 20" signpost: the Papers Please Fandom wiki returned HTTP 402, papersplease.wiki did not resolve, and TouchArcade's endings guide returned HTTP 403. Neither Wikipedia nor the Game Developer article lists them or describes the ending screen. (General knowledge, not verified: each ending screen is labelled with its number, which tells the player how many others exist.)

## Dispatch (AdHoc Studio, 2025): hidden counters, choice statistics, a finale that tests the roster

### Takeaway
Dispatch combines Telltale-style dialogue choices with a hero-dispatching management minigame. Its endings read hidden counters (hero alignment, a mentor counter for Invisigal, a romance counter) that the game never displays, plus the roster-cut choice from Episode 3, and the finale's last shift is the one place where management performance decides story. Players can see aggregate choice percentages, and the ending gives Robert a named personality assessment.

### Cited Findings
- Three ending axes: romance (Blonde Blazer, Invisigal, or neither), mentor (succeed or fail to redeem Invisigal), and hero alignment (True Hero, Antihero, Everyman). Choices feed hidden counters; a guide lists a "Sweet Exit" counter (at least 10 for the Invisigal romance), a Robert Mentor counter (45+ for the mentor ending), and Hero and Antihero counters (20+ each; Everyman if both are below 20). The guide says the game never displays these values and some players use community trackers. Episode 8 evaluates the accumulated counters; changing course in the final episode is usually not enough. — [Player.One, Dispatch choices guide](https://www.player.one/dispatch-choices-guide-all-important-decisions-endings-romance-options-best-outcomes-163568) (a guide; counter names and thresholds are presumably community-datamined and not confirmed by AdHoc)
- Co-founder Nick Herman: at the end, Robert receives a personality assessment based on how the player acted (examples "Everyman" and "Antihero"); there are four variants; AdHoc's server-side data shows only three have been reached; the fourth requires a near-perfect, non-random run, and Herman allowed it might be a bug. — [PC Games (German), "Dispatch: Trotz Millionen Verkäufen bleibt ein Ende unerreicht"](https://www.pcgames.de/Dispatch-Spiel-75245/News/Geheimes-Ende-1488997/) (read through the fetch tool's translation)
- Public choice statistics exist per episode, e.g. Episode 3: 62% cut Coupé versus 38% Sonar; 89% protected Victor Vazlav. The wiki says they were made public on 2025-11-04 but does not say whether they came from an in-game screen. — [Dispatch Wiki (wiki.gg), Episode 3: Turnover](https://dispatch.wiki.gg/wiki/Turnover)
- That Dispatch shows, Telltale-style, what percentage of players made each choice at the end of each episode. — ⚠️ search-engine summary only (results included the WBUR and Daily Lobo pages); when I read the Daily Lobo review in full it did not mention statistics, so this specific claim remains unverified.
- The hero cut in Episode 3 returns as an enemy in the final dispatch, and defeating them allows forgiving them. Failing to redeem Invisigal makes her turn evil (with a trophy for failing as a mentor). The reviewer's main complaint: dispatch performance affects the story only in the very last dispatch of the last episode, undercutting the on-screen claim that choices matter. — [Daily Lobo review (Marley Herndon, 2025-12-02)](https://www.dailylobo.com/article/2025/12/review-dispatch-revived-a-genre-and-changed-my-heart-in-a-big-way)
- The final shift pits the Z-Team against Coupé or Sonar depending on the earlier cut. AdHoc's Polly Raguimov said it was deliberately harder (opponents have upgraded enhancements), so even 90% calls can fail; Dennis Lenart said rates are not scripted per moment but reflect game state. Raguimov called an auto-fail rule for over-high combat stats "a bit controversial". — [PCGamesN, final-shift failure rate (2025-12-02)](https://www.pcgamesn.com/dispatch/final-shift-failure-rate)
- At GDC 2026 Herman and Lenart described a percentage system in dispatching that guarantees even poor picks a small chance of success, for fairness. — ⚠️ [OpenCritic relay of TheGamer, 2026-03-11](https://opencritic.com/news/27810/dispatch-had-secret-guardrails-that-made-failing-its-rng-checks-impossible-under-certain-circumstances) (excerpt cuts off mid-sentence; the full TheGamer article was not read)
- AdHoc's year-end data reportedly showed about 1.9 million players romanced Invisigal and about 1 million Blonde Blazer, and AdHoc admitted the scenes favour one romance. — ⚠️ search-engine snippet of [Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/adhoc-studios-reveals-dispatch-player-romance-stats) only; not opened.

### Inferences
- Dispatch is the closest analogue to Game Night: a **management minigame wrapped in story choices**. Its weakest point, by the one review read, is that management performance mattered to story only at the very end. Game Night should let investment choices show up in era-by-era narrative beats, not only in the final era.
- **A named verdict on who you were** (True Hero / Antihero / Everyman) is cheap to build from hidden counters and gives the player a label to argue with; but because the counters are invisible, players built community trackers. That suggests the label lands better if its inputs were at least partially visible during play.
- **The cut that comes back** (the hero you dropped returns as the final opponent) is a strong causation device: the finale's antagonist is literally the consequence of an earlier management decision. Game Night parallel: the rival lab, regulator or departed researcher the player alienated in era 2 reappears as the agent of an era-5 failure.
- **Aggregate statistics** say "others chose differently" and so prove the choice was real, but they do not prove consequence; they are a complement to, not a substitute for, causal callbacks.

### Gaps
- No primary AdHoc source on how the hero relationships feed the finale; the Dispatch wiki's Episode 8 page is a stub with an empty stats section ([Dispatch Wiki, Episode 8](https://dispatch.wiki.gg/wiki/Episode_8)).
- ⚠️ Whether the in-game end-of-episode screen shows percentages could not be confirmed from a source I read in full.

## Undertale (Toby Fox, 2015): a hidden counter made diegetic, characters who call you out, a phone-call epilogue

### Takeaway
Undertale's routes read concrete counters (kills per area, which bosses lived, who was befriended, LV). It signposts them not with a meter but by changing the world: save points show remaining kills, encounters return "But nobody came", music slows, NPCs vanish, and characters (Sans, Flowey) address the player's conduct directly. The Neutral ending ends with a phone call whose content enumerates the fates of characters based on who the player killed or spared, and Flowey tells the player how to reach a better ending.

### Cited Findings
- Genocide requires exhausting a per-area kill counter (Ruins 20, Snowdin 16, Waterfall 18, Hotland/CORE 40) and killing named bosses and minibosses; failing any requirement aborts to the Neutral route, though earlier areas keep their changes. — [Undertale Wiki, Genocide Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Genocide_Route)
- In-world signals: after the Ruins, save points show the remaining kill count in red, then "Determination" when complete; once an area is cleared, encounters show "But nobody came." and the music becomes a distorted ambient track; overworld music slows and lowers; many NPCs vanish; puzzles are pre-solved. — [Undertale Wiki, Genocide Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Genocide_Route)
- Characters as signposts: Sans warns the protagonist twice that they will have a bad time; Flowey identifies the protagonist as the fallen human after Toriel's death and later realises the protagonist would kill him too. — [Undertale Wiki, Genocide Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Genocide_Route)
- Permanent consequence: after a completed Genocide run the world is destroyed; restoring it requires selling the protagonist's SOUL, and all later True Pacifist endings are permanently altered (e.g. the final photo has crossed-out faces), even after a reset, because the flag is stored outside the normal save. — [Undertale Wiki, Genocide Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Genocide_Route)
- Neutral endings read whether Toriel, Undyne, Papyrus, Mettaton and Alphys are alive, how many monsters were killed, whether Undyne was befriended, whether Alphys was dated, and whether the True Lab was visited. — [Undertale Wiki, Neutral Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Neutral_Route)
- After the credits Sans calls; the call describes who now rules the Underground and what happened to each major character, structured as a tree on who is alive (Toriel rules if spared; else Undyne; else Mettaton; else Papyrus; else a dog; else nobody). Sans's opening line reacts to specific quirks of the run (never ate, still wearing the bandage, never saved). In the leaderless ending he ends differently if 20 or more monsters were killed. — [Undertale Wiki, Neutral Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Neutral_Route)
- Flowey's parting words act as an explicit route hint: after a no-kill Neutral run he names what the real happy ending needs (befriend Papyrus, Undyne and Alphys); if the player befriended them but skipped the True Lab, he says to go see Alphys; repeat endings make him increasingly dismissive. — [Undertale Wiki, Neutral Route](https://undertale.wiki/w/Neutral_Route)

### Inferences
- **Turn the hidden counter into world texture.** Undertale's kill counter is never a HUD bar, yet the player always knows it is there: the save point literally counts down, music and population thin out. Game Night analogue: as misalignment risk or concentration of power rises, change the lab's ambience (fewer staff in the office scene, quieter news feed, a researcher who stops showing up) rather than only a number.
- **Callback epilogue structured as a who-survived tree**: the phone call is a priority tree over a handful of booleans, yet it reads as a personal report. That is cheap to author and maps directly to an AI-lab epilogue ("who runs the field now: you, a rival, a regulator, nobody").
- **A character who tells you what the better ending needs** after a weaker ending (Flowey) converts a failure into a visible path for the next run. In a 20-minute replayable game, that is valuable: the failure screen itself can name the missing investment.
- **Permanent memory across runs** is powerful but best used sparingly; for Game Night a lighter form (an endings gallery or a remark from a recurring character on replay) captures the idea without punishing experimentation.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ The exact True Pacifist requirements and the Pacifist ending's epilogue content were not separately sourced (only the Genocide-altered version is described above). The Undertale Fandom wiki returned HTTP 402; the undertale.wiki pages loaded.

## Pentiment (Obsidian, 2022): an accusation without an answer, a town that remembers

### Takeaway
Pentiment makes the climax of each act an accusation the player must make with incomplete evidence, and its director deliberately designed the game so that there is no canonical murderer: the player decides who pays, not who is guilty. The ending's causation is moral and social, carried by the town's later memory of what Andreas did, rather than by being right.

### Cited Findings
- Josh Sawyer wanted the mystery to be compelling without a fixed solution: "there cannot be a right answer". Colleagues and Xbox staff repeatedly asked him who did it; his answer was that there is no one, though some suspects seem more likely in hindsight. — [Shacknews, relaying PC Gamer (2025-10-08)](https://www.shacknews.com/article/146276/pentiment-josh-sawyer-no-correct-answer)
- Gameplay: after gathering proof and talking to townsfolk, the player accuses someone, based on either who they think did it or who most deserves punishment; dialogue choices define Andreas's background (travels, languages, studies), which can affect how mysteries are resolved. In 1518 the accused is executed; in 1525 the accused flees to the mill and dies when townspeople burn it. — [Wikipedia, Pentiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentiment_(video_game))
- Investigations rest on rumour and innuendo with no modern forensics; Sawyer aimed for players to understand people from the past. Destructoid praised the lack of a "golden route"; PC Gamer criticised the choice mechanic as unclear. — [Wikipedia, Pentiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentiment_(video_game))
- In 1543 Magdalene takes over the mural commission and pieces together the town's controversial past. — [Wikipedia, Pentiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentiment_(video_game))
- **Conflict:** Wikipedia's plot summary says the 1543 act reveals the town priest, Thomas Sprecher, as "the mastermind behind the events" who "orchestrated the murders". This sits uneasily with Sawyer's statement that there is no canonical culprit; the article does not tie that sentence to the 1518 or 1525 deaths by name. — [Wikipedia, Pentiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentiment_(video_game)); contradicted in spirit by [Shacknews/PC Gamer](https://www.shacknews.com/article/146276/pentiment-josh-sawyer-no-correct-answer). Treat as unresolved; it should be checked against the game before anything depends on it.
- In a pre-release interview, Sawyer said the player is basically deciding who will pay for the crime, which could be the one they suspect or the one they like least. — ⚠️ search-engine snippet of a [GameBanshee relay of IGN](https://www.gamebanshee.com/9xdaf) only; not opened.
- The team cut an alibi mechanic because it only excluded suspects, while the design aim was to rule people in, not out. — ⚠️ search-engine snippet only, attributed to a Game Developer interview that I did not locate or open.

### Inferences
- **Make the judgment the player's, and make the town react to the judgment** rather than scoring correctness. For an AI-lab game this maps to decisions under deep uncertainty (was the model misaligned? was the rival bluffing?), where the ending should reflect what the player chose to believe and whom they sacrificed, not a hidden truth.
- **Time-skips that let the world remember**: acts set years apart give later acts room to show consequences as history. Game Night's five eras are a natural fit: each era opener can show what people now say about the previous era's decision.
- The criticism that the mechanic was unclear (PC Gamer) is the price of withholding the answer; it argues for being very clear about *what* is being decided even when the *right answer* is withheld.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ I could not verify that the Act 3 town mural records the player's specific choices across acts; the only source read says Magdalene takes over the mural and reconstructs the town's past. Needs a source (a wiki page or Obsidian interview) before use.
- No primary Sawyer postmortem on how later acts reflect earlier choices was read.

## Mass Effect 2 (BioWare, 2010) and the Mass Effect 3 ending backlash: preparation decides survival, and what happens when it doesn't

### Takeaway
Mass Effect 2's final "suicide mission" is the clearest example of an ending computed from the player's investments: specific ship upgrades, loyalty missions and role assignments each decide whether a named squadmate lives. Mass Effect 3's original ending is the counter-example: an ending that did not reflect the player's history drew a backlash so large that BioWare shipped a free epilogue patch adding choice-dependent slides.

### Cited Findings
- ME2: three purchasable ship upgrades each guard against a specific death during the approach (no Multicore Shielding: one of seven squadmates dies; no Heavy Ship Armor: Jack or Kasumi; no Thanix Cannon: one of four). Delaying after the crew is abducted kills crew in proportion to missions done. Role assignments at the base (vent specialist, fire-team leader, biotic shield) kill a disloyal or ill-suited pick. A hidden per-character defence value, averaged over those left holding the line, decides further deaths, disloyal ones first. Deaths themselves are the main feedback; the defence calculation is invisible. — [RPGSite, ME2 suicide mission guide](https://www.rpgsite.net/feature/11118-mass-effect-2-suicide-mission-choices-how-to-make-sure-everyone-lives-to-get-the-best-ending)
- The upgrade checks play out as visible branching scenes at the relay: the player watches a specific squadmate die because a specific upgrade is missing. — [RPGSite](https://www.rpgsite.net/feature/11118-mass-effect-2-suicide-mission-choices-how-to-make-sure-everyone-lives-to-get-the-best-ending)
- A PopMatters critique argues tension collapses on replay because keeping everyone alive is easy once the rules are known ("least suicidal suicide mission"). — ⚠️ search-engine snippet of [PopMatters](https://popmatters.com/131951-least-suicidal-mission-2496128499.html) only; not opened.
- ME3 original ending: three choices (Destroy, Control, Synthesis), each destroying the relays; the article says the choices did not reflect player progress in detail. Criticisms: choices felt inconsequential, little closure, plot holes. A fan charity campaign raised $80,000; complaints went to the FTC and BBB; the UK ASA ruled the endings different enough not to mislead. — [Wikipedia, Mass Effect 3 ending controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Effect_3_ending_controversy)
- The Extended Cut (free, June 2012) added cutscenes and a montage epilogue showing the fates of characters, species and worlds depending on earlier choices and the Effective Military Strength score, plus a refuse option. Reviews were mixed ("substantial improvement" versus "too little, far too late"). — [Wikipedia, ME3 ending controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Effect_3_ending_controversy)
- A reviewer of the Extended Cut said players who felt their decisions did not matter would still feel that, citing the Rachni, whose fate barely changes. — ⚠️ search-engine snippet of [Big Shiny Robot](https://bigshinyrobot.com/video-games/reviewrecap-mass-effect-3-extended-cut/) only; not opened.

### Inferences
- **One-to-one investment-to-outcome mapping** (this upgrade protects this person) is the most legible causation possible: the player sees the missing upgrade's consequence on screen, by name. Game Night can do the same with era investments: "you never funded interpretability, so when the model deceived the evaluators, nobody caught it" is the AI-lab version of the Thanix cannon.
- **The cost is replay tension**: once the mapping is known, the climax becomes a checklist (the PopMatters critique). A 20-minute game played a few times may accept that cost, or mix deterministic mappings with one or two uncertain outcomes.
- ME3's lesson is about **the final beat ignoring accumulated history**: even a large, well-tracked state (war assets) fails if the closing scene does not visibly use it. BioWare's fix was precisely the epilogue-slide technique.

## Fallout: New Vegas (Obsidian, 2010): narrated epilogue slides, one per faction and companion

### Takeaway
New Vegas ends with a sequence of narrated slides, one per faction, location and companion, each picking a variant from the player's actions and reputation. The designers invested heavily in slide variety because players love them.

### Cited Findings
- ⚠️ Josh Sawyer said the team put a lot of effort into ending slides because they are popular and wanted a huge amount of variety and reactivity; the team considered post-ending play but rejected it because ending changes would either feel major in slides and minor afterwards, or minor in both. — search-engine summary of [Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/articles/fallout-new-vegas-will-have-a-real-ending/1100-2469/) and [Prima Games](https://primagames.com/news/why-fallout-nv-comes-proper-close); the Giant Bomb page returned HTTP 403 and the Prima page was not opened.
- ⚠️ Sawyer later said side quests got their own slides mostly by how the developers felt about the quest, not by its plot importance. — search-engine snippet of a Spanish [Notebookcheck article](https://www.notebookcheck.org/El-desarrollador-de-Fallout-New-Vegas-habla-de-como-surgieron-los-iconicos-toboganes-finales-de-las-misiones-secundarias-menores-Es-sobre-todo-arbitrario.1284406.0.html) only; not opened.
- ⚠️ Chris Avellone called the slideshow ending a missed opportunity because post-story content was cut for time. — search-engine snippet only ([Quarter to Three](https://quartertothree.com/fp/?p=48628)); not opened.

### Inferences
- **Epilogue slides are the cheapest high-reward causal-proof device**: each slide is one short paragraph conditioned on a few flags, and naming the player's specific act in each slide is what makes them feel earned. Game Night's ending could be followed by 4 to 6 slides (the lab, the field, the public, the regulators, your researchers, the model) each conditioned on one or two investments.

## Telltale's "X will remember that" and the illusion-of-choice critique

### Takeaway
Telltale's on-screen memory notice makes every choice feel consequential in the moment, but critics found that most choices did not change outcomes, producing a lasting "your choices don't matter" reputation. Scholarly defence reframes the choices as moral dilemmas whose value is reflective rather than causal.

### Cited Findings
- Elise Favis (Game Informer, 2015): in The Walking Dead and The Wolf Among Us, choices mainly change dialogue and how characters treat you; examples where outcomes converge regardless (Shaun dies whoever you save; Ben's death is only postponed); being brutal or kind to a suspect gives no advantage. She argues choices matter "for the moment" and calls for branching subplots that resolve and feed the main story; she cites ME3 as the same failure. She still credits the games with strong emotional impact. — [Game Informer, "Your choices don't matter in Telltale games"](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2015/02/03/why-your-choices-dont-matter-in-telltale-games.aspx)
- Sarian (2018) argues the Telltale model works like a philosophical thought experiment, a series of themed moral dilemmas; the penultimate choice of Walking Dead Season 2 responds to whether the player absorbed the series' themes rather than to the accumulated record; choices have pedagogical rather than consequential value. — ⚠️ [Eludamos, "No Going Back": The Telltale Model as Thought Experiment](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/eludamos/article/view/vol9no1-2) (abstract page only; the full paper was not read)
- ⚠️ Alex Mitchell describes the Telltale model as an "ELIZA effect" (seeming more responsive than its branching is); Den of Geek describes Season One's major-choice flowchart as essentially linear. — search-engine snippets only; not opened.

### Inferences
- **A memory notice is a promise**; if the ending does not cash it, the notice backfires. For Game Night, a "the board will remember this" style notice should be reserved for choices that actually feed an ending condition.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ No source read in full describes Telltale's end-of-episode percentage screens.

## Detroit: Become Human (Quantic Dream, 2018): the flowchart

### Takeaway
Detroit shows a branching flowchart during and right after each chapter, revealing which branch the player took and that others exist, with the ability to rewind to checkpoints. Reviewers split: some called the flowcharts a highlight, others said they hurt immersion.

### Cited Findings
- Choices can be viewed in a flowchart during and after a chapter; the player can rewind to some points. Connor, Kara and Markus can each die and the story continues without them. David Cage tracked branches in five or six thousand pages of notes and compared the story to a Rubik's Cube. IGN praised the flowcharts; VideoGamer called them one of the few redeeming qualities; GameSpot found the flowchart exposition "ultimately detrimental" to immersion and wished it could be turned off. — ⚠️ [Wikipedia, Detroit: Become Human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit:_Become_Human) (fetch read only the first 100,000 of about 148,000 characters; the unread part appears to be references)
- ⚠️ The flowchart is reachable from the pause menu, shows branches and possible endings per chapter, and lets players compare their choices with other players at chapter end. — search-engine snippet of [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/10-things-we-learned-from-the-detroit-become-human-demo) only; the fetched page returned no article body.

### Inferences
- **A flowchart proves causation structurally** (you went left, here is right) but spends surprise: it shows that hidden alternatives exist and roughly where. GameSpot's complaint shows it can feel like a spreadsheet interrupting a story. For a 20-minute game, an end-of-run "path map" (eras as columns, the player's key choices highlighted, unseen endings greyed) gets most of the benefit at the end without breaking play.
- **Characters who can die while the story continues** is a strong form of consequence that a management game can mirror: a lab leader, a key researcher or a partner lab can be lost mid-game and the game goes on.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ No source read in full confirms the global-percentage display.

## Reigns (Nerial, 2016): four visible bars, death at either extreme

### Takeaway
Reigns represents the whole kingdom as four bars at the top of the screen; every swipe moves them; a reign ends when balance fails. Its designer chose persistence across a dynasty so death is a step forward, not a reset.

### Cited Findings
- François Alliot: Reigns strips management sims to bare elements, with "the four bars" at the top representing the kingdom; there is no branching; each decision changes the kingdom's state and the game selects from 770 cards fitting that state; kings can die within two or three swipes. All kings belong to one dynasty so actions carry over across reigns, giving a secondary goal beyond the longest reign and reducing frustration. Alliot frames it as a critique of politics where balance is nearly impossible. Players collect deaths and cards. — [Inverse interview (2016-11-02)](https://inverse.com/gaming/23117-reigns-francois-alliot-nerial-developer-interview)
- ⚠️ Alliot's GDC 2017 talk covered constraints from swipe-based play. — search-engine snippet only; not opened.

### Inferences
- **Visible meters with failure at both extremes** make every ending perfectly legible (you died because the church bar hit zero or maxed out) at the cost of narrative nuance. A useful middle ground for Game Night: make the *failure* conditions this legible (the misalignment line, the board's patience), while keeping the *win* texture narrative.
- **Collecting deaths** turns failure endings into content and gives an "ending N of M" style goal to replays.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ The names of the four bars and the exact rule (both empty and full kill) were not confirmed by the source read; the Inverse interview names the bars only collectively. (General knowledge, not verified: Church, People, Army, Treasury, and either extreme ends the reign.)

## Cross-cutting: legibility versus surprise, and what the structure literature says

### Takeaway
The best games keep the *direction* of consequences visible while hiding the *exact payoff* or the *exact threshold*: Disco Elysium shows a thought's name but not its effect; Undertale shows a countdown but expresses it as world texture; Mass Effect 2 shows which upgrades exist but not the defence math; Papers, Please shows a sparse nightly ledger. Fully hidden counters (Dispatch) drive players to community trackers; fully exposed structures (Detroit's flowchart, Reigns' bars) are legible but risk feeling mechanical.

### Cited Findings
- Emily Short's "quality-based narrative" (a term from Failbetter Games): storylets unlocked by numeric qualities; later stories can reward earlier inventory with callbacks; but authors must track progression across many qualities manually. In "salience-based" systems that pick content by scoring world state, she flags a risk from The King of Chicago, where numeric scores could lead to an unplanned bad ending, and suggests explicit dramatic-arc variables. — [Emily Short, "Beyond Branching" (2016)](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)
- Short also suggests running thousands of simulated playthroughs to find unused or overused content. — [Emily Short, "Beyond Branching"](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)
- Pope: kept the family screen sparse and non-judgmental so as not to sway the player. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Nowak (Suzerain): wanted players to follow instinct or roleplay, not a numbers game. — [AV Club](https://www.avclub.com/suzerain-interview)
- Sawyer (Pentiment): no right answer by design. — [Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/146276/pentiment-josh-sawyer-no-correct-answer)
- Dispatch's counters are never displayed; players use community trackers. — [Player.One](https://www.player.one/dispatch-choices-guide-all-important-decisions-endings-romance-options-best-outcomes-163568)

### Inferences
- **Show the axis, hide the cutoff.** Name what is being tracked (in Game Night: alignment investment, board confidence, public trust, capability lead) and show which way each choice pushes it, but do not show the exact threshold that flips the ending. Foreshadow thresholds through in-world warnings instead (Monica asking Rayne to retire; Sans warning of a bad time; the save point counting down).
- **Make failures explain the path; make wins feel like a story.** Failure endings benefit most from explicit causal proof (Flowey's hint, ME2's named deaths); win endings benefit from texture (slides, a compass, a named verdict).
- **Avoid the King of Chicago trap** Short describes: an ending chosen by opaque scores can feel arbitrary. Game Night's endings should each be traceable to one or two named investments the player can recognise, which also makes them testable by simulated bot runs (consistent with Short's suggestion and the project's existing before/after bot-run practice).
- **Promise only what you cash.** Telltale's notices and ME3's ending show that signalling consequence without paying it off is worse than not signalling at all.

### Gaps
- ⚠️ I did not read an academic paper on endings or player agency in full; the Eludamos paper was abstract-only. A peer-reviewed source on the "illusion of choice" would strengthen this section.

## Summary table

| Technique | What it does | Example games | What it needs from the game's state | Pitfalls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Named stance objects | Player equips or adopts a named identity (ideology, copotype, doctrine) that the ending names back | Disco Elysium (Ideologies, Copotypes in the Thought Cabinet) | A small set of mutually exclusive or stackable named flags the player explicitly chooses | Effects hidden too long feel arbitrary; too many labels dilute meaning |
| Face-down commitment | Show the name and direction of an investment, reveal its payoff after committing | Disco Elysium thoughts | Delayed-effect flags with a reveal moment | Players may feel tricked if the reveal contradicts the name |
| Tribunal or debrief | Characters who witnessed the game judge the player, citing specific acts | Disco Elysium debrief (Jean, Kim); Wolf Among Us Fabletown judgment (per Favis) | Per-act flags that characters can cite; a per-character opinion score | Judgment that does not change the outcome reads as cosmetic (Favis on Wolf Among Us) |
| Nightly or per-era ledger | Regular sparse summary of the variables that can end the game | Papers, Please family screen | A few end-condition variables updated each cycle | Too much detail turns it into a spreadsheet; too little hides the trend |
| Diegetic counter | Hidden counter expressed through world changes and countdown text | Undertale save-point kill count, "But nobody came", music and NPC changes | A counter per area or era with authored world states at thresholds | Players who miss the cues feel ambushed |
| Character call-outs and warnings | NPCs comment on the player's trajectory before the ending fires | Undertale (Sans, Flowey); Suzerain (Monica asks Rayne to retire) | Threshold checks that trigger warning beats ahead of the ending condition | Warnings that fire too late or too rarely do not help |
| Failure ending names the fix | After a weaker ending, a character states what a better ending needs | Undertale Flowey's post-Neutral hints | Knowledge of which conditions were missed | Can feel hand-holding if overused |
| Who-survived callback epilogue | Short epilogue enumerating each character's fate from the run | Undertale Sans phone call; ME3 Extended Cut montage | A handful of alive/dead or allied/hostile booleans and a priority tree | Combinatorial writing cost; variants must differ visibly |
| Epilogue slides | One narrated slide per faction, companion or place, each conditioned on player acts | Fallout: New Vegas (⚠️ snippet-sourced); ME3 Extended Cut | Per-faction outcome flags and reputation | Slides that ignore a big decision undo the effect |
| Investment-to-survival mapping | Each specific investment protects a specific named outcome in the climax | Mass Effect 2 suicide mission | Purchased upgrades, loyalty flags, role choices, hidden defence values | Becomes a checklist on replay; invisible math can feel unfair the first time |
| Faction-agent failure endings | The neglected or angered faction is the one that ends you | Suzerain (assassination, coup, impeachment variants) | Per-faction relationship scores with thresholds | Endings that seem to come "from nowhere" if relationships were not visible |
| Stance compass and named verdict | A results screen placing the player on an ideological map or giving a personality label | Suzerain political compass; Dispatch True Hero / Antihero / Everyman | Two-axis stance scores or alignment counters | Hidden inputs push players to community trackers |
| The cut that comes back | A party member the player dropped returns as the climax's opponent | Dispatch (Coupé or Sonar in the final shift) | Record of the cut, with a reappearance hook | Only works if the cut was a memorable, deliberate choice |
| Withheld verdict | The game asks for a judgment but never confirms the truth; consequences follow the judgment | Pentiment | Who was accused; how the community reacted | Players may find the choice mechanic unclear (PC Gamer) |
| Time-skip memory | Later acts set years later show what people remember of earlier choices | Pentiment (1518, 1525, 1543) | Flags from earlier acts surfaced as dialogue and setting | Requires authored variation per act |
| Choice statistics | Shows what share of players chose each option | Dispatch (stats published; in-game screen ⚠️ unverified); Telltale; Detroit (⚠️) | Server telemetry | Proves choices were real, not that they mattered |
| Flowchart or path map | Shows the branch taken and the unseen alternatives | Detroit: Become Human | A branch graph with tracked nodes | Breaks immersion mid-story (GameSpot); spoils surprise |
| Visible meters, death at extremes | Every ending is a meter hitting a limit | Reigns | A few bounded meters | Legible but thin; endings feel mechanical |
| Memory notice | On-screen note that a character will remember a choice | Telltale "X will remember that" | Per-choice flags | Backfires if the ending does not use the flag (Favis; ME3) |
