# Run a clock over the turn engine tonight

The fastest safe way to make Game Night AI Lab feel like flowing time before the 2026-09-27 12:00 AM PT deadline is a clock that runs over the unchanged turn engine: a visible calendar advances on its own, every quarter, month or week of story time the UI calls the existing `endTurn`, and the clock pauses whenever a decision card or dialog is open. That design needs no change to `sim/`, keeps all of the roughly 500 existing tests and the balance work, and fits the codebase's own convention that the UI imports the sim and never modifies it. It has one hard catch and one soft one. The hard catch is that auto-pause is mandatory rather than optional: the engine auto-resolves any card still unanswered when the next turn fires, and treats an unanswered President meeting as a walkout. The soft catch is that player actions still only take effect when the next turn fires, so it feels like Game Dev Tycoon at a glance but not under the hand. Game Dev Tycoon itself, contrary to the working assumption, ships with **no speed buttons at all**. Its only speed lever is a total-game-length choice made once, before the game starts. A deeper conversion to continuous weekly simulation steps would deliver the "organic" feel more fully, but it touches almost every file in `sim/` plus the balance work, and cannot ship by the deadline. With about 21 hours left at the time of writing (02:51 PDT on 2026-09-26), the recommendation is the clock-over-engine design with a constant real-time length per turn, "End turn" kept as a "Skip ahead" button, and "2 moves" re-worded as "2 team actions per quarter, month or week."

> **Correction to a working assumption: vanilla Game Dev Tycoon has no pause, play or fast-forward buttons.** A Greenheart Games staff member said on the forum that speed is set once, through a cog icon on the company-creation screen, as a total number of years ("less years = faster game"), and that it cannot be changed after the game starts ([Game Speed, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239820/discussions/0/490123938428364475/) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat). A community mod, TimeMod, exists specifically to add "pause, slow motion, play and fast" buttons, which implies the base game lacks them ([dgalaktionov/TimeMod](https://github.com/dgalaktionov/TimeMod) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat, and the tool could not retrieve the mod's full README body, only its repository summary). Search results over several Steam threads reach the same conclusion ([Steam Community threads](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239820/discussions/0/1631916887482527517/) ⚠️ WebSearch synthesis only, never opened and read to the end). No source here was read as raw page text, but three independent routes agree, including the developer. The pause/play/fast control in our own `K2-events-crisis.html` mockup is therefore our addition, borrowed from games like RimWorld, not a copy of Game Dev Tycoon.

## Game Dev Tycoon runs one fixed-speed clock and pauses only for menus and big dialogs

Game Dev Tycoon's time is a continuously running calendar written as year/month/week, for example `13/2/3` ([Date, gdt-modAPI wiki](https://github.com/greenheartgames/gdt-modAPI/wiki/Date) ⚠️ WebFetch returns an AI-summarized extraction of the page, not raw text, so exact wording/completeness of the source page is not fully guaranteed, though the concrete syntax and table quoted appear precise). A reverse-engineered engine flag, `SKIP_FRAME`, confirms the clock is a real-time render loop rather than a turn engine ([Game Flags, GDT wiki](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Flags), read in full). The only concrete rate found is one critic's impression that "a week will last around five seconds" ([ZTGD review](https://ztgd.com/reviews/game-dev-tycoon/) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat; this is the only source found with a concrete real-seconds-per-week figure, and it is a single critic's impression, not measured or officially documented). The pre-game length setting stretches or compresses *when* scripted content fires, such as new consoles and trade shows, rather than changing the tick rate. The sources even disagree on what the settings are: the modding API lists 30, 35 and 42 years ([Date, gdt-modAPI wiki](https://github.com/greenheartgames/gdt-modAPI/wiki/Date) ⚠️ same WebFetch-summarization caveat as above), while a fully read community post says "The story ends at 25/30/35 years" ([How long is the game, GDT wiki discussions](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/f/p/2011745795178687335)). This is an unresolved contradiction, likely a difference between game versions.

Pausing is a side effect, not a control. Players and staff describe the menu (reached by Escape or a click) as the only way to pause ([Pause Key, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239820/discussions/0/864977564198556533/) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat). A second thread adds that "certain pop-up windows (like the develop new game dialog) also trigger a pause" ([Pause time in the game?, Greenheart forum](https://forum.greenheartgames.com/t/pause-time-in-the-game/15748) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat). Only that one dialog is directly confirmed. The research note infers, without confirming dialog by dialog, that most full-screen decision dialogs also pause and that side panels such as hiring and research do not. Some events are fixed to the calendar: the G3 trade-show booth offer always opens at month 5, week 1 ([G3, GDT wiki](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/wiki/G3), read in full).

The most transferable finding is that Game Dev Tycoon has **no action budget**. Nothing caps how many things a player starts per week. Work is limited by cash, by staff (each person's time is split by percentage across fields, [Game Development Based on Experience, GDT wiki](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Development_Based_on_Experience/1.4.3), read in full), and by office tier. Contracts, which unlock after the third game, run in parallel with their own deadlines ([Contracts, GDT wiki](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/wiki/Contracts), read in full). The tension comes from money draining while time runs. Bankruptcy hits at more than $200K of debt after a bank bailout ([Bankruptcy, GDT wiki](https://gamedevtycoon.fandom.com/wiki/Bankruptcy), read in full). Players name the gap between finishing a game and its sales arriving as the most dangerous moment ([Frustrating late game bankruptcy, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239820/discussions/0/864977564336994349/) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat). One critic puts a single project at about two in-game months ([Caesoose review](https://caesoose.com/gaming-week-37-game-dev-tycoon/) ⚠️ WebFetch summarization caveat; this is the only source found with a concrete real-time-independent estimate of project duration in calendar terms, and it is one critic's characterization rather than an official or wiki-documented number). Whole playthroughs are reported at anywhere from 8 to 20 hours ([GameFAQs and Steam completion-time pages](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/pc/713603-game-dev-tycoon/answers/1-how-long-does-it-take-to-beat-this-game) ⚠️ None of these individual pages were opened and read to the end in this session — this is search-engine synthesis only). That matters for us: Game Dev Tycoon's slow calendar is tuned for a many-hour game, and ours is a single game night, so copying its rate literally does not fit. No developer commentary explaining any of these time-design choices was found across two research passes.

Game Dev Story, Game Dev Tycoon's inspiration, uses the same year/month/week shape with yearly fixed-date events. One search summary says it has "a fast mode available right from the start" ([Kairosoft wiki](https://kairosoft.wiki.gg/wiki/Game_Dev_Story) ⚠️ WebSearch synthesis only, none individually opened and read to the end). A second summary says Kairosoft titles commonly lock high-speed mode until a second playthrough ([Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/kairosoft-history) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). Both sources are weak, and they partly disagree.

## Comparable games share one pattern: a fixed tick, a speed dial, and auto-pause on decisions

Real-time-with-pause games (RTwP: the clock runs by itself and the player can stop it to think) are nearly all turn engines in disguise. A fixed simulation step, called a *tick*, resolves automatically, and "speed" only means how many ticks run per real second. RimWorld is the best-documented case: 60 ticks per real second at Normal, 2,500 ticks per in-game hour, and speeds of Pause, ×1, ×3 and ×6 ([RimWorld Wiki: Time](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Time), read in full). Stellaris reportedly runs about one in-game day per real second at normal speed ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/281990/discussions/0/133261907130714227/) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). For a small web game, the transferable idea is that our existing `endTurn` can *be* the tick, and a scheduler around it decides how often it fires.

Auto-pause when a decision appears is close to universal. Crusader Kings III pauses on decision-bearing events in single-player and shows a distinct "auto-paused" indicator ([Paradox Forums](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/is-autopause-on-events-a-setting.1445451/) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). Stellaris and RimWorld let players choose which events auto-pause ([Steam Community, Stellaris](https://steamcommunity.com/app/281990/discussions/0/357286033299834552/); [Steam Community, RimWorld](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/1744480967028550929/) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). Frostpunk shows the cost of doing it too hard: it pauses and resets speed to Normal on every story event and every in-game 5 a.m., and the speed reset is a top player complaint ([Steam Community, Frostpunk](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708643572493/) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). The lesson is to pause on every decision, but resume at the player's previous speed. RimWorld and Prison Architect also let players issue orders while paused ([Prison Architect Wiki: Clock](https://prison-architect.fandom.com/wiki/Clock) ⚠️ (search-summary only)), so pause stops the world, not the player.

None of the surveyed games keeps a "moves per turn" counter. They cap the player with build times, work queues, money that accrues over time, and the number of workers available. In RimWorld, throughput is set by how much simulated labour a task needs ([RimWorld Wiki: Time](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Time), read in full). Paradox games cap the player through durations and slowly accruing currencies ([Paradox Forums](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/is-it-turn-based-real-time-or-real-time-with-pause.913904/) ⚠️ (search-summary only — general characterization, not fetched)).

Two cautions apply directly to us. Sid Meier first built Civilization as real time, found players overwhelmed by many interacting systems, and rebuilt it as turns so each player could set their own pace ([Designer Notes: Turn-Based vs. Real-Time](https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-8-turn-based-vs-real-time/), read in full). Hybrids such as Civilization III's optional "turnless mode" are cited as having struggled ([Game Developer: Turn-Based vs Real-Time](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/turn-based-vs-real-time), read in full). Our game already has many systems (compute, money, safety, rivals, events, the President), so auto-pause and an easy manual pause are protections, not polish. Players of Crusader Kings III also report slowing down or pausing constantly whenever several things are going on ([Paradox Forums](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/how-fast-do-you-play.1520770/page-2) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). Engaged players will spend much of their time paused and planning, so the paused-planning experience deserves the design effort.

Two research gaps stay open. No shipped game with a "skip to the next decision" button was found; the closest thing is running at top speed until auto-pause fires, and that is absence of evidence rather than a proven pattern. No inbox-style design, where decisions wait while time keeps running, was verified in any surveyed game (⚠️ I did not find a primary or fully-read source describing a "decisions queue in an inbox that the player can address on their own schedule while time keeps running" pattern). Nothing on RTwP accessibility for players who need more time was found either (⚠️ I found no sources in this pass — from either full reads or search summaries — specifically addressing RTwP accessibility).

## Turns are baked into our sim in three layers, and only one needs a sim change to go real time

The turn is foundational, not bolted on. `MAX_MOVES = 2` (`sim/turn.js:34`), the counters `turn`, `turnInEra` and `monthsElapsed`, and the era gate that fires every 4th turn (`sim/turn.js:300-316`) were all present in the first sim skeleton. That comes from the research note's reading of the original plan (⚠️ `docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-25-sim-core.md` was read only in parts: Tasks 1, 3-10, 12 and the self-review notes were only grepped for `turn`/`era` mentions, not read in full). The five eras all have 4 turns each, but the turns are wildly uneven in story time: 3 months per turn in eras 1-2, 1 month in eras 3-4, and 0.25 months (a week) in era 5 (`sim/data/eras.js`, re-read in full for this report). Era 5's four turns cover one story month, about 1/28th of the whole run.

The first layer comes free with a clock over the unchanged engine. Every schedule and deadline counted in raw turns keeps working because the clock only decides *when* `endTurn` runs, not *what* it does. That covers President meetings, the summit gate, promise and lawsuit due-turns, contract arrivals, the warning-then-card delay for events, and era transitions with their office swap. The budget is already a rate ("$M per month", validated at `sim/turn.js:47`) whose effect scales by the era's months per turn (`sim/turn.js:79`), so it is already native to flowing time. Retrospective screens such as the race chart only read stored turn numbers. The balance tool calls the sim directly and should be unaffected (⚠️ `tools/balance.js` was never opened in the research pass; this is an inference from what the plans say it does).

The second layer needs UI work only. The HUD prints "Era N · Turn N of 20" (`ui/hud.js:35`, re-read for this report). The "End turn" menu item is the one control that commits a turn (`ui/menu.js:19, 275-277`). Every screen repeats "uses 1 of 2 moves this turn" (`ui/screens/company.js`, `compute.js`, `sites.js`). Recipe and compute screens phrase delays as "+N turns" and "arrives in N turns", yet show contract terms in months on the same screen. `ui/logic/eraIntro.js` tells the player "Each turn is now a quarter / a month / a week". The one real-time clock anywhere in the repo is in the `K2-events-crisis.html` mockup on the `events-research` branch. It shows a `Y2 M1 W2` date, Pause/Play/Fast buttons, a pause on every crisis card, a "while you were busy" summary panel, and story-time countdown bars such as "the leak goes public in about 2 weeks". Those countdowns are invented flavour text: the sim has no deadline shorter than one turn.

The third layer needs sim changes to *feel* right, even though nothing breaks without them. Most random rolls are flat per turn, not per month. Rival progress, poaching (15% per turn), weight theft (20%), price wars (12%), viral demos (15%) and internal-deployment escalation all ignore how long the turn is. Only the CoreFlame failure roll compounds a monthly rate correctly (`sim/contracts.js:17`). Lawsuits (+6 or +8 turns), promise deadlines (+4 or +2 turns), training runs (1-3 turns), site builds (4 turns) and the grid reservation are also counted in turns. A lawsuit filed in era 1 therefore comes due after 1.5-2 story years, while the same lawsuit filed in era 5 comes due after 6-8 weeks. A visible calendar will make that inconsistency obvious to players. Two triggers fire only on one exact turn: the President meeting uses an exact `era` and `turnInEra` match (`sim/president.js:43-48`, re-read in full), and two internal-incident cards fire only on the exact turn their stage rose. A scheduler that ever skipped or merged a turn would silently lose them.

Re-reading `sim/turn.js` for this report confirmed the catch that makes auto-pause mandatory. At the start of every `endTurn`, any card still in `pendingEvents` is resolved with its fallback choice (`sim/turn.js:182-188`), and a President meeting opened on the previous turn but not answered with a meeting move expires as a walkout that costs 10 US favour (`sim/turn.js:228-234`, `sim/president.js:118-122`). Under a running clock, a card left open while the next turn fires is decided for the player. The first turn also has to wait for the constitution screen, because the constitution can only be set on turn 0 (`sim/turn.js:104-115`). The research note also warns that turn-boundary timing has already been a repeated source of real bugs. Plan 2C's review record documents four rounds of fixes for deliveries arriving "a turn late" (`docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-25-plan2c-compute.md`; ⚠️ that plan was read only in parts, but the review record was in the part that was read).

## Four design choices decide what "real time" means here

**What replaces "2 moves per turn".** The cheapest answer keeps the sim's cap and re-labels it: "2 team actions per quarter, month or week," with the counter refilling each time the calendar crosses a turn boundary. A second UI-only answer lets the player click actions freely into a queue that feeds the engine two per turn. That hides the counter, but queued actions can become invalid by the time they run. Game Dev Tycoon's answer is no counter at all, with work limited by staff, cash and time. That needs a sim change and a rebalance. The per-turn budget needs no replacement because it is already a monthly rate.

**The clock-speed policy.** Months per turn shrink 12× from era 1 to era 5, so the real-time length of a turn and the speed of the calendar cannot both stay constant. The table compares three policies, using about 13 story weeks per quarter and about 1 per era-5 turn.

| Policy | Real time per turn (eras 1-2 / 3-4 / 5) | Calendar speed | Flat per-turn rolls per real minute, era 5 vs era 1 | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constant time per turn (for example 45 s) | 45 / 45 / 45 s, about 15 min of running clock | Fast early (about 3.5 s per story week), slow late (45 s per story week) | Same as today (1×) | Keeps today's balance; late eras read as "every week now matters" |
| Constant time per story week (Game Dev Tycoon's reported ~5 s, ⚠️ single critic, WebFetch summarization caveat) | 65 / 22 / 5 s, about 12 min | Constant | About 13× more often | Era 5 becomes 20 seconds with the summit, second President meeting and hold-or-ship crammed in; not safe without re-rating the sim |
| Compromise curve (for example 60 / 40 / 30 s) | 60 / 40 / 30 s, about 15 min | Mildly faster late | About 2× | A middle path if constant time per turn feels too slow at the end |

Constant time per turn is the recommendation for this deadline. It is the only policy under which every balance number tuned so far still means what it meant. It also matches the spec's approved "about 1 minute" per turn (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-25-game-night-ai-lab-design.md` §2). A pause button plus one fast speed (×2) is worth adding even though Game Dev Tycoon has neither, because every comparable RTwP game offers them.

**Whether "End turn" survives.** It should survive as **"Skip ahead,"** which fires the next turn immediately. That is literally what `game.endTurn()` does today (`ui/game.js:54-65`, re-read in full), so it carries zero engine risk and gives impatient players the old pace. It must fire exactly one turn per press, and fast speed must run turns one after another rather than merging them, or the exact-match President and incident triggers can be lost.

**Sim-level change versus UI only.** UI only means the calendar between turns is a visual estimate. The date, progress bars and countdowns creep forward smoothly, but the sim's state still jumps once per turn. A sim-level change means real weekly steps. Between the two sits a middle option: split `endTurn` so player actions apply the moment they are clicked, while a clock-driven step resolves the world.

## Four ways to make the game real time, and which can ship by midnight

### A. A clock over the unchanged turn engine ships tonight

A new UI scheduler owns a running calendar and calls `game.endTurn()` each time a turn's worth of story time has passed (45 real seconds per turn under the recommended policy). It pauses whenever a card, the President meeting, the constitution screen, a hazard choice or any menu dialog is open, and resumes at the previous speed. It gets a pause/play/×2 control and a "Skip ahead" button in the HUD. "Turn N of 20" becomes a `Y M W` date derived from `monthsElapsed` and interpolated between turns. The "moves" copy becomes "2 team actions this quarter/month/week", and the era-intro "each turn is now a quarter" copy is reframed. The end-of-turn summary must stop being blocking; if it opens as a dialog, the game pauses every turn and is turn-based again. It could become a non-blocking feed, or the mockup's "while you were busy" panel. Nothing changes in `sim/`, the tests or the balance work. `tests/ui-game.test.js` still holds, since it asserts two moves and turn 1 after one `endTurn` (⚠️ the other test files were only grepped for turn-related patterns, not read end to end). This matches plan 2B's rule that "the sim is imported, never modified."

The risks are real but bounded. Actions still land only when the next turn fires, so starting a training run shows nothing until the calendar crosses the boundary. Smooth progress bars would be a visual estimate, not real progress. The inconsistent turn-counted durations (lawsuits, promises, training) become visible against a date. Missing a pause rule for any decision surface means the fallback choice or a walkout silently decides for the player. The research note sizes the pieces as small (date readout, copy changes) to medium (clock control and pause rules, the summary panel, smooth bars). **Verdict: shippable by the deadline** if work starts today. The core is the scheduler, pause rules, date readout, Skip ahead and copy. The busy panel and smooth bars are polish that can be cut. The owner's own review rules (one adversarial Codex pass, plus the pre-merge pair) need time reserved for them.

### B. Plan-then-play turns are the cheapest option but miss the brief

This is the WeGo pattern (both sides plan, then the plans play out together), as in Frozen Synapse ([Lines of Battle](https://linesofbattle.net/simultaneous-turns), read in full; [Wikipedia: Frozen Synapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_Synapse) ⚠️ (search-summary only)). The clock sits paused while the player plans. "Play" animates one turn's worth of calendar over a few seconds, then auto-pauses at the boundary. It is option A's code with "pause at every turn boundary" switched on, so it costs even less. It keeps everything, but it is still visibly turn-based, which is exactly what the owner said they do not want. **Verdict: shippable, but only worth offering as a setting or fallback**, for example if playtesters find option A overwhelming, as Civilization's players once did.

### C. Instant actions over a ticking engine are a stretch goal, not a deadline plan

This option splits `sim/turn.js` at its natural seam. Lines 116-234 apply the player's input; lines 236-323 resolve the world and advance the clock. The split produces two functions: `applyActions`, called the moment the player clicks, and `resolveTick`, called by the clock. `endTurn` stays as the two composed, so existing tests keep their meaning. This delivers the Game Dev Tycoon feel of clicking and seeing something start right away, and it can keep "2 actions per window" as a counter the tick resets. The re-read of `turn.js` shows why it is fiddly. The `before` snapshot used to update the board (line 101), the per-turn emergency flag (line 102), the auto-resolve of last turn's unanswered cards (lines 182-188), release activation (line 196) and the two-call President meeting flow (lines 119-129, 228-234) all assume one call per turn and would need to move carefully. Doing it changes the order of fallback resolution relative to moves, which is a behaviour change that needs tests. The same UI work as option A is still required on top. **Verdict: not safe by the deadline.** It is a medium sim refactor in the exact area where plan 2C needed four review rounds, and it would need its own sim plan. It is the natural first step after the deadline.

### D. Continuous weekly simulation delivers the full "organic" feel after the deadline

The sim itself advances in story weeks, and the turn disappears. Money, growth, the budget and contract terms already scale by months, so they convert by multiplying per week. Every flat per-turn roll would be re-derived as a weekly chance, compounded the way `sim/contracts.js:17` already does for CoreFlame. Every turn-counted duration (lawsuits, promises, training runs, release gaps, site builds, arrivals, the grid reservation) would be re-expressed in weeks. Eras would end on story dates instead of every fourth turn. The exact-match triggers would become due-by windows. "2 moves" would give way to concurrency slots or cooldowns, as in the surveyed games. This touches `sim/turn.js`, `rivals.js`, `internal.js`, `events.js` and both event data files, `training.js`, `release.js`, `contracts.js`, `power.js`, `economy.js`, `promises.js`, `president.js` and `summit.js`. It also touches many of the 496 tests, and it needs a full balance re-run, which the plans require for any tuning change. It also invites the late-game slowdown and "always paused" pitfalls documented for Paradox games. **Verdict: cannot ship by the deadline.** It is the honest long-term version of what the owner asked for.

| Option | Sim change | Keeps tests and balance | Feels like flowing time | Ships by 2026-09-27 12:00 AM PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Clock over unchanged engine | None | Yes | Mostly (actions land at the next turn) | Yes |
| B. Plan-then-play turns | None | Yes | No (visibly turns) | Yes, as a setting |
| C. Instant actions, ticking engine | Medium refactor of `sim/turn.js` | Mostly, needs new tests | Yes | No (stretch at best) |
| D. Continuous weekly simulation | Large, most of `sim/` | No, rebalance needed | Fully | No |

## Conclusion

The owner's picture of Game Dev Tycoon is half right. It does run on a continuous calendar with no turns, but it has no speed buttons, no action budget and only incidental pausing, and its designers never explained why. The speed dial, the pause-on-decision rule and the Skip ahead button in the recommended design come from RimWorld, Paradox and Frostpunk, not from Game Dev Tycoon. That is fine, because those are the parts the genre's own players rely on. The real design risk is not in the clock at all. It is that our engine was built to decide things *for* a player who does not answer before the next turn, which was harmless when the player chose when turns ended and becomes dangerous once a clock does.

The deeper finding is that the "uneven clock speed" worry and the "2 moves" question are one problem seen from two sides. Both exist because a turn means 3 months early and a week late. Holding real time per turn constant is what keeps every rate, budget and balance number honest tonight. The cost is a calendar that slows in the final era, and it can be framed as the stakes rising rather than hidden. After the deadline, option C (instant actions) is the step that most changes how the game feels in the hand, and option D is where turn-counted durations like lawsuits and training runs finally become real lengths of time.
