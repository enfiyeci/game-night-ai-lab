# Make compute deals move, bite and expire

> **Revision note (2026-10-26, after this report was written).** A fresh review revised `diagnosis.md`, and the
> "what this means for the game" paragraphs and ranking tables below quote the earlier version in places. Where they
> differ, the diagnosis wins. The revised readings are:
> - Idle compute is a quarter to two fifths of the bill by era (daily averages), not "about half".
> - "Money stops binding late" holds only in one traced run; across seeds, runway is shortest in eras 4–5.
> - The era 2 stall is real for the balanced and speed bots but escapable: a one-round card was often on the board.
>   The game's fault is that nothing points at it. Serving takes about 84% of online compute on an average era 2 day.
> - The grid reservation is a large lever (balanced bot: 5 against 69 overtaken of 200), not proof that it "decides
>   the late game"; the careful bot's 200 of 200 is confounded by its bot rules.
> - The denier probe is confounded (its deny rule skips the bill check), so "denial hurts the denier" is unproven.
> - Contract exits are unmeasured (no bot has a policy for them), not "unused".
> - Azuria's exclusivity also blocks the Gulf campus; the letter of intent appears in era 4 only; the random bot does
>   sign deals in era 5.

Real frontier labs get compute by committing to more than they can yet pay for. They sign multi-year **take-or-pay** contracts (you pay for the reserved capacity whether you use it or not), prepay to move up chip queues, and stack reservations whose real worth depends on strings, on the supplier's health and on the power grid. In 2026 those bargains got sharper. Rental contract prices **rose about 40%** instead of falling, which corrects the September report. The cheapest suppliers' bonds began trading like distressed debt. The slowest clocks are now grid queues, local permits and a Gulf export licence that expires in April 2027. The tensions that make good player decisions are the ones a player can read in one sentence and cause through their own choices: how much to commit against your own forecast, whether to pay for speed, whether to trust a cheap but fragile supplier, and whether to accept being switched off first in exchange for power sooner. Games make acquisition playable in a few proven ways. They give each player one decision per auction and a soft way out (Ra, Modern Art, For Sale). They let everyone's buying move one shared price (Power Grid, Offworld Trading Company). They make debt visible and tiered instead of permanent (Offworld, Brass). And they count shocks and show them in advance (Pandemic, Into the Breach). Free-form haggling against bots fails: Soren Johnson called his own attempt a "vending machine". This lines up with the measured state of Game Night AI Lab in `docs/research/compute-deals/diagnosis.md`. Today's deal board has one dominant card (CoreFlame, cheap, fast and safe at once, which the real market says cannot exist). Its prices never move, the player never picks a size, and a decisive era 2 bet is never shown as a bet. One caveat covers everything below. Every web source reached the researchers through a fetch tool that returns a model-written extraction of the page, and most board-game rule text comes from RulesPal, a site that writes its rule summaries with AI. Treat exact numbers as leads to check, not as settled facts.

## Real labs buy compute as stacked bets on their own growth

The September report found that real labs stack seven kinds of deal at once, and that prepayment buys queue position. That still holds. What the new notes add is how those bets behave under stress in 2026, and which stresses a player could feel.

### Labs signed more than their revenue supports, and the price of waiting went up

The defining 2026 story is a buyer whose own forecast wobbled. OpenAI told investors it targets about **$600B of total compute spending by 2030**. Altman said the world will be short of capacity for some time, and launched a "Guaranteed Capacity" product. It sells 1-, 2- or 3-year commitments, with bigger discounts for longer terms, until OpenAI's own allocation runs out ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/openai-new-guaranteed-capacity-offering-lets-customers-secure-long-term-ai-compute/), a news write-up of Altman's post, not the post itself). That $600B replaced the roughly **$1.4T** Altman had talked about in late 2025 ⚠️ (this comparison comes only from a search-result summary of Forklog citing CNBC; neither article was opened) ([Forklog via search](https://forklog.com/en/openai-reduces-spending-plans-by-800-billion-amid-stargate-project-setback/)). In late April 2026 the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI had missed internal revenue and user targets. It said CFO Sarah Friar had worried OpenAI might not be able to pay for future computing contracts, and that OpenAI had pulled back from Stargate projects in Texas, the UK and Norway. Supplier shares fell the next day ⚠️ (the WSJ article was not read; one short summary confirms the CFO worry and board scrutiny, and the rest is search synthesis) ([Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/openai-misses-revenue-and-user-growth-targets-9dc87aea)). OpenAI paused Stargate UK on 9 April 2026, citing high energy costs and unclear regulation ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/04/09/2026/openai-pauses-uk-stargate-project)).

Anthropic shows the other side of the same bet. A summary of a September 2026 report in The Information counts at least **14.8 GW and up to $517B** of reservations: AWS at $100B over 10 years for 5 GW of Trainium3 chips, Google and Broadcom at $200B over 5 years for 5 GW, and smaller blocks with Fluidstack, Nscale, SpaceX and Lambda. The summary calls them take-or-pay reservations kept off Anthropic's balance sheet ⚠️ (only a summary page was read; The Information's article was not; the listed items add to only about $475B and 10 GW, and the starting year is not stated) ([AI News Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/the-information-anthropic-has-locked-in-148-gw-of-compute-worth-up-to-517b)). The real pattern across these cases is that **the commitment survives and the schedule or site moves**. When CoreWeave's data center slipped in late 2025, customers agreed to new delivery dates instead of cutting the backlog ⚠️ (search synthesis; the article itself returned HTTP 403) ([DCD via search](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/coreweave-q3-earnings-show-revenue-backlog-doubled-to-556bn/)). Cancellation shows up as "paused", "exited a site" or "reduced the plan", not as a broken contract.

Meanwhile, waiting got more expensive. **Correction to the September report:** GPU rental prices have risen since October 2025. SemiAnalysis's H100 one-year contract index went from about $1.70 per GPU-hour in October 2025 to about $2.35 in March 2026. H100 contracts were renewing at their original rates or for four years, through 2028. On-demand capacity was sold out across GPU types, and lead times for NVIDIA's Blackwell chips ran into June–July 2026 ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/the-great-gpu-shortage-rental-capacity); the monthly index values and term structure are paywalled). The same source gives the first non-aggregator picture of terms. Large labs sign **4–5 year offtakes** (long contracts for a fixed block of capacity) of 50–100 MW, about 24,000–48,000 GB300 GPUs. Smaller labs sign 1–3 year deals with prepayment above 20%. Neoclouds (GPU-only cloud companies such as CoreWeave and Nebius) now push for higher prepayments, longer terms and flexible start dates. A vendor page reports Nebius raising its H100 rate from $3.85 to $4.50 on 1 October 2026, and some providers renting only in 8-GPU bundles, so a buyer who needs one GPU pays for seven idle ones ⚠️ (the vendor sells GPU time, and all prices are its own listings) ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-price-increase-2026-hyperbolic-h100/)).

Allocation is first come, first served in public, and ordered by balance sheet in practice. NVIDIA's 10-Q for the quarter to 26 July 2026 shows supply commitments rising from $119B to **$279B** in one quarter. It shows **$36B** of "AI cloud" agreements, under which NVIDIA commits to buy cloud capacity from the clouds it sells chips to, and those clouds may resell that capacity to others. It also shows five customers holding 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of receivables ⚠️ (a targeted extraction of the filing, not a read of the whole document) ([NVIDIA 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)). The next chip generation is late. TrendForce cut Rubin's share of NVIDIA's 2026 high-end shipments from 29% to 22%, with Blackwell filling 71%, because of new memory, networking, power and cooling demands ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/08/nvidia_supply_chain/)). Some buyers route around the queue entirely: Meta signed to rent Google's TPU chips ([Dataconomy](https://dataconomy.com/2026/02/27/meta-signs-multibillion-dollar-deal-to-rent-google-tpus-for-ai-training/), relaying Reuters, which was not opened). The September report's 10–15% prepayment terms for NVIDIA's multi-year purchase agreements, and its "use it or lose it" demand-inflation claim, are still unconfirmed by any primary source.

A futures market for compute was announced but has not opened. CME Group and Silicon Data announced H100 and B200 rental-index futures in August 2026. Each contract covers 730 GPU-hours (about one GPU for a month) and is cash-settled, meaning it pays out in money against an index price, not in GPUs ⚠️ (search synthesis; the CME release timed out) ([CME press release](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/cmegroup/en/media-room/press-releases/2026/8/11/cme_group_and_silicondatatolaunchcomputefuturesonoctober5tounloc.html)). The 5 October launch slipped to a pending 9 November start, still awaiting regulators ⚠️ (that page came back truncated, and why the date slipped is not stated) ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/silicon-data-cme-compute-futures-november-2026)). No trade had happened by 26 October 2026.

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis finds that sizing is not a decision: card sizes are fixed by rank and the player picks a card, never a size. It finds that idle compute is the normal state, with **about half** the compute bill paying for idle units in eras 1, 3 and 4. And it finds that "money stops binding late": in a traced run, era 4 jumped from 126 to 926 units online with 730 idle while cash rose to $5–10B. The real 2026 story is exactly the decision the game is missing. It is not "which supplier" but "how much to commit against a forecast you might miss". OpenAI's cut from $1.4T to $600B and Friar's worry are the cost of over-committing. Rising contract prices are the cost of under-committing and hoping to buy later. Today the game has neither cost. The over-committer probe already runs out of money in 200 of 200 runs, so the cost of over-committing exists only at the extreme. Prices never move, so waiting costs nothing.

### Strings, short leashes and fragile suppliers set a contract's true worth

The cleanest new example of a string is the shortest leash on a big contract. SpaceX's IPO filing describes Anthropic paying about **$1.25B a month through May 2029** for compute on xAI's Colossus clusters, with either side able to end it on **90 days' notice** ⚠️ (search synthesis of several outlets; the filing itself was not opened) ([Techloy via search](https://www.techloy.com/spacex-ipo-filing-reveals-anthropic-pays-41-million-a-day/)). Musk then said the real deal is a 180-day base lease followed by a mutual 90-day exit. He said the short term was SpaceX's request, and that SpaceX may need the compute back if compute gets very tight ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/musk-spacex-colossus-anthropic-180-day-lease)). Read as 36 months, the deal is worth about $45B. Read as six months, it is worth at most $7.5B. The headline and the binding commitment differ by a factor of six, and the supplier is a rival lab.

Some strings bind the supplier before they bind the buyer. Nebius's own filing for its Microsoft contract (about $17.4B through 2031, up to $19.4B) says Nebius's obligations begin only once Nebius confirms it has raised the financing. Microsoft may end a GPU service if delivery dates are missed after a grace period, and either side may exit after an uncured breach of 60 days ([Nebius 6-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1513845/000110465925088312/tm2525580d1_6k.htm), primary, read through the tool's extraction). So a buyer can sign a headline contract that does not start until a fragile supplier closes its loans. Waiting on that supplier costs the buyer time, not money.

The fragile layer became visibly fragile in October 2026. CoreWeave's second quarter showed revenue of $2.58B, a net loss of $626M and net interest expense of $640M. Total liabilities were about $72B against $5B of equity, and its backlog was about **$104B** at 30 June 2026 ([CoreWeave Q2 2026 release](https://investors.coreweave.com/news/news-details/2026/CoreWeave-Reports-Strong-Second-Quarter-2026-Results/); the debt split is the tool's own calculation from the balance sheet). This updates the September report's $99.4B at 31 March. On 14–15 October its 2032 bonds yielded about **14.8%**. A bond yield is the yearly return buyers of a company's debt demand, and a higher yield means the market sees more risk of default. Its shares fell about 16%. S&P rates it B+, well below investment grade ([investingLive](https://investinglive.com/stocks/coreweave-bonds-trade-like-distressed-debt-as-ai-spending-outpaces-balance-sheets/)). Insurance against a CoreWeave default (a credit default swap) cost more than 1,000 basis points a year, that is more than 10% of the insured amount ⚠️ (search synthesis only; the source pages returned HTTP 403) ([roic.ai via search](https://www.roic.ai/news/ai-debt-fears-surge-as-tech-default-insurance-hits-record-highs-10-15-2026)). Gartner forecast that **over 80% of independent neoclouds will stop operating independently by 2030**, mostly by being absorbed by hyperscalers (the giant general clouds such as Microsoft and Amazon), and said capacity already exceeds demand ([247WallSt](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/20/gartner-80-of-neocloud-firms-face-extinction-by-2030-as-ai-capex-outpaces-returns/)). The source itself holds a tension worth keeping: rental prices are rising at the same time as forecasts of oversupply. No named neocloud has actually defaulted. The failure evidence is market pricing and forecasts.

NVIDIA has become the backstop. Its 10-Q records **$108.5B** of maximum guarantee exposure. That includes lease guarantees capped at $105B for an OpenAI-affiliated campus in Ohio, which end when OpenAI reaches a satisfactory credit rating ⚠️ (partial extraction of the filing) ([NVIDIA 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)). A summary of SemiAnalysis describes a floor rate of about $2.35 per GPU-hour that NVIDIA guarantees partner clouds, against a market rate of $4.50–4.60, with NVIDIA taking a share of revenue above the floor ⚠️ (summary only; the $530B total exposure figure it gives is contested, and another critique counts about $164.5B) ([AI News Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/nvidias-off-balance-sheet-backstops-hit-530b-in-latest-10-q)). That credit-rating trigger is a string that works backwards: the support switches off just as the lab stops needing it. Financing has its own strings. Oracle's $16.3B financing for its Michigan campus was mostly 7.5% bonds, interest-only for six years and secured against the campus. US banks reportedly pulled back, and the rating agencies turned negative on Oracle ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/oracle-data-centre-16-billion-financing-stargate)). Traders pricing a possible December rate rise hit the most indebted builders hardest ([investingLive](https://investinglive.com/stocks/coreweave-bonds-trade-like-distressed-debt-as-ai-spending-outpaces-balance-sheets/)).

Headlines still shrink. The September report's main case was NVIDIA's "up to $100B" letter of intent to OpenAI (a letter of intent is a non-binding statement of a planned deal), which became a reported $30B investment. New cases follow the same pattern. Meta received a warrant (a right to buy shares at a fixed price) for 160 million AMD shares at one cent each, tied to buying 1 GW and then up to 6 GW of AMD GPUs. Its reported value is disputed, from "more than $100B" to "up to $60B" ⚠️ (snippets only; AMD's filing was not opened) ([Latham & Watkins via search](https://www.lw.com/en/news/latham-advises-amd-in-partnership-with-meta-to-deploy-6-gigawatts-of-gpus)). Bernstein reportedly sees only **35%** of announced North American capacity getting built ⚠️ (an aggregator snippet; no Bernstein document was opened) ([AI News Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/information-industry-execs-see-only-7-10-of-hundreds-of-neoclouds-surviving-as)).

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis says strings rarely bite. CoreFlame's fragility is one refinancing card at month 11. Azuria's exclusivity matters only if you wanted CoreFlame. The most interesting strings, Gulf and letter of intent, appear only in eras 3–4. Above all, CoreFlame is cheap (1.1x base), fast (one round), has no upfront payment and has only a mild string, so the balanced bot signs it **276 times in 100 runs**. The real market says a card cannot be cheapest, fastest and safest at once. In 2026 the cheap fast supplier is precisely the one whose bonds yield 14.8%. Real failure is driven by legible triggers: interest rates, a big customer's forecast cut, financing that does not close. That suits the owner's no-dice rule (2026-09-26), because a supplier can weaken visibly and for a stated reason instead of on a roll. The SpaceX lease also gives era 5, which today has no deals, a real-world beat. Capacity borrowed from a rival can be called back "if compute gets super tight", and that is exactly the endgame situation.

### Power, permits and licences run on slower clocks than chips

The most game-ready new power fact is a bargain that was offered and then torn up. PJM, the grid operator for much of the eastern US, proposed letting large loads of 50 MW or more connect sooner if they agreed to be **curtailed first** (switched off before others when the grid is short) ⚠️ (filing details from a search summary) ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-pjm-large-load-interconnection-iras-byonc/832716/)). On 9 October 2026 FERC, the federal energy regulator, rejected that plan. It said who gets curtailed first is for the states to decide, and one commissioner said non-critical data centers should be cut before homes, hospitals and schools ⚠️ (the page returned only its opening summary and excerpts) ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-pjm-large-load-interconnection-iras-byonc/832716/)). Texas runs the same bargain through ERCOT, whose large-load queue is now about **474 GW**, more than four times its record peak. **This corrects the September report's 238 GW.** A commissioned model for Google and another firm finds that loads taking ERCOT's faster, curtailable connection would be switched off in every modelled weather year: 50–241 hours in a typical year and up to 550 in an extreme one ⚠️ (only the executive summary of the 182 KB deck was read, and the study was paid for by interested parties) ([Aurora/ERCOT deck](https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2026/09/10/Curtailment-Mitigation-for-PCLRs-Modeling-the-Effects-of-Front-of-Meter-Netting_LLWG.pdf)). Google has made up to 1 GW of its load curtailable at peak through deals with five utilities ⚠️ (secondary coverage, snippet) ([Vozpopuli](https://www.vozpopuli.com/indux/en/google-is-signing-new-utility-deals-to-curb-data-center-power-use-during-peak-demand-and-the-ai-boom-is-now-forcing-big-tech-to-rethink-how-it-consumes-electricity/2862/)). The government also now claims data centers' backup generators in emergencies. A May 2026 Department of Energy order lets PJM direct data-center backup generation to run before firm load is cut ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/doe-emergency-order-signals-new-role-for-data-centers-backup-power.pdf)). And on 22 July 2026 a single line fault in Virginia knocked more than 3 GW of data-center load off the grid in seconds ([Data Center Knowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/energy-power-supply/fault-in-data-center-alley-triggered-3-gw-load-drop-on-pjm)).

Building your own gas is fast, and it is gated by permission and trust. xAI ran 27 gas turbines in Southaven, Mississippi without permits under a temporary exemption. The NAACP announced it would sue, and the state permit board then approved 41 permanent turbines in March 2026 despite unanimous local opposition at the hearing ([Mississippi Free Press](https://www.mississippifreepress.org/mississippi-permit-board-grants-xais-request-for-41-southaven-gas-turbines-to-power-memphis-data-center/)). Cleanview counts 56 GW of planned behind-the-meter capacity (power made on site instead of drawn from the grid), about 30% of tracked planned data-center capacity ([Power Engineering](https://www.power-eng.com/gas/data-center-power-crunch-lifts-engines-aeroderivatives-into-larger-role/)). A heavy-duty turbine ordered now reportedly would not arrive until 2031 ⚠️ (trade snippets) ([search summary](https://www.power-eng.com/gas/data-center-power-crunch-lifts-engines-aeroderivatives-into-larger-role/)). Meta's Louisiana campus now plans ten gas plants and 7.5 GW, for about $11B, with seven plants still awaiting state approval ([The Lens](https://thelensnola.org/2026/03/30/meta-triples-order-of-gas-fired-power-plants-for-its-ai-campus-in-louisiana/)). Nuclear is the slow, reputation-positive path, and it slips. The Palisades restart in Michigan missed late 2025, early 2026 and end-February 2026. Fuel loading began on 30 August and was paused after a fuel assembly tilted, and there was still no restart date on 22 October ([ANS Nuclear Newswire](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-07-08/article-8187/palisades-restart-projects-holtec-ipo-lawsuit-dismissal-but-no-restart-date/); [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/future/technology/2026/10/22/palisades-power-plant-nuclear-controversy/)). Three missed official dates make the September report's "50% chance of slipping 1–2 turns" look mild.

Announced capacity and online capacity are far apart. Epoch AI counts seven US Stargate sites with about 9.3 GW planned and only Abilene operating ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/openai-stargate-where-the-us-sites-stand)). **Correction:** Abilene was about 0.3 GW in April and 421 MW in July, not a running 1.2 GW campus ([Epoch Abilene explorer](https://epoch.ai/data/data-centers/satellite-explorer/OpenAIStargateAbilene)). Local opposition is the second constraint. **Correction:** the September report's "$64B plus $68B in Q2 2026" joined two different Data Center Watch reports. The Q2 2026 figure is about $68B across 45 projects, Q1 was about $130B across 75, and opposition now counts 843 groups in 49 states ([Political.org](https://political.org/2026/09/21/68-billion-in-data-center-projects-blocked-or-delayed-in-q2-2026-as-local-opposition-reaches-843-groups-across-49-states/)). The older $64B covers May 2024 to March 2025 ([Data Center Watch](https://www.datacenterwatch.org/report)), and a rival tracker's numbers cannot be reconciled with either ([The Deep Dive](https://thedeepdive.ca/one-tracker-found-47-troubled-us-data-centres-another-found-75-in-just-one-quarter/)). New York's governor paused state environmental permits for facilities of 50 MW or more for up to a year ([Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/07/new-york-governor-signs-nations-first-moratorium-large-data-centers/414752/)). Voters in Warrenton, Virginia ousted council members who backed an Amazon project ([Political.org](https://political.org/2026/09/21/68-billion-in-data-center-projects-blocked-or-delayed-in-q2-2026-as-local-opposition-reaches-843-groups-across-49-states/)). "Blocked or delayed" mostly means delayed, so opposition behaves like a delay timer with a chance of withdrawal.

The Gulf deal is now a chain of dated strings. Stargate UAE plans a 1 GW cluster inside a 5 GW campus, with a first 200 MW phase of about 100,000 GB300 chips. Its target slid to "4Q 2026", and no source confirms it is operating, which **updates the September report's "first 200 MW online in 2026"** ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ksa/2026/10/26/gulf-champions-and-western-firms-are-staying-the-course-on-ai-infrastructure-in-the-middle-east-but-more-attacks-could-change-their-calculus)). On 10 July 2026 BIS, the US Commerce bureau that licenses chip exports, moved the UAE into a more trusted export tier and let named entities receive advanced chips without a licence. But G42 and Core42's approval **expires 270 days later**, on 6 April 2027 by two law firms' reading or 10 April by a third, unless they become US companies or win further approval ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/us-eases-export-controls-for-the-uae-4-key-takeaways); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/07/bis-liberalizes-export), whose expiry text did not come through the fetch). G42 was reported in September to be weighing a sale of a majority stake to US investors to keep access; this is unconfirmed, and G42 declined to comment ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/g42-us-ownership-chip-access-export-control-endgame)). A war adds a third clock. Iranian drones struck three AWS data centers in the UAE and Bahrain on 1 March 2026 ([Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2026/gulf-war-aws-data-center-attack-ai-investment-risk/), an opinion essay). Iran's Revolutionary Guard then named Stargate UAE as a potential target ([DataCentreNews](https://datacentrenews.uk/story/iran-threat-puts-stargate-uae-data-centre-in-focus)), and the UAE is reportedly redesigning the campus as smaller hardened sites, a Reuters report relayed by a second outlet without independent confirmation ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/uae-stargate-data-centre-redesign-iran-threat-underground)). No licence has actually been revoked anywhere. The real leverage is the expiry date and its conditions, not a surprise cut-off.

Government leverage over compute runs through power and licences, not through seizing GPUs. In May 2026 BIS required licences for advanced chips going to China-headquartered firms outside China ([Khaleej Times/Reuters](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/business/tech/us-takes-step-to-halt-nvidia-ai-chip-shipments-to-chinese-firms-outside-china-2)). The notes found no 2026 use of the Defense Production Act (a US law that lets the government direct private production) on lab compute; only proposals turned up ⚠️ (snippets) ([IFP RFI response](https://files.nitrd.gov/90-fr-9088/IFP-AI-RFI-2025.pdf)). **This updates the September report:** its "Defense Production Act-style pooling" for era 5 has no real-world instance. Its closest honest basis is the AI 2027 scenario plus the emergency backup-generator orders above.

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis's sharpest finding is that a single cheap era 2 grid reservation ($50M, one team action) decides the late game. Careful bots with it end 193 aligned out of 200. Careful bots without it are **overtaken in 200 of 200 runs**. Yet nothing in era 2 tells the player this is the hinge, so it reads as a trap, not a plan. The real world offers the vocabulary to make that bet legible: a 474 GW queue, Microsoft walking from a UK site because the grid would not be ready until the 2030s, and a "connect sooner if you accept being cut off first" bargain that a regulator can strip. The Gulf licence gives a hard date, the kind of string that fits a no-dice game. Nuclear slippage and opposition delays are real too, but each is a one-shot choice. The diagnosis already lists the era 4 gas-or-nuclear site as real but one-shot, so these add flavour more than recurring decisions.

### Tensions that make good player decisions pass three tests

Across the real-world notes, the tensions that would play well share three traits. First, they fit one sentence and one visible number. Second, the player's own choice causes the outcome, not a random roll. Third, the cost of being wrong is felt in the race. By those tests, the strongest are: committing against your own forecast (OpenAI's $1.4T to $600B, Friar's worry), paying for speed when prices are rising (the H100 index up 40%), a supplier whose health you can watch (CoreWeave's 14.8% bonds), a fast connection that comes with being switched off first (PJM and ERCOT), a long headline with a short leash (SpaceX's 90 days), a licence with an expiry date (6 April 2027), and a headline that shrinks once signed (Bernstein's 35%). Buy-now-or-wait-for-the-next-chip (Rubin cut from 29% to 22%) is real and legible, but it is a timing bet the game already half-has in Verde's delayed order.

The notes agree on what is too fiddly for a 20-minute game: loan covenants and debt-coverage ratios, residual-value schedules, credit-default-swap maths, tranche-by-tranche penalties, PJM capacity-auction mechanics, export country groups, and compute futures (which had not traded by 26 October). The nearest real "revenue share" string, NVIDIA's floor-plus-share deals with partner clouds, is legible but has undisclosed percentages.

## Games turn scarcity into play by limiting each decision

No game models the full real menu of compute deals; the September report already found that the prototype covers more of it than any single title. What games offer is a set of proven devices. Each device works because of one or two rules that, if removed, break it. The notes identify those load-bearing rules.

A word on sources before the detail. The **Power Grid Deluxe** rulebook is the only auction rulebook read as raw text (converted from the publisher's PDF). ⚠️ Only its setup, phases 1–3 and the start of phase 5 were read; its build phase, Step 2/3 text, two-player rules and summary page were not. The **Quacks of Quedlinburg** rules PDF was read raw and in full. Almost every other rule claim comes from **RulesPal** (marked [RulesPal] below), whose index page is titled "Board Game Rules Answered by AI". One researcher treated it as a transcription; the other found it is an AI-written summary, which applies to every RulesPal citation in both notes. Soren Johnson's designer notes on Offworld Trading Company and Old World are the strongest design reasoning in the set.

### Fast auctions give each bidder one decision and a soft exit

The auctions that resolve quickly cap each player at one decision. In **Ra** (Reiner Knizia, 1999), each player bids once or passes, in order, and the auction always ends with the player who called it. If a caller invokes the auction on a part-filled track and everyone passes, the caller must bid [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Ra](https://rulespal.com/ra/rulebook), tables garbled). Bids are made with face-up sun disks, so everyone sees who can still afford what. A reviewer notes that opponents use this to bid low and force a high-disk holder into a bad lot ([Theology of Games](https://www.theologyofgames.com/blog/2016/10/8/here-comes-the-flood-a-double-take-review-of-ra)). (The review and RulesPal disagree on the number of sun disks, 13 against 16, and this was not resolved.) **Modern Art** (Knizia, 1992) ties the auction format to the card being sold: open, once-around, hidden, fixed price or double. In the fixed-price form, if nobody buys at the auctioneer's named price, the auctioneer must buy it themselves, which stops absurd prices [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Modern Art](https://rulespal.com/modern-art/rulebook), wording is the tool's paraphrase).

The best auctions build a consolation into leaving. In **For Sale** (Stefan Dorra), a player who drops out of a round takes the cheapest card left and gets half their bid back, and there are exactly as many cards as players, so nobody leaves empty-handed ([UltraBoardGames For Sale](https://ultraboardgames.com/for-sale/game-rules.php)). In **High Society** (Knizia, 1995), passing returns your money but loses the card. Each player has a fixed set of 11 money cards and no income, and the poorest player at the end is "cast out" and cannot win, which stops the "spend everything early" strategy [RulesPal] ([RulesPal High Society](https://rulespal.com/high-society/rulebook)). **Medici** lets the seller choose a bundle of one to three cards, which is a push-your-luck choice (a bet on how far to press before it goes wrong), while each ship's five-card limit keeps one player from buying everything [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Medici](https://rulespal.com/medici/rulebook)). An interview summarising Knizia says auctions create instant interaction between players, but need a stabilising rule such as Medici's cargo limit so one player does not snowball ([Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/medici/reiner-knizia-auction-board-games), the phrasing is the article's summary of Knizia).

Open ascending bidding is the slowest form. In **Power Grid**'s plant auction (Friedemann Friese, 2004), only the four "current market" plants can be bought, while four "future market" plants are visible but not biddable. Each player buys at most one plant a round, and a player who passes cannot re-enter that auction ⚠️ (partial read of the official rulebook) ([Power Grid Deluxe rules](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Power-Grid-Deluxe-Rules.pdf)). Take away the one-per-round limit and the leader buys everything. Take away the visible future market and nobody can plan. The only real-time auction found is **Merchants of Amsterdam** (Knizia, 2000), whose spring-driven clock counts the price down until a player slaps it ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/boardgame/merchants-of-amsterdam/)). That is a Dutch auction, where a falling price is taken by the first bidder to accept it. No source says how well the clock plays.

**What this means for the game.** Two of the diagnosis's problems meet here. Rivals take their named card at the end of the round, and the player who loses it gets nothing. And there is "no timing pressure": cards stay until signed or taken, and prices never move. The auction research points to formats that give the human exactly one prompt and let three bots decide instantly from visible budgets: once-around with the caller last, fixed price, or sealed bids. It also points to For Sale's consolation for the loser. Open back-and-forth bidding against three bots would either feel scripted or force the player to babysit.

### Shared markets create urgency through moving prices and expiry

"Wait or grab" tension needs an item whose price changes with time or position, visibly and slowly enough that a rival could take it first. Remove the gradient or the expiry and nobody ever acts early.

**Power Grid's resource market** is the clearest price ladder. Players buy fuel at the printed price of the space it sits on, the last-placed player buys first, and when a type runs out, nobody can buy more until the next refill. The refill adds a **fixed amount** per round, starting from the most expensive empty space. The rulebook's own example shows coal getting dearer after one heavy round while the other fuels get cheaper ⚠️ (partial read of the official rulebook) ([Power Grid Deluxe rules](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Power-Grid-Deluxe-Rules.pdf)). **This confirms a rule the September report had only from snippets.** The fixed refill is what makes prices climb: buy more than the refill and the cheap spaces never come back. If the refill simply replaced what was bought, prices would never move and the market would be a shop. Friese's stated aim was that "the leading player gets the biggest disadvantage" ([Opinionated Gamers](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/07/11/funkenschlag-at-fifteen-the-story-of-power-grid/)).

Rows of offers add expiry. In **Suburbia**, one tile always leaves the market each turn, either the one bought or one the player chooses to discard. The rest slide along and a new tile enters at the far end, and price follows position [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Suburbia](https://rulespal.com/suburbia/rulebook); [UltraBoardGames Suburbia](https://ultraboardgames.com/suburbia/game-rules.php)). **This corrects the September report**, which said the oldest tile is removed each turn. Neither rules page says "oldest", and the $10-to-free price scale is ⚠️ unverified. **Through the Ages** discards cards that drift into the cheapest end of a 13-card row before each refill: three spaces with two players, two with three, one with four. Waiting is therefore risky even when no rival wants the card [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Through the Ages](https://rulespal.com/through-the-ages/rulebook)). **Century: Spice Road** makes you place a cube on every card to the left of the one you take [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Century](https://rulespal.com/century-spice-road/rulebook)). ⚠️ The September report's claim that the taker also collects the cubes left on a card was not confirmed by the rule text read. **Concordia** prices a card by its position plus its own cost, and one action ignores the position cost [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Concordia](https://rulespal.com/concordia/rulebook)). Power Grid adds obsolescence tied to leaders: any plant numbered at or below a player's city count is removed at once, so the leader's growth puts a clock on everyone else ⚠️ (partial read) ([Power Grid Deluxe rules](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Power-Grid-Deluxe-Rules.pdf)). All of these fast markets show four to seven visible offers.

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis finds the same board every run: offer sizes depend only on rank, and 100 balanced runs make 100 grid reservations and 100 queue orders. Under the no-dice rule, variety cannot come from a random draw. Power Grid shows how to get variety without dice: prices move because of what every player bought, so the board changes because rivals and the player changed it. Suburbia and Through the Ages give "act now" a reason through a visible slide toward cheap-then-gone. Both answer the diagnosis's call for "a reason to act now".

### Denial is fun only as a side effect with a visible cost

Telegraphing a rival's next move (showing it in advance) turns guesses into puzzles. Subset Games made shown enemy attacks a core mechanic of **Into the Breach**, with fewer random outcomes, so that losses would feel like the player's own fault ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-)). Board games telegraph through public state. Power Grid shows the turn order, the future market and every player's plants and fuel. Ra shows disks, and Century shows each player's cubes.

Denial stays interesting when it comes free with something you wanted anyway. **Splendor**'s reserve action takes a card before a rival can and is also the only way to get gold, so denial is a side effect of a useful move [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Splendor](https://rulespal.com/splendor/rulebook)). In a **7 Wonders** draft, every pick is also a choice of what to pass to your neighbour [RulesPal] ([RulesPal 7 Wonders](https://rulespal.com/7-wonders/rulebook)). One review argues that denial grows stronger in two-player, winner-take-all games ⚠️ (snippet only) ([Miniature Market](https://miniaturemarket.com/reviewcorner/7wondersduel-review)). In Power Grid, a player who bids up a rival must pay if the rival drops out. Pure blocking, spending a turn only to stop someone, is the form that feels like a chore. Worker-placement games soften it with limits or a payoff for the blocked player ⚠️ (blog and forum summaries only) ([Punchboard](https://punchboard.beehiiv.com/p/the-joy-of-worker-placement)). The notes found no designer essay on why denial becomes a chore.

On catch-up, a League of Game Makers panel of seven designers mostly prefers a "headwind" built into the economy, where winning makes the next win cost more, over a bolted-on gift to the trailer, which one called "a bandage rather than a fix" ([League of Game Makers](https://leagueofgamemakers.com/ask-the-league-should-games-have-a-catch-up-mechanic)). Quacks gives every player except the leader "rat tails" that start their pot further along, one per space behind the leader ([Quacks rules](https://gusandco.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Quacksalber_Rules_English_v1.pdf)).

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis shows denial is not felt and, worse, hurts. The balanced bot denies 176 times in 100 runs with no visible effect. A probe that takes every card a rival named ends left behind 19 times and overtaken 15 times in 200 runs, against 0 and 5 for plain balanced play. That is the pure-block pattern exactly: denial costs race heat, pushes the player into worse cards, and the rival still grows off the board. The games say denial must either pay the denier something they wanted anyway or stop being presented as a fight. The diagnosis's fix list already names those two branches (item 9).

### Timing bets and debt work when the bill is visible, tiered and survivable

The games that make "lock in early or stay flexible" interesting share one structure. The commitment buys something now. Its cost keeps draining a currency later. And the trigger that punishes waiting is partly caused by rivals, so it can be watched and raced.

**18xx** railway games are the model. In **1830**, a new phase starts the moment anyone buys the first train of a new type, and older types are then removed from every company that holds them. Players call this "rusting". A company with a route but no train must buy one, paying from company cash first, then from the president's own money, then by selling shares, and bankruptcy ends the game [RulesPal] ([RulesPal 1830](https://rulespal.com/1830/rulebook)). The forced purchase is what turns rusting into a rush. Without it, rusting would just be a stat drop. Variants show the design dial. In System18's "UK Limited", trains rust on a fixed schedule, not on purchases, and the Mississippi variant gives new trains a warranty token ([System18 wiki](https://github.com/tobymao/18xx/wiki/System18)). A predictable rust turns the bet into a puzzle. A player-triggered rust makes it a race. A designer stripping 18xx down to its train rush named the core as companies being "both assets and liabilities" ([Snellman design diary](https://www.snellman.net/blog/archive/2010-10-11-design-diary-minimal-18xx-train-rush-game-1.html)).

Debt that taxes the future works when it is cheap early and costly late. A **Brass: Birmingham** loan gives £30 and drops income three levels, never below -10. If you cannot pay negative income, you remove your own buildings at half their cost, then lose points [RulesPal; the page has "TODO" gaps] ([RulesPal Brass](https://rulespal.com/brass-birmingham/rulebook)). That half-value sale is a soft landing: a bad commitment hurts without ending the game. **Age of Steam** shares give $5 each but cost $1 a turn and 3 points at the end, so a share is worth it only if it buys more than 3 points of position [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Age of Steam](https://rulespal.com/age-of-steam/rulebook)). **Food Chain Magnate** salaries make hiring a recurring commitment, and its milestones vanish once someone claims them, so they are a timing bet with an expiry [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Food Chain Magnate](https://rulespal.com/food-chain-magnate/rulebook)); a strategy guide calls the training milestone the most important one in the game ([Erik Twice](https://eriktwice.com/en/2021/06/18/food-chain-magnate-understanding-milestones/)).

The best-documented design reasoning is Johnson's on **Offworld Trading Company**. His first penalty for unmet needs was a stock-price hit that lasted the whole game, and it pushed players into playing it safe. He replaced it with debt that works like an unlimited line of credit, rated from AAA to D. Interest is 2–6% at the top ratings and jumps to 30% at D, which also locks you out of the black market. Even so, the best players found that ignoring debt to buy out rivals ("debt diving") was a dominant strategy, and the rating tiers exist to price it ([OTC Designer Notes #5](https://designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-5-debt)). Drawing on these games, the notes conclude that a commitment avoids being a no-brainer or a trap when four things hold. The cost is visible and tiered before you sign. Failure costs an asset you value, at a discount, rather than ending the game. There is a clear way to pay it down. And there is a reason to take it before you need it, as with Brass's cheap early loan.

**Sidereal Confluence** is the nearest board game to a delivery contract. Deals are binding even across turns, and broken promises cost points by a fixed table [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Sidereal Confluence](https://rulespal.com/sidereal-confluence/rulebook); [UltraBoardGames](https://ultraboardgames.com/sidereal-confluence/game-rules.php)). Neither note found a board game with a true locked-price futures contract (pay now, receive later at a fixed price). Roguelikes give a compact format for "a deal with a string". **Slay the Spire**'s opening blessing pairs one reward with one drawback and bans pairs that would cancel out, such as gaining 250 gold with losing all gold ([Slay the Spire Wiki](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Neow); some numbers garbled). **Balatro**'s Gros Michel gives a big bonus with a stated 1-in-6 chance each round of being destroyed ([Balatro Wiki](https://balatrowiki.org/w/Jokers)). That stated-odds string conflicts with the owner's no-dice rule, so only its "shown in advance" half transfers.

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis finds that scale-down, break and buy-out are unused: no bot uses them in 200 runs. It finds that money stops binding late, with runway falling to about 5 months in eras 4–5 while cash still grows from raises, and that compute is 82% of burn in era 4 and 91% in era 5, above the spec's 40–70% band. And it finds an era 2-to-3 cliff: the starter and CoreFlame contracts end together, leaving **0 compute online** in the first era 3 round on 50 of 50 seeds for the balanced and careful bots ⚠️ (measured with bots and one traced seed, not seen in a human play-through). Offworld's lesson fits the late-game slack: debt only matters if its price rises with how much you carry, in tiers the player can see. 18xx's shared phase trigger fits the cliff. A clock that everyone can see and that rivals' purchases advance gives the player a reason to renew before it strikes, where today nothing warns that the contracts all end at once.

### Negotiation against bots needs a price for asking

Johnson's own account is the strongest evidence here. Civilization III's open bargaining table was "a big mistake": the computer players traded among themselves like a cartel, and in Civilization IV diplomacy felt like walking up to a vending machine. Players simply searched for the offer the computer would accept. Adding uncertainty just swapped the vending machine for a slot machine, because players kept asking until they got the answer they wanted ([Old World Designer Notes #10](https://www.designer-notes.com/old-world-designer-notes-10-diplomacy)). **Old World** instead runs diplomacy through events that react to the game state. Requests cost resources, only one mission runs at a time through an ambassador, and missions take years, so they cannot be undone by reloading (same source). **Crusader Kings 3** stores leverage as "hooks", a favour owed or a means of coercion. A weak hook is used once and adds acceptance. A strong one forces acceptance with a five-year cooldown, and you can hold only one hook on a character ([CK3 Wiki](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Hooks)). **Frostpunk 2** lets you buy council votes with promises that usually carry a time limit, and breaking one hurts relations with that faction ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/frostpunk-2/council), a press guide, not an official wiki).

Board-game negotiation mostly does not transfer, because it runs on reading a human face. **Chinatown** reveals everyone's tiles at once and then lets all trades happen simultaneously, and money is secret [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Chinatown](https://rulespal.com/chinatown/rulebook)). **Cosmic Encounter** gives negotiators one minute and a penalty if no deal is reached [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Cosmic Encounter](https://rulespal.com/cosmic-encounter/rulebook)). **Diplomacy** makes no promise enforceable ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomacy_(game)); ⚠️ the last 15,000 of 115,473 characters were not read). Two rhythms survive against bots. Chinatown's "reveal all of this period's offers at once, then act" suits a real-time game. And Sidereal Confluence's fixed price for a broken promise makes "binding" mean something to a computer player that cannot feel social pressure.

**What this means for the game.** The diagnosis says there is "no reading of rivals": rival appetite is fixed and printed on the cards, and nothing needs inferring. The fix is not a haggling screen, which Johnson's experience predicts would collapse into searching for the accepted offer. What survives is asking that costs something, rival needs the player can see, and stored favours with a strength and an expiry. Those fit letters of intent and exclusivity clauses better than a menu item does.

### Shocks feel fair when they are counted and shown in advance

The best shocks are bounded, countable and partly visible. **Pandemic** shuffles one epidemic card into each of several equal piles, so players know how many shocks will come and roughly when. Its "Intensify" step puts recently infected cities back on top of the deck, and players may look at the discard piles at any time, so they can predict the next hit [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Pandemic](https://rulespal.com/pandemic/rulebook)). **Power Grid** places its Step 3 card under the plant stack, and when it appears the market shrinks to six plants with no future market [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Power Grid](https://rulespal.com/power-grid/rulebook)). ⚠️ The shuffle detail comes from a snippet of a fan-hosted copy. **Ra**'s epoch ends the moment the Sun Boat reaches the last space of a track driven by a known number of tiles in the bag [RulesPal] ([RulesPal Ra](https://rulespal.com/ra/rulebook)).

Offworld's black market shows how a price can reveal rivals' actions. Every purchase of a sabotage item raises its price for everyone, so a jump tells all players that someone bought one, though not who or whom they will hit. That pushes players to buy defences. Without rising prices, the designers feared players would spend everything hurting each other ([OTC Designer Notes #13](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-13-black-market-part-i)). Johnson also removed unexplained luck. A 50% pirate roll that "felt streaky" became a sure hit that steals half, and remaining odds are shown on mouseover, because if players roll a virtual die it should be as transparent as possible. Items a computer opponent could not use convincingly were turned off by default in single-player, because players would assume the AI was cheating. (The note attributes these points to the three black-market notes together, without saying which part holds which: [Part I](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-13-black-market-part-i), [Part II](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-14-black-market-part-ii/), [Part III](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-15-black-market-part-iii/).) Push-your-luck games let the player choose how close to stand to the shock. In Quacks, your pot explodes if your white chips total more than 7. An exploded pot forces a choice between points and coins instead of costing everything, and a white chip is added each round so the risk rises over the game ([Quacks rules](https://gusandco.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Quacksalber_Rules_English_v1.pdf)). Can't Stop works the same way with dice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Can%27t_Stop_(board_game))).

**What this means for the game.** Three diagnosis findings are shocks or near-shocks: the era 2-to-3 contract cliff with no warning, CoreFlame's refinancing card at month 11, and the era 2 stall. The games say a shock is fair when the player knows it is coming and how big it is. Offworld's price-reveals-a-buyer is a no-dice way to make rival moves visible. That answers the diagnosis's "something to read (rivals' intent)". Dice-driven push-your-luck conflicts with the owner's rule. The transferable part is the player choosing how far to press against a visible limit.

### Real-time games show the constraint as a number with the fix beside it

**Offworld Trading Company** is the best-documented real-time market against computer rivals. It has one game-wide price per resource that every player's buying moves ([OTC Designer Notes #2](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-2-free-markets/)), matches of about 20 minutes with market values "front and center" ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/93533/offworld-trading-company-taking-stock-in-space)), and orders allowed while paused ([Matchstick Eyes](https://matchstickeyes.com/2014/10/01/offworld-trading-company-interview-with-soren-johnson)). Late in development, the team let players buy a rival's shares one at a time, so remaining stock became **a visible countdown** to a buyout. They did this because players kept asking how close a rival was ([OTC Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)). Before that fix, early-access players reported losing within seconds to computer buyouts they could not see coming, and a developer replied that the team would make it more transparent ⚠️ (only 15 of 49 comments were shown, from a 2015 pre-release build) ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/271240/discussions/0/618453594754245349)). The opposite case is **Anno 1800**, whose trade a reviewer found fiddly and opaque. It was not clear what a route earned, and a ship assigned to several routes failed without notice ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/anno-1800/review)).

Timed offers work when the window is shown in the game's own clock. **RimWorld** traders stay about 16 in-game hours, and goods a trader does not want are greyed out ⚠️ (only 100,000 of 115,032 characters of the wiki page were read) ([RimWorld Wiki](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Trade)). **Against the Storm** shows how long a trader remains, a fairness bar and an "Auto" button ⚠️ (search summary only; the wiki returned HTTP 403 and may be stale) ([Against the Storm wiki](https://wiki.hoodedhorse.com/Against_the_Storm/Trading)). **OpenTTD** announces subsidies to everyone and gives them to the first company to deliver ([OpenTTD wiki](https://wiki.openttd.org/en/Manual/Subsidy)). **Victoria 3** cut its notifications by about half and made auto-pause configurable per message type ([Victoria 3 Dev Diary #74](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/victoria-3/news/dev-diary-74-ux-improvements)).

Splitting one pool among uses fails in recognisable ways. In **Oxygen Not Included**, players push every task to the top priority, and the developers called their 2018 priority system "hard to understand" and simplified it ([ONI wiki](https://oxygennotincluded.wiki.gg/wiki/Versions/OC-253538); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/457140/discussions/0/1488861734118395135), ⚠️ 15 of 17 comments shown). A **Cities: Skylines** player never noticed that the two sliders per service were day and night, because the icons were too small ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/255710/discussions/0/1776010325128559462)). The fixes designers shipped were to show the consequence at once and to explain what blocks an action. **Factorio**'s power screen shows how much demand is met, and when supply falls short every machine slows in proportion ([Factorio wiki](https://wiki.factorio.com/Electric_system)). Victoria 3 added tooltips that say why something is blocked and moved relevant information higher up ([Victoria 3 Dev Diary #74](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/victoria-3/news/dev-diary-74-ux-improvements)).

**What this means for the game.** This is the closest match in the whole set to the diagnosis's confusion findings. In era 2, serving takes 62% of online compute for the balanced bot, and **in every era 2 round of all 100 seeds no training run fits the free compute** ⚠️ (measured with bots and one traced seed, not seen in a human play-through; a playtester reported the same stuck state). The compute bar's right handle sits at the far edge and does nothing visible until it passes a small marker. Training has no handle. The HUD's Compute button opens an information screen with no way to the deal board, which is two menus deep. And the CFO's idle-compute line fires almost every round, so it stops meaning anything. Offworld, Factorio and Victoria 3 all say the same thing: make the constraint a visible number before the player hits it, and put the fix beside it. Offworld's early-access thread adds a warning that the diagnosis echoes, that "a stall that hurts nothing reads as a bug". An unexplained outcome against computer rivals reads as a bug or as cheating.

## Conclusion

The research shifts where the drama in compute deals lives. In September, the lesson was "obligations, not shopping". The 2026 evidence adds that obligations are dramatic only when two clocks run against each other: your own forecast, which can fall (OpenAI's $1.4T to $600B), and the market's price, which can rise (H100 contracts up 40%). A lab that commits too much pays for idle compute and frightens its CFO. A lab that waits pays more and joins a sold-out queue. Game Night AI Lab currently runs neither clock: prices are frozen, sizes are fixed, and money stops binding exactly when the gigawatt era should make it bind hardest. The games research says the fix lies less in adding suppliers than in letting player and rival actions move shared numbers that everyone can see, as in Power Grid and Offworld. That is also the only route to run-to-run variety the no-dice rule allows.

The second shift is about fairness. Nearly every successful device in the notes is visible before it bites: Power Grid's future market, Pandemic's counted epidemics, Into the Breach's shown attacks, Offworld's share countdown and debt tiers, and the Gulf licence's printed expiry date. Nearly every diagnosis failure is the opposite: a stall nobody is told about, a cliff nobody is warned of, a decisive grid reservation nobody is told matters, and a denial whose cost appears only in endings. Real-world deals supply the strings, but the games show that a string becomes a decision only when its trigger can be watched.

## Ranking: which tensions and mechanics transfer best

The ranking weighs three things: how clean the evidence is, how well the item fits a 20-minute real-time game with one human against three bots and no dice, and how many diagnosis problems it addresses. The ranking is judgement. The notes contain no playtest of any of these.

**Real tensions, ranked**

| Rank | Real tension | Key evidence | Diagnosis problems it addresses |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Commit against your own forecast: over-commit and carry idle bills, under-commit and pay more later | OpenAI $1.4T to $600B ⚠️ and Friar's worry ⚠️; Anthropic's stack ⚠️; renewals at original rates or for four years | Sizing is not a decision; idle compute is the normal state; money stops binding late |
| 2 | Speed costs money, and waiting costs more when prices rise | H100 one-year index up about 40% (a correction); on-demand sold out; smaller labs prepay above 20% | CoreFlame is cheap, fast and safe at once; no timing pressure |

**Game mechanics, ranked**

| Rank | Mechanic (game) | What makes it work | Diagnosis problems it addresses |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Constraint shown as a number, with "why it is blocked" and the fix beside it (Offworld share countdown, Factorio, Victoria 3) | The player sees the limit before hitting it and the remedy at the point of action | Era 2 stall ⚠️; no path from "need compute" to "get compute"; confusing bar; the cliff with no warning |
| 2 | One shared price moved by everyone's buying, with a fixed refill (Power Grid fuel market ⚠️ partial rulebook read; Offworld market) | Rival demand moves price with no negotiation; a price jump reveals that someone bought | Same board every run; no timing pressure; no reading of rivals |
| 3 | Debt with visible tiers and a survivable failure (Offworld bond ratings; Brass loans and half-value sales [RulesPal]) | The cost rises with how much you carry, is shown before you sign, and failure costs assets rather than the game | Money stops binding late; exits unused; idle compute |
| 4 | A shared phase clock that rival purchases advance (1830 train rush [RulesPal]; Power Grid's visible future market ⚠️) | Everyone can watch and race the trigger; forced purchase turns obsolescence into a rush | Hidden era 2 bet; contract cliff; no timing pressure |
| 5 | One-decision auctions with a consolation for the loser (Ra [RulesPal], Modern Art fixed price [RulesPal], For Sale) | The human gets one prompt and bots decide instantly; leaving still gets you something | Losing a named card gets you nothing; no timing pressure |
| 6 | A sliding row with expiry at the cheap end (Suburbia [RulesPal], with its row rule corrected; Through the Ages [RulesPal]) | Waiting saves money but risks a rival or expiry | No timing pressure; same board every run |
| 7 | Denial as a side effect with a cost (Splendor reserve [RulesPal]; Power Grid bid-up ⚠️) | The denier gains something it wanted anyway; blocking is paid for | Denial is not felt and hurts the denier |
| 8 | One benefit paired with one string, and stored favours with expiry (Slay the Spire's blessing; CK3 hooks; Sidereal Confluence's broken-promise table [RulesPal]) | Each offer reads in two lines; strings have a price and a clock | Strings rarely bite; 9–11 facts per card |
| 9 | Counted, telegraphed shocks (Pandemic piles [RulesPal]; Into the Breach) | Players know how many shocks will come and can prepare | Contract cliff; refinancing card |

Some mechanics transfer poorly. Free-form negotiation against bots collapses into the vending machine. Open ascending bidding is too slow against three bots. Dice-driven push-your-luck and Balatro's stated odds conflict with the no-dice rule. High Society's fixed money with no income conflicts with a game that has income. And the Dutch clock of Merchants of Amsterdam is the only real-time auction found, but no source evaluates how it plays.
