# Who really sits on frontier AI lab boards (research for the game's board)

Compiled 2026-09-26. Every claim carries a dated source. Claims marked ⚠️ rest on a source I could not read in full; the note says what was missing. The coverage statement at the end lists every page opened.

## Bottom line

Real frontier-lab boards are not mostly finance people. The two labs whose boards can actually fire a CEO, OpenAI and Anthropic, deliberately mix in people with **no equity**: an AI-alignment researcher, a former NSA director, a machine-learning professor who can halt model releases, nonprofit trustees who elect directors, and, from outside the boardroom, state attorneys general who set conditions on the structure. The only CEO firing so far (OpenAI, November 2023) was carried out by a board with no investor on it, over **candor**. Investors (Microsoft, Thrive) then played a big part in reversing it, from outside the board. The labs owned by big companies or controlled by founders (Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI/SpaceX) have no board that could realistically remove the lab's leader.

## 1. OpenAI

### Structure over time
- **2015 to 2019.** A nonprofit, OpenAI, Inc. In 2019 it created a capped-profit subsidiary but stayed its "only controlling shareholder". A majority of the nonprofit's board was barred from holding financial stakes in the subsidiary (Wikipedia, "OpenAI", section on the 2019 restructuring; ⚠️ read only the relevant sections of a 130,000-character page).
- **2023 board.** The board that fired Altman was Ilya Sutskever (chief scientist) and three independent directors: Adam D'Angelo (Quora CEO), Tasha McCauley (technology entrepreneur) and Helen Toner (Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology). The announcement stated that "independent directors do not hold equity in OpenAI" (OpenAI, "OpenAI announces leadership transition", Nov 17, 2023, archived copy). The board had shrunk earlier that year when Reid Hoffman, Shivon Zilis and Will Hurd resigned, which left four members able to remove Altman (Wikipedia, "Removal of Sam Altman from OpenAI").
- **2025 recapitalization.** The nonprofit became the **OpenAI Foundation**, and the for-profit became **OpenAI Group PBC** (a public benefit corporation, a for-profit company legally allowed to weigh a stated mission alongside profit). The Foundation holds about 26% of the PBC, worth about $130B at the time, plus a warrant for more shares if the valuation rises a lot (OpenAI, "Built to benefit everyone", Bret Taylor, Oct 28, 2025). Microsoft holds about 27% and employees and investors the remaining 47% (Wikipedia, "OpenAI", citing NYT and CNBC of Oct 28, 2025; ⚠️ partial read).

### The 2023 firing and reinstatement
- **Stated reason.** Altman "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities" (OpenAI, Nov 17, 2023).
- **Sutskever's role.** Reporting credited him as instrumental. He had had his role reduced by Altman in October 2023 and then "successfully appealed to several members of the board". Three days later he signed the employee letter demanding the board resign, and he apologized publicly (Wikipedia, "Removal of Sam Altman…", citing Kara Swisher, WSJ and The Verge). The New Yorker reported in April 2026 that Sutskever and others had compiled a roughly 70-page dossier for the board alleging a "consistent pattern of … lying", especially on safety issues (same Wikipedia article).
- **Toner's account (May 2024).** She said Altman withheld information (about the ChatGPT release and his ownership of OpenAI's startup fund) and gave "inaccurate information about the small number of formal safety processes". She also said two executives reported "psychological abuse" to the board (same Wikipedia article, summarizing her TED AI Show interview; I did not listen to the podcast itself).
- **The counter-pressure came from outside the board.** 745 of 770 employees threatened to quit. Microsoft offered Altman a job, and Microsoft, Thrive, Sequoia and Tiger Global pushed for his return. Satya Nadella helped mediate (same Wikipedia article). Altman returned on Nov 21–22, 2023 with an interim board of **Bret Taylor (chair), Larry Summers and D'Angelo**.
- **The official review (Mar 8, 2024).** WilmerHale found "a breakdown in trust between the prior Board and Mr. Altman". It found the decision did **not** arise from product safety, the pace of development, finances or investor statements. It found the board acted "within its broad discretion" but that Altman's "conduct did not mandate removal". Altman rejoined the board, and Sue Desmond-Hellmann, Nicole Seligman and Fidji Simo were added. OpenAI also created a whistleblower hotline and a Mission & Strategy committee (OpenAI, "Review completed & Altman, Brockman to continue to lead OpenAI", Mar 8, 2024, archived copy). **The official finding (not about safety) and Toner's account (partly about safety processes) conflict.** That conflict is a useful game mechanic in itself.

### Current board (as of September 2026)
From CNBC (Jul 21, 2026) plus the Christiano announcement (Sep 9, 2026): **Bret Taylor** (chair; ex-Salesforce co-CEO), **Sam Altman**, **Adam D'Angelo**, **Sue Desmond-Hellmann** (ex-Gates Foundation CEO, health), **Zico Kolter** (CMU professor; Foundation board only), **Paul Nakasone** (national security), **Adebayo Ogunlesi** (infrastructure investor), **Nicole Seligman** (ex-Sony general counsel), **David Vélez**, **Robin Vince**, **Paul Christiano**. ⚠️ OpenAI's own structure page (openai.com/our-structure) returned HTTP 403 and has no archived copy. The same list of 11 appears in a Unite.AI restatement of that page, but that column is labeled AI-generated. Fidji Simo is no longer on the board and is now an OpenAI executive. Wikipedia lists her board tenure as 2024–2025 and calls her OpenAI's head of AGI deployment (⚠️ partial read; I did not confirm the exact date she left).

### What the attorneys general imposed (Oct 27–28, 2025)
California and Delaware AGs regulate charities; Delaware's AG acts as "representative of beneficiaries", which for OpenAI means "all of humanity". Neither objected to the recapitalization, but both extracted conditions (Delaware AG press release, Oct 28, 2025; California AG–OpenAI memorandum of understanding (MOU), Oct 27, 2025):
- The Foundation alone appoints and removes all PBC directors, through Class N stock.
- On **safety and security**, PBC directors must consider **only the mission**, not shareholders' financial interests.
- The **SSC stays a committee of the nonprofit**. It can require mitigations "up to and including halting the release of models … even where the applicable risk thresholds would otherwise permit release".
- The SSC chair (Kolter) sits on the Foundation board only, with full observer rights at the PBC.
- Within a year, at least one more Foundation director must serve only on the Foundation board.
- The AGs get advance notice of governance changes. California gets semiannual meetings with the Mission & Strategy committee and 21 days' notice before any change of control or headquarters move. The PBC must stay in California.
- Critics said the nonprofit is "nominally" in control and that dual board membership creates "a bazillion conflicts of interest" (CalMatters, Oct 2025).

Other outside pressure: Musk's $97.4B unsolicited bid for the nonprofit (Feb 2025) and his lawsuit to undo the restructuring, which he lost in May 2026 on statute-of-limitations grounds (Wikipedia, "OpenAI"; ⚠️ partial read).

## 2. Anthropic

### The Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT)
- **What it is.** A Delaware "purpose trust" of up to five trustees with **no financial stake**. It holds **Class T stock**, which lets it elect and remove a growing share of the board: "the Trust will elect a majority of the board within 4 years". It is meant for "extreme events" (for example, making sure leadership evaluates catastrophic risks or has nation-state-level security), not day-to-day decisions. It also receives notice of actions that could significantly alter the company (Anthropic, "The Long-Term Benefit Trust", Sep 19, 2023, including a later footnote).
- **The escape hatch.** Large enough **stockholder supermajorities can change the Trust without trustee consent**, and the required thresholds rise over time (same source). Critics say the thresholds are not public, so the Trust's real power cannot be judged (Zach Stein-Perlman, LessWrong, May 27, 2024).
- **Initial trustees (2023).** Neil Buddy Shah (chair; Clinton Health Access Initiative), Jason Matheny (RAND), Kanika Bahl (Evidence Action), Paul Christiano (ARC) and Zach Robinson (Effective Ventures). In other words: global health, national security, AI safety and effective-altruism nonprofits.
- **Turnover.** Matheny left in Dec 2023 (conflict with RAND's policy work). Christiano left in Apr 2024 for the US AI Safety Institute. Richard Fontaine (CEO of the Center for a New American Security) joined in May 2025. Bahl and Robinson left in Jan 2026, and Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar (ex-California Supreme Court justice, Carnegie Endowment) joined in Jan 2026. Ben Bernanke (former Fed chair) joined in Jul 2026 to help the company "understand how AI is changing the economy" (Anthropic LTBT page footnote; Anthropic, Jan 21, 2026; CNBC, Jul 9, 2026). Cuéllar then **left the Trust to become Anthropic's first chief global affairs officer** (CNBC, Aug 4, 2026).
- **Current trustees.** Shah, Fontaine and Bernanke (Anthropic company page, read Sep 26, 2026). That is three people, below the original five.

### The board
- **Current directors.** Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, **Yasmin Razavi** (Spark Capital; holds the seat reserved for Series C and later investors), **Reed Hastings** (Netflix; appointed by the Trust, May 28, 2025), **Chris Liddell** (ex-CFO of Microsoft and GM, deputy White House chief of staff in Trump's first term; appointed Feb 13, 2026) and **Vas Narasimhan** (Novartis CEO, physician-scientist; appointed by the Trust, Apr 14, 2026) (Anthropic company page; Anthropic announcements of those dates).
- With Narasimhan's appointment, Anthropic said **Trust-appointed directors became a majority** (Apr 14, 2026). The Trust earlier appointed Jay Kreps (Confluent CEO, May 29, 2024), who replaced Luke Muehlhauser (Open Philanthropy).
- ⚠️ Kreps is no longer on the company page. A LessWrong commenter says he stepped down on May 22, 2026, citing a LinkedIn post I did not open. Whether the Trust still holds a majority on the six-member board is not publicly clear.

### Investor rights
- Amazon (about $8B invested by Nov 2024) and Google (over $3B) are large holders (Wikipedia, "Anthropic"; ⚠️ partial read).
- **Google owns about 14%, capped at 15%, with "no voting rights, no board seats and no board observer rights"**, according to court filings obtained by the NYT (quoted in Spyglass, 2025). ⚠️ Spyglass is paywalled after that excerpt, and I did not read the NYT article.
- An Anthropic spokesperson told TIME (May 2024) that **Amazon and Google do not own voting shares**, so they cannot elect directors and do not count toward the supermajority that could rewrite the Trust. ⚠️ TIME returned an error; I saw this only as a quotation in the LessWrong comments.
- So the big strategic investors supply cash and compute but are shut out of formal control. **Voting investors get one designated seat (Razavi).**

## 3. Other labs and oversight bodies

- **Google DeepMind (inside Alphabet).** There is no independent board. Internal review runs through the **Responsibility and Safety Council**, co-chaired by COO Lila Ibrahim and VP Responsibility Helen King, which checks projects against Google's AI Principles. Beside it sits an **AGI Safety Council** led by co-founder Shane Legg (DeepMind "Responsibility & Safety" page, read Sep 2026). DeepMind's 2014 acquisition came with an AI ethics board whose members were never disclosed. Its executives' long push for more autonomy from Google ended with the 2023 merger with Google Brain (Wikipedia, "Google DeepMind"; ⚠️ partial read). At the Alphabet level, a new **Risk and Compliance Committee** (Oct 2025) oversees legal, policy, reputational and operational risks "including those related to AI". The board recommended against a 2026 shareholder proposal to give a committee formal AI oversight (Alphabet 2026 proxy statement; ⚠️ searched, not read in full, and I did not verify the vote outcome).
- **Meta.** Mark Zuckerberg's dual-class shares give him voting control. In May 2024 he created an unpaid **AI and product advisory group** (Patrick Collison, Nat Friedman, Tobi Lütke, Charlie Songhurst). These are not elected directors and have no fiduciary duty (CNN, May 23, 2024). At the 2026 annual meeting, activist shareholders filed proposals on AI training data, a data-protection impact assessment for AI chatbots, and dual-class recapitalization. The board opposed all of them and pointed to its audit & privacy committee (Boardroom Alpha meeting summary, May 27, 2026; I did not open the proxy itself).
- **xAI (now SpaceXAI).** Incorporated in 2023 as a Nevada public benefit corporation, xAI **quietly dropped that status by May 9, 2024**. Musk's own lawyer still called it a PBC in May 2025 court filings (CNBC, Aug 25, 2025). SpaceX acquired it on Feb 2, 2026 (Wikipedia, "xAI"; ⚠️ partial read). SpaceX's IPO filing gives Musk **about 85% of the vote** and the power to "elect, remove or fill any vacancy" among directors. The company is a "controlled company" (a listed company controlled by one holder, exempt from some board-independence rules) (CNN on the S-1, May 21, 2026; I did not open the S-1 itself). In practice, nobody can fire the CEO.
- **Mistral.** ASML led a €1.3B round for about 11% and got a seat on Mistral's **Strategic Committee**, taken by its CFO Roger Dassen (ASML press release, Sep 9, 2025). This is the clearest example of a **strategic industrial investor** getting a governance seat in exchange for capital and partnership.
- **Governments as quasi-overseers.** OpenAI's SSC seat went to Christiano, who evaluates frontier models inside the US government and recuses from OpenAI matters (OpenAI, Sep 9, 2026). The Pentagon designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" in March 2026 after Anthropic refused to drop limits on surveillance and autonomous weapons. A federal judge blocked the designation (Wikipedia, "Anthropic"; ⚠️ partial read). Antitrust regulators were the reason Microsoft gave up its OpenAI observer seat (CNN, Jul 10, 2024).

## 4. Kinds of board members and what they actually push on

| Type | Real examples | What they push or vote on in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Founder or CEO director | Altman; Dario and Daniela Amodei; Musk | Keeps control, speed and fundraising. Altman insisted that directors who removed him resign. Musk's super-voting stock makes removal impossible |
| Governance independent (lawyer or ex-executive) | Seligman, Taylor, Liddell | Process, conflicts policy, audit, IPO readiness. Taylor ran the post-crisis review and rebuilt the board |
| Candor and ethics watchdog (policy academic) | Toner, McCauley | Whether the CEO tells the board the truth. Voted to fire Altman for not being "consistently candid" |
| Safety or alignment researcher | Kolter, Christiano, Sutskever (2023) | Release decisions. The SSC can delay or halt launches and reviewed o1. Christiano joined saying the industry is off track |
| National-security figure | Nakasone; Fontaine and Matheny on the LTBT | Cybersecurity, model-weight security, relations with government and the military |
| Mission trustee (no equity) | Buddy Shah; the OpenAI Foundation board | Elects and removes directors, guards the mission, handles rare extreme-risk decisions rather than daily strategy |
| Money: growth investor or financier | Razavi (investor-elected seat), Ogunlesi, Vélez, Vince | Capital, compute financing, valuation, IPO timing |
| Strategic partner or big-tech backer | Microsoft (27%, observer seat 2023–24); Amazon and Google at Anthropic (no votes); ASML at Mistral | Exclusivity, cloud deals, protecting its investment. Microsoft was decisive in reinstating Altman without holding a vote |
| Sector expert (health, economics) | Desmond-Hellmann, Narasimhan, Bernanke, Summers | Safe deployment in regulated industries, economic disruption |
| Outside regulator (not a member) | California and Delaware AGs; the Pentagon; antitrust agencies | Conditions on restructuring (the safety-only-mission rule, SSC halting power, notice rights), blacklisting, forcing observers off the board |

Patterns that matter for game design:
1. **Firing needs a non-investor majority, and rehiring runs through investors and staff.** In 2023 the firing needed only four independents, while the reversal came from employees and Microsoft outside the board.
2. **Safety power lives in a committee, not the full board.** OpenAI's SSC holds a launch veto. Anthropic's Trust controls seats but stays out of daily choices.
3. **The mission-guardians lose people to the company.** Examples: Cuéllar left the Trust to become an executive, Christiano left the Trust for government, and Simo left the board for an executive role.
4. **Scandal removes directors** (Summers, 2025).

## 5. Board archetypes for the game

Seven types, mixing money and non-money, each based on a real person or body:

1. **The Safety Chair** (non-money). Cares about whether the next model is safe to ship and holds a launch veto. *Based on Zico Kolter's Safety and Security Committee, which can halt releases under the 2025 AG deal.*
2. **The Alignment Hawk** (non-money). Cares about loss of control and says so publicly; joins after incidents and threatens to go public. *Based on Paul Christiano joining OpenAI's board in Sep 2026.*
3. **The Candor Watchdog** (non-money). Cares whether the CEO tells the board the truth, and will vote to fire over a pattern of spin. *Based on Helen Toner and the Nov 2023 "not consistently candid" firing.*
4. **The Securocrat** (non-money). Cares about model-weight security, espionage and good relations with government and defense. *Based on Paul Nakasone (ex-NSA) and Richard Fontaine (CNAS) on the Anthropic Trust.*
5. **The Mission Trustee** (non-money, holds no shares). Cares about the charter and elects or removes other directors rather than voting on products. *Based on Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust and the OpenAI Foundation's power over the PBC board.*
6. **The Strategic Backer** (money). Cares about its cloud deal and its stake; often has no vote but huge leverage in a crisis. *Based on Microsoft's 27% stake, its 2023 reinstatement role and its observer seat.*
7. **The IPO Financier** (money). Cares about capital, infrastructure financing and getting the company ready to list. *Based on Adebayo Ogunlesi, and on David Vélez and Robin Vince, added in 2026 ahead of OpenAI's possible IPO.*

Optional off-board "event" actors: **the Attorney General**, who can set conditions on any restructuring (Delaware and California, 2025); **the Pentagon**, which can blacklist the company (2026); and **the staff letter**, where 745 of 770 employees threatened to quit (2023).
