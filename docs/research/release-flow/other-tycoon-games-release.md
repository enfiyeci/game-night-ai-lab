# Comparable Tycoon/Management Games — How They Stage a Product Release

Scope note: this covers the five assigned games (Game Dev Story, Mad Games Tycoon 2, Software Inc.,
Startup Company, Big Pharma). Game Dev Tycoon itself is out of scope — a separate researcher is
covering it. "Kairosoft's other titles" and film-studio sims were not reached given the time
budget (see Gaps/Coverage below); this pass stayed within the five named games.

## Game Dev Story (Kairosoft)

### Takeaway
Game Dev Story's release is a short, discrete ceremony: name the game right after it finishes
development, four critics each hand out a score out of 10 (40 total), a sales-chart placement is
announced, and the game then sells for a few in-game months before it's pulled from the market.
A high combined critic score (32+/40) is the gate that gates Hall of Fame entry, sequels, and
entry into the annual awards show; a 36+/40 is needed to have a shot at Game of the Year.

### Cited Findings
- Naming step and reviews happen after release, in this order: "After a game is released, you
  will have the option to name it, it will be reviewed by critics, and then copies will start to
  be sold." — [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- "The critics can give you 10 points each, for a total of 40." Four critics review every
  released game. — [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- "If the critics find a bug, your score will drop." Debugging can be skipped to ship earlier, at
  the risk of critics finding the remaining bugs and marking the score down. — [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- Sales-chart placement is revealed right after release: "You will be given an announcement if
  your game will make the sales charts, and how close to the #1 spot it will be depending on
  initial sales." — [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- How long a game stays "live": "The sales will then go into your funds for the next few months
  before it wanes and the game is finally taken off the market." A rival's same-genre release
  timed close to yours will cut into your sales. — [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- A minimum total critic score of 32/40 is needed for a game to enter the Hall of Fame, and only
  Hall-of-Fame games are eligible for a sequel. — [Game Dev Story, Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Dev_Story); [Creating games (Game Dev Story), Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_games_(Game_Dev_Story))
- Post-release follow-up: an annual "Global Game Awards" show (every December) and an annual
  "Gamedex" trade-show event (every July, a parody of E3) exist as recurring beats, separate from
  the release-day critic scoring. A studio only enters the Global Game Awards once it has a
  32+/40 game; after that, all its games compete every year regardless of score. Game of the Year
  requires 36+/40 total and, more specifically, either 160+ in each of the four score categories
  (Fun, Creativity, Graphics, Sound) or a 640+ combined total with a 155 floor in each category —
  "Even a review of 40 does not ensure winning this award." — [Game Dev Story, Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Dev_Story)
- Post-release marketing exists but is explicitly gated to after launch: in-game dialogue reads
  "There's no point in advertising until you've released a game," and "post-game launch
  advertising will increase sales." Advertising options range from $30K magazine ads to $9.9M
  "Lunar Writing," with diminishing returns from repeating the same ad type. — [Game Dev Story, Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Dev_Story)
- Sequels start with a stat head start (extra Fun/Originality/Graphics/Sound) but must be
  started while the original is still one of the latest 32 games made, can change platform but
  not genre/type, and a franchise can only be sequeled again if the new entry itself clears the
  32/40 bar — one failure ends the franchise's sequel chain. — [Game Dev Story, Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Dev_Story)
- Positive reviews trigger a secondary feedback event ("fan letters") that raises popularity with
  a specific age/gender demographic; a bad score triggers the opposite. This is the game's
  mechanism for "what the release teaches you going into the next product." — [Game Dev Story, Kairosoft Wiki](https://kairosoft.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Dev_Story)

### Inferences
- Because naming happens only after the game is finished (not at project start, and not tied to
  a pricing decision — Game Dev Story does not appear to expose a price lever at all in the
  sources read), the "reveal" beat is almost entirely about the critic score and chart position,
  not about a pricing or marketing decision the player makes at that moment.
- The four-critic panel giving independent 0–10 scores that sum to a 40-point total, shown as a
  single ceremonial reveal, is the most distinctive and frequently-cited mechanic of this game
  relative to the others researched — none of the other four games use a small fixed panel of
  named critics.

### Gaps
- No source found describing exactly how the critic reveal is *animated/paced* on-screen (e.g.,
  whether scores appear one at a time with a pause, or all at once) — the wikis describe the
  scoring mechanic and its thresholds but not the presentation choreography.
- No source found for a numeric sales curve (units/week) — "the sales will then go into your
  funds for the next few months before it wanes" is qualitative only; no source gives exact
  duration in weeks/months or a decay formula.
- Pricing was not found as a player-facing lever in any source read; it may not exist as a
  distinct release-time decision in this game (unlike the other four games, which all expose
  price setting).

## Mad Games Tycoon 2

### Takeaway
Mad Games Tycoon 2 names the game at project creation (not at release), then at release the
player chooses self-distribution vs. a publisher, a price, a release-date delay (to "build up"
pre-orders), and a marketing spend — after which a single weighted review score (0–100%, several
dozen additive/multiplicative modifiers) is calculated and revealed, and post-launch the game
continues to sell copies weekly until it falls off shelves; sequels, ports, remasters and add-ons
are the systems for reusing what a release taught you.

### Cited Findings
- Naming happens up front, not at release: "When creating a game in Mad Games Tycoon 2, you can
  enter a name for the game, and it's also possible to randomly choose one from a list of
  possible names." ⚠️ This claim is drawn from a WebSearch synthesized summary of Steam Community
  discussion threads (titles: "it would be nice to be able to change the names of the games of
  the subsidiaries we buy," "Companies names"); the individual discussion threads were not opened
  directly, only their search-result synthesis was read. — [Mad Games Tycoon 2 discussions, Steam Community (search synthesis)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1342330/discussions/0/3363650131516263324/)
- The review score is built from many layered modifiers, calculated in this order: base points
  from Gameplay/Graphics/Sound/Technical discipline points against a rising difficulty curve;
  a platform-experience penalty (up to −2% per discipline if the studio lacks experience on the
  chosen platform); a −1% per-discipline penalty per missing specialized-room feature (QA,
  Graphic Studio, Sound Studio, MoCap Studio) after certain in-game years (1979/1982/1985/Nov
  1993); an "outdated engine feature" penalty (−2%/−2%/−1%/−1% per outdate point for
  Graphics/Sound/AI/Physics respectively); a game-size-vs-year debuff (e.g. a "B"-size game loses
  up to −5% by 2020); licensing bonuses/penalties (±3–5× the license's quality score depending on
  genre match); genre/theme/audience-fit penalties (up to −4% Gameplay / −3% Final if genre
  doesn't suit the target audience, plus per-missing-XP-point penalties); design-focus and
  design-direction slider-delta penalties (weighted by difficulty, e.g. Medium = ×0.5 on
  Gameplay, ×0.4 on Final); a "Financing" (Basic Costs slider, 50–100%) penalty if the game is
  under-budgeted; a per-bug penalty (Gameplay −0.2%, others −0.1% each); a small RNG dice-roll
  that can push a 98% score up to 99% or 100%; and finally review bonuses (+3% flat for a
  "Pre-Release For Press" marketing action if the score is already ≥50%, −3% if not; +3% flat if
  the main genre matches the studio's specialization; and a difficulty-based flat bonus from +4%
  on Very Easy down to +0% on Legendary). Scores are rounded to the nearest integer (bankers'
  rounding on .5) and clamped to 1–100%. — [Game Review, Mad Games Tycoon 2 Wiki](https://mad-games-tycoon-2.fandom.com/wiki/Game_Review)
- Release-date and marketing choices are explicit player decisions: "you can choose the right
  price, decide how many copies you think you can sell and start marketing campaigns to increase
  your chances of success." — [Mad Games Tycoon 2 on Steam, via WebSearch summary](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1342330/Mad_Games_Tycoon_2/)
- There is a release-delay option separate from the price/copies decision: after setting price,
  the player can choose to delay the actual on-sale date by a number of weeks (to accumulate
  pre-orders) or select "next week" for an immediate launch; a mis-set 20-week delay was
  diagnosed on the forum as the reason a game "isn't selling" post-launch (games generate no
  revenue until they've accrued orders, which starts the week they actually go on sale). — [How to self publish?, Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1342330/discussions/0/591760218823618201/)
- Self-publishing vs. publisher is an irreversible choice made at release ("You need to choose
  'self distribution' when releasing a game... After the game's released there is no way to
  change how it's published"). Publisher quality is a 1–5 star rating; "a 5-star publisher is
  always better than a 4-star publisher with very, very specific and unlikely exceptions." At the
  "optimal" self-publish price range ($30–39 in the example given), a self-published standard
  edition can net about $33.50 profit per copy sold; self-publishing requires building physical
  production-line capacity (machines) to keep up with demand. Self-producing is only recommended
  once the studio reaches "Prestigious game studio" (3 stars) or higher. — [How to self publish?, Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1342330/discussions/0/591760218823618201/)
- Studio prestige ("Studio Level," 11 half-star tiers from "Unknown garage company" to "Legendary
  game company") is earned partly from game releases: points awarded scale with both game type
  (new game/sequel/spinoff = base 1 point; remaster 0.5; port/publishing-offer 0.2; addon 0.1;
  budget/bundle/GOTY 0.05) and review-score tier (weight rises from ×1 at a 0–19% review up to
  ×50 at a 100% review), and awards (Best Sound/Graphics = 40 pts, Best Game = 75, Best
  Studio/Publisher = 100) and completed contract work (1 pt each) also contribute. — [Studio Level, Mad Games Tycoon 2 Wiki](https://mad-games-tycoon-2.fandom.com/wiki/Studio_Level)
- A platform's active-user count acts as a hard sales cap: "If a game is being published for a
  platform that has 10k active users, you won't sell much more than 10k copies, no matter how
  many points you fulfill." ⚠️ Sourced from a WebSearch synthesis of several Steam discussion
  threads (including "Ridiculously low game sales" and "Random platform popularity Issue"); the
  individual threads were not opened and read directly. — [Ridiculously low game sales, Steam Community discussion (search synthesis)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1342330/discussions/0/4632611078379972082/)
- A published review of the game (from 2026, covering the console port) describes the
  release/feedback loop qualitatively: "Seeing review scores climb into the 90s, sales figures
  soar, and fans clamour for sequels is immensely rewarding... The addition of features like
  post-launch support, patches, and live service elements adds further depth. You're not just
  releasing games — you're maintaining them, adapting to player feedback, and navigating an
  ever-changing market." The same review flags the late-game loop as a risk: "once you've
  mastered the systems... the gameplay loop can start to feel routine." — [Mad Games Tycoon 2 Review, Game Critix](https://gamecritix.co.uk/mad-games-tycoon-2-review/)

### Inferences
- Compared with Game Dev Story's small fixed 4-critic panel, MGT2 replaces the "reveal" with a
  single opaque percentage score assembled from dozens of stacked modifiers — the ceremony is
  less about a critic panel and more about a release-prep checklist (specialized rooms, engine
  currency, licensing, genre/theme/audience fit, budget) the player must get right *before*
  release, with the score itself feeling more like a report card than a live event.
  The 8 "TL;DR" bullets the wiki itself lists (full platform experience, newest engine feature,
  discipline points, sub-genre/sub-theme, studio-room features, pre-release press marketing,
  matching genre/theme/audience, correct focus/direction sliders, squashed bugs, adequate
  financing) function as the game's version of a release checklist.
- The release-date delay mechanic (accumulate orders before going on sale) is the game's stand-in
  for a "pre-order hype window," distinct from Software Inc.'s explicit press-release marketing
  window (see below) but serving a similar purpose.

### Gaps
- No source found for what the sales/units-sold chart actually looks like on screen (bar chart,
  weekly ticker, competitor comparison, etc.) or how long, in weeks, a released game continues to
  generate sales before falling off.
- No primary/first-hand source was read (beyond WebSearch's own synthesis) for the exact naming
  step or the platform-active-users-as-sales-cap claim; both are flagged with ⚠️ above and should
  be treated as lower-confidence pending direct verification.
- Could not confirm whether there is an on-screen "critic magazine" or press-outlet visual reveal
  analogous to Game Dev Story's four critics, versus the review score simply appearing as a
  number/percentage in a report screen.

## Software Inc.

### Takeaway
Software Inc. stages release as a multi-month marketing campaign that starts with announcing a
release date (which opens a difficulty-dependent "marketing boost window"), runs through
scripted press-release and "press build" beats during design/development/beta, and ends with a
release-day sales spike; price is set in the design document before development starts and can
only be changed within the promised price's expectations, under penalty of visibly angry
customers, and a young product line ("IP") has zero market recognition until its first release
proves itself with good reviews.

### Cited Findings
- Marketing window length is tied to game difficulty and starts on announcement: Easy = 2 years
  8 months, Medium = 2 years, Hard = 2 years, Impossible = 1 year 4 months. ⚠️ This guide's page
  text was cut off by the fetch tool at 12,000 characters; the cited passages above were within
  the portion read, but later sections of the guide (a further "Post marketing and Deals"
  discussion) were not read to the end. — [Optimal Marketing Guide, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2926392699)
- "Marketing recognition" (MR) is a per-IP value distinct from raw fan count; "every new IP
  starts with Marketing recognition of 0 (zero) value. If the first release have good reviews, it
  will start acquiring recognition" — building good MR (4+ of 6 hearts) is described as slow, and
  a large existing fanbase speeds up MR growth for a brand-new IP release under the same company
  ("any new Apple crap is going to be flying off the shelves on release" as the guide's own
  analogy). — [Optimal Marketing Guide, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2926392699)
- The prescribed pre-release marketing beats, in order: (1) announce the release date (0
  followers gained by itself); (2) issue a press release during the Design phase (1,000+
  followers); (3) issue a second press release during Development (10,000+ followers); (4) do a
  "Press build" (tens of thousands of followers) once the product reaches Beta; (5) release.
  Each press release's boost fades over roughly 3–4 months, and repeat press releases give
  diminishing returns — "less marketing boost" each time, to the point that a third release
  during Alpha/Beta can cause net follower decline. — [Optimal Marketing Guide, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2926392699)
- There is no penalty for releasing earlier than the announced date, only for very large
  slippage against the marketing window; post-marketing (after release) is explicitly called out
  as low-value in the current build: "the majority of the sales will be done in first month after
  the release... there is NO point in doing post-marketing tasks for any of these products." — [Optimal Marketing Guide, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2926392699)
- Expansions/add-ons/new console peripherals ("Joysticks") follow a compressed version of the
  same beat sequence (announce → press release at Alpha → press build at Beta → release) and are
  capped near current active-user count for how many copies are worth printing, though the guide
  author found a console peripheral could spike to 3.1 million units sold in a month versus a
  console's 1.3 million active users at the time, i.e., peripheral demand did not track 1:1 with
  the base console's live user count. — [Optimal Marketing Guide, Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2926392699)
- Price is committed in the product's design document before development, and changing it later
  is punished: in a controlled test, a product designed with a "Suggested Price" of $75 could not
  be sold above roughly $80 without sales collapsing toward zero, because "I 'promised'
  consumers that the price would be $75 in the design document... when I raised the price to
  something above $75, consumers understandably got angry and didn't buy." ⚠️ This guide's page
  text was likewise cut off by the fetch tool (at 8,000 characters) partway through the
  "Conclusion" section; the price-elasticity data table and the mid-analysis were read in full,
  but the final conclusion/recommendation paragraphs were not reached. — [Researching the price elasticity of software..., Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3256150569)
- Concrete price/sales data point from the same controlled experiment (identical "Visionary and
  outstanding" quality Computer OS, released the same day in four parallel saves, $10M spent on
  5M copies for every release): at $0, release-day sales were 163,627 units with ~0.7 "hearts" of
  market recognition, reaching 3.85M total units and a $13.7M *loss* after one year; at $1, sales
  fell to 37,034 units and ended $12.2M in the red; at $75 (the design-document "suggested"
  price), 53,813 units sold on release and the product was $13.9M in profit after one year; at
  $150 (roughly double the suggested price), 50,289 units sold on release and profit after one
  year was $45.7M — i.e., raising price well above the "suggested" figure produced *more* profit
  despite marginally fewer unit sales, but going to $0/free crashed profitability entirely despite
  by far the highest unit-sales volume, and reviews call out an underpriced product as
  "suspiciously cheap." — [Researching the price elasticity of software..., Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3256150569)
- Post-release support and bug-fixing is staffed like any other function: employees need
  "specialisation stars in support" to actually log/report bugs (otherwise they only reduce the
  ticket queue without surfacing bugs); 2-star support employees work twice as fast, and 3-star
  support employees can fix bugs themselves. General programming skill also speeds up both ticket
  resolution and bug-squashing. ⚠️ This claim is drawn from a WebSearch synthesized summary of a
  Steam Community discussion ("Support Team"); the discussion thread itself was not opened and
  read directly. — [Support Team, Steam Community discussion (search synthesis)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/362620/discussions/0/1608274347715715141/)
- A product's market share/score is computed from (quality/price) × complexity (unless the
  product is open source), then scaled by the company's marketing recognition and marketing
  awareness relative to current active users; changing price between a product and its sequel can
  itself hurt popularity. ⚠️ Also drawn from a WebSearch synthesis rather than a direct read of
  the underlying Steam guide/discussion. — [Software Inc. discussions/guides (search synthesis)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/362620/discussions/0/451850213952593244/)

### Inferences
- Software Inc. is the only one of the five games in which the *price is locked in as a promise*
  before the product exists, and where breaking that promise (raising price post-announcement) is
  modeled as a direct, sharp consumer-trust penalty — this is a genuinely distinctive mechanic
  relative to the other four games, none of which model price as a pre-committed "promise" to
  the market.
  The corollary — that going free (price $0) explodes unit sales but is separately, and severely,
  penalized in profit — suggests the game models "free" as its own regime rather than the low end
  of a continuous price curve, matching the price-elasticity guide's own surprise finding.
- The release sequence has no discrete "review reveal" moment analogous to Game Dev Story's four
  critics; instead, quality is expressed continuously through "market recognition hearts" that
  build up over the marketing window and after release, with the real payoff/punishment visible
  in the ongoing sales-per-week and profit figures rather than a single review score at launch.

### Gaps
- No source found for what the release-day/post-release UI actually displays (e.g., is there a
  visual sales chart, a competitor comparison, or a written review blurb?) — the guides describe
  the mechanics (followers, hearts, sales numbers) but not the screen presentation.
- Both Steam guides used were read only up to a fetch-tool character cutoff (12,000 and 8,000
  characters respectively); their concluding sections were not reached, so any claims specifically
  from those unread tail sections are absent here rather than potentially misrepresented.
- No numeric detail found on how long a Software Inc. product typically stays profitable/"live"
  before being end-of-lifed, beyond "the majority of the sales will be done in first month."

## Startup Company

### Takeaway
Startup Company has no discrete "release day" for the company's core product at all — the
player's single website is live from the very start of the game and is grown incrementally by
shipping individual features, each of which nudges registered-user count and satisfaction rather
than triggering a review/score event; the closest things to release beats are per-feature
additions (tracked via milestones) and a public-reaction/stock-tracking in-game social network
called "Jitter."

### Cited Findings
- The company's website exists and is "released" (i.e., goes live to the public) essentially from
  turn one of a new game — early milestones are about staffing and researching a "Landing Page,"
  not about a launch event: milestone 1 is "Rent an office building," milestone 4 is "Research...
  Landing Page," milestone 5 is "Increase potential users to 2,000," and milestone 6 is "Get 2,000
  registered users." — [Milestones, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Milestones)
- Feature additions are themselves milestoned and quantified, not staged as one-off launch
  events: milestone 7 is "Add a total of 2 features," milestone 8 is "Reach at least 75%
  satisfaction," and milestone 15 is "Reach a feature max level of at least 20." — [Milestones, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Milestones)
- Growth and competitive standing are tracked as long-run cumulative milestones rather than
  per-release results: "Get 1,000,000 registered users" (milestone 19), "Get 10,000,000
  registered users" (milestone 22), "Become Top #3" (milestone 24), "Become Top #1" (milestone
  25). Support-ticket handling is also milestoned directly: "Resolve 2 support tickets" (milestone
  20). — [Milestones, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Milestones)
- There is an in-game social network, "Jitter," used to "gauge public reaction to new features and
  keep track of stocks," i.e., the feedback-on-release mechanic is folded into a
  Twitter-style feed rather than a review score or a critic panel. — [Getting Started, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Getting_Started)
- The wiki has no populated page for "Marketing," "Features," "Release," "Reviews," or "Users" —
  a direct navigation to the wiki's "Marketing" page returns "There is currently no text in this
  page," and the full page index (31 articles total) lists no dedicated page on any of those
  topics. — [Marketing, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Marketing); [Special:AllPages, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Special:AllPages)
- User reviews are "Very Positive," with 80% of 2,897 Steam user reviews positive as of the
  source's snapshot. ⚠️ This figure is from a WebSearch synthesis of the Steam store page and
  RAWG, not a page opened and read directly by this researcher. — [Startup Company on Steam (search synthesis)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/606800/Startup_Company/)
- Reported player complaints (from Steam review/discussion synthesis): the game "becomes tedious
  and hard to manage" after about 10 hours, especially once many employees must each be
  individually assigned a manager and desk; the "production plan" feature (which governs
  component/feature output) was reported as buggy, with employees getting stuck rather than
  switching to the assigned plan's rules; and the developer publicly noted their Steam "Recent"
  review score once dropped from about 85% to 62% within a month without being able to pin down a
  specific cause. Development of the game has since ended. ⚠️ These points come from a WebSearch
  synthesis of several separate Steam discussion threads and news posts; none of the individual
  threads were opened and read directly by this researcher. — [Startup Company discussions/news (search synthesis)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/606800/discussions/0/2913220877917005713/)
- The "Production plan" system — which is the closest analogue to a release-pipeline tool in this
  game — lets a plan assign minimum/relative output levels for components across one or more
  managers, either by employee type or by cross-discipline teams "that build components needed
  for specific features," with an option to keep producing after the goal is met or to skip a
  component if a prerequisite ingredient is missing. — [Production plan, Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Production_plan)

### Inferences
- Startup Company is a clear outlier among the five games: it has no naming step (the *company*
  is named once, at game start, and there is no evidence of a per-product/per-feature naming
  step), no pricing decision comparable to the other four games (the core product — the website —
  appears to be free to end users, monetized indirectly via user growth/ads/deals rather than a
  unit price), and no ceremonial review/score reveal. Feedback is continuous (user counts,
  satisfaction %, Jitter reactions) rather than event-based.
- Because there's no single "release," the design lesson this game teaches about "informing the
  next product" is structural: it rewards continuous iteration/feature-shipping cadence over a
  single make-or-break launch, which is the opposite design philosophy from Game Dev Story, Mad
  Games Tycoon 2, and Big Pharma, all of which stage a launch as a discrete event with a
  score/verdict attached.

### Gaps
- Because the wiki (31 articles) does not cover "Reviews," "Marketing," or "Features" as
  standalone topics, and this researcher did not locate a detailed devlog, Reddit deep-dive, or
  Let's Play writeup describing the feature-shipping loop moment-to-moment (what exactly appears
  on screen when a feature goes live, how Jitter reactions are presented, whether there is a
  discrete "satisfaction score" popup), those specifics are not sourced here. This is a genuine
  gap, not an inference — flagging rather than guessing at the presentation.
- No source found on whether/how a "product" separate from the core website (e.g., a mobile app
  spin-off, if the game has one) gets its own release treatment — the sources read describe only
  the single-website company structure.

## Big Pharma

### Takeaway
Big Pharma is unusual among the five: naming happens only *after* the player has actually
produced a working batch of the drug on the factory floor (not before development, not at a
pre-announced release date), and in the base game there is no player-set price at all — price is
recomputed automatically from supply, demand, and market saturation every day; a later expansion
("Marketing and Malpractice") added a manual price slider (set as a % of the market "norm") plus
Executives who can market drugs and manipulate competitors' markets, and separately added a
patent system letting a player lock out competitors from a specific formula/product-type
combination.

### Cited Findings
- Naming happens post-production, not pre-release: "Once the player has produced a complete pill
  from specific materials for the first time and delivered it to an output port, they are given
  the opportunity to name the product. This product is then sold on the game's virtual market."
  — [Big Pharma (video game), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Pharma_(video_game))
- The product is then rated with a letter grade ("based on the effectiveness and lack of side
  effects as well as current market demand") and separately given a "Cure Rating" once on the
  market, ranging S+ down to F, based on how well the drug is performing (curing frequency raises
  the rating, side effects lower it); the average rating (C) carries no price modifier, but an
  "E" rating cuts total price by −20% while an "A" rating adds +20%. — [Big Pharma (video game), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Pharma_(video_game)); [Marketing & Sales.., Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/344850/discussions/0/520518688951336366/)
- Base-game pricing is fully automatic, not player-set: "in the original game you can't manually
  set the price of your drugs... the game recomputes supply and demand every day without the DLC
  and adjusts prices, but with the DLC you manually set the % from the norm of what you want to
  sell at." ⚠️ This is drawn from a WebSearch synthesis citing the game's designer, not a page
  this researcher opened directly. — [Big Pharma pricing discussion (search synthesis, designer statement)](https://steamcommunity.com/app/344850/discussions/0/1640913421081101905/)
- Market saturation is the central post-release competitive pressure: saturation "decreases net
  demand when above 100%," and the higher a cure's "sensitivity" value, the more can be supplied
  before hitting 100% saturation and the less price falls once saturated. Rival companies release
  their own competing drugs on the same open market, and if the market is already saturated with,
  say, a cold remedy, a better cold remedy from the player may still fail to succeed commercially.
  Saturation can be pushed to the point of total price collapse — one player reported a single
  production line making 30 liver-disease pills/month pushed saturation past 500% and the price
  to £0. — [Big Pharma (video game), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Pharma_(video_game)); [Marketing & Sales.., Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/344850/discussions/0/520518688951336366/)
- Even at severe over-saturation, demand does not vanish, only price: "They will continue to buy,
  but you may lose money due to 1) saturation and 2) low quality product." A player can therefore
  push out large volumes of an inferior product and still find buyers, just at collapsed margins.
  — [Marketing & Sales.., Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/344850/discussions/0/520518688951336366/)
- A patent covers "a specific set of active effects and a finished product type e.g. Pill, Cream
  etc." (the order of active effects doesn't matter), takes 4 months and 2 scientists to research
  at the "Master" tier, and requires the "National Headquarters" building/prerequisite already
  built; patents "allow you to protect your drug formulas and stop your competitors from selling
  the same products as you." Patent research has upgrade tiers that raise the maximum cure level
  a patent can cover (0→2→3→4→6) and one upgrade that lowers future patent cost to 80% of normal.
  — [Patents, Official Big Pharma Wiki](https://bigpharma.fandom.com/wiki/Patents)
- The "Marketing And Malpractice" expansion (released April 26, 2016) added Executives, who "can
  be used to market the drugs produced by the player's production lines, as well as to try to
  manipulate and corner the market" — i.e., active post-release market manipulation as a distinct
  late-game system layered on top of the base saturation/pricing mechanics. — [Big Pharma (video game), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Pharma_(video_game))
- The base game shipped August 27, 2015 on Windows/OS X/Linux, with console ports (Xbox One,
  Switch, PS4) following in December 2019; it holds a 72% Metacritic score and an 8/10 from IGN
  Spain. — [Big Pharma (video game), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Pharma_(video_game))
- A press profile of the game (STAT News) opens with an anecdote illustrating the tension between
  drug quality and profitability as the game's core theme: a player who built "the perfect drug"
  with no side effects went into "ruinous debt," then switched to "cheap drugs with bad side
  effects" and found "the money rolled in" — framed by the article as the game deliberately
  making it "easy to be villainous." ⚠️ Only the article's opening ~150 words were reachable before
  the page cut to unrelated "Recommended Stories" content (apparent paywall/content gate); the
  rest of the article was not read. — [Big Pharma, the game, makes it 'easy to be villainous', STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2015/11/11/big-pharma/)

### Inferences
- Big Pharma's post-development naming step (versus Game Dev Story's post-release naming, versus
  Mad Games Tycoon 2's pre-development naming) suggests the "when do you name it" beat is one of
  the more genre-variable choices across these games — there's no single convention.
  The base game's fully automatic pricing (no player price lever at all until the expansion) is
  the most distinctive design choice found across all five games — every other title studied gives
  the player a direct or semi-direct price control at release.
- The saturation mechanic (continuing to sell at collapsing price/margin rather than simply not
  selling) mirrors real-world commodity oversupply dynamics more literally than the other four
  games' review-driven demand curves, and is probably the single most "economically educational"
  mechanic found in this research set.

## Recurring patterns across the genre

- **A price-or-value decision precedes launch in most of these games, but *when* it's locked in
  varies**: Software Inc. locks price in at the design stage (before development) and punishes
  breaking that promise; Mad Games Tycoon 2 sets price at the release step itself; Big Pharma's
  base game removes the decision entirely (fully automatic); Startup Company and Game Dev Story
  show no direct unit-price lever in the sources read. None of the five studied games set price
  at the same *moment* as the ceremonial reveal/reviews.
- **A scored, ceremonial "verdict" moment is common but not universal.** Game Dev Story (four
  critics, 40-point total) and, less visually, Mad Games Tycoon 2 (single stacked percentage) and
  Big Pharma (letter grade + Cure Rating) all attach a score to the release moment. Startup
  Company is the clear exception — it has no discrete release event or score at all, only
  continuous metrics.
- **Naming timing is inconsistent across the genre and is not simply "always at the start."**
  Game Dev Story names after release; Big Pharma names after first successful production but
  before market listing; Mad Games Tycoon 2 names at project creation. This is worth deciding
  deliberately for Game Night AI Lab rather than assuming a genre default.
  ⚠️ The Mad Games Tycoon 2 naming-timing claim rests on a WebSearch synthesis rather than a
  directly-read source (see Gaps above); treat it as lower-confidence pending direct verification.
- **Market saturation / diminishing returns from flooding the same niche recurs in three of the
  five games.** Big Pharma models it explicitly and numerically (net-demand and price collapse
  above 100% saturation); Software Inc. models a related idea through per-IP "marketing
  recognition" that takes time to build and can be spent down by over-marketing; Game Dev Story
  models a softer version (a same-genre rival's simultaneous release directly cuts your sales).
- **Post-release "maintenance" — patches, bug fixes, support tickets, live-service — is treated
  as a distinct system, separate from the launch-day reveal, in every game that has one.** Game
  Dev Story's debugging happens *before* the critic review (skipping it risks the score); Software
  Inc. and Mad Games Tycoon 2 both model post-release bug/support work as an ongoing staffed
  function; Startup Company mile­stones explicitly include "resolve 2 support tickets" as a
  late-game goal.
- **Marketing spend before release is consistently modeled as a build-up window with diminishing
  returns from repetition**, not a single lever pulled at launch: Software Inc.'s scripted
  press-release/press-build sequence and its difficulty-scaled "marketing boost window," and Mad
  Games Tycoon 2's pre-release-for-press bonus/penalty and release-date delay (to build up
  orders) are the clearest examples; Game Dev Story explicitly disables advertising until after a
  first release exists, which is the opposite framing (pre-release hype isn't modeled at all,
  only post-release ad spend).
- **A first product/IP typically starts from zero brand recognition and must earn it through
  quality**, which is explicit in Software Inc. ("every new IP starts with Marketing recognition
  of 0") and functionally true in Game Dev Story (a low first score blocks Hall of Fame / sequel
  eligibility) and Mad Games Tycoon 2 (Studio Level gates self-publishing effectiveness). This is
  the clearest "what the release teaches you for next time" pattern across the set: your first
  release's reception directly gates or accelerates your next one, rather than each release being
  independent.
