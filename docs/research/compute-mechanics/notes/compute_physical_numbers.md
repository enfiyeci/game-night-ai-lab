# The Physical and Economic Numbers of AI Compute, 2020–2026 (for game calibration)

Scope note: this file is a companion to the existing `docs/research/ai-lab-mechanics/notes/compute_race.md`
(read in full before starting this pass, not repeated here), which covers supply-chain actors,
deal structures, mega-projects, power/political constraints, geopolitics, and market dynamics.
This file instead covers the raw physical/economic numbers needed to calibrate a game "compute"
unit (prototype: ~1,000 top GPUs, ~$2/GPU-hour rental) across five eras: 2022–23 chat assistants,
2024 scale-up, 2025 reasoning/agents, 2026–27 gigawatt race, 2028+ self-improvement.

## Frontier training runs by year: FLOP, GPU count, cluster size, run length, cost, and growth rate

### Takeaway
Training compute for frontier models has grown at a remarkably stable ~4–5x per year since 2010,
accelerating slightly to ~5x/year for frontier language models specifically since 2020 (compute
doubling roughly every 5.2 months); amortized training cost has grown more slowly, at ~2.4–2.6x
per year, and Epoch AI projects the largest training runs will cross $1 billion by 2027 — a number
your game's "cost per era" curve should roughly track (each era should cost noticeably more per
training run than the last, not just require more raw compute units).

### Cited Findings
- Training compute of notable AI models grew ~4.1x/year (90% CI: 3.7x–4.6x) from 2010 to May 2024; frontier models specifically grew ~5.3x/year over the same span — [Epoch AI, "Training compute of frontier AI models grows by 4-5x per year"](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year)
- Frontier language models specifically: training compute grew ~5x/year since mid-2020, i.e. compute has been doubling roughly every 5.2 months — [Epoch AI, same publication](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year)
- Sub-period breakdown from the same Epoch analysis: language models post-Transformer (mid-2017–2024) grew compute at ~9.5x/year; leading labs (OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta) individually ranged 4.9x–7.1x/year (2012–2024); a growth slowdown is visible around 2016–2019, after which frontier compute growth stabilized near 4x/year — [Epoch AI, same publication](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year)
- Named-model FLOP figures: GPT-3 (May 2020) ≈ 3e23 FLOP; GPT-4 (March 2023) ≈ 2e25 FLOP; Gemini Ultra (December 2023) ≈ 5e25 FLOP — [Epoch AI, same publication](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year)
- 2026 frontier training runs are estimated in the 1e26–1e27 FLOP range — [search-result synthesis of Epoch-adjacent commentary](https://deluair.com/consultancy/insights/frontier-ai-training-cost-2026) ⚠️ this is a secondary aggregator's characterization, not directly verified against Epoch AI's own frontier-model database in this pass
- Training cost growth: amortized hardware-plus-energy cost of the final training run has grown ~2.4x/year since 2016 (95% CI 2.0x–3.1x); using a cloud-rental-cost methodology instead, the growth rate is ~2.6x/year, and cloud-based cost estimates run roughly 2x higher than amortized-hardware estimates for the same model — [Epoch AI, "How much does it cost to train frontier AI models?"](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models)
- Cost composition across GPT-3, OPT-175B, GPT-4, and Gemini Ultra: hardware is 47–67% of total development cost, R&D staff compensation is 29–49%, and energy is only 2–6% — [Epoch AI, same publication](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models)
- Named training-run costs: GPT-4 ≈ $80 million (hardware+energy, ~2e25 FLOP); Claude 3.5 Sonnet ≈ 3e25 FLOP; Gemini 1.5 Ultra ≈ 1e26 FLOP; Meta spent an estimated $170 million on Llama 3.1 405B; xAI's Grok-2 cost an estimated $107 million — [search-result synthesis](https://epoch.ai/data/frontier_ai_models.csv) ⚠️ the specific dollar figures for GPT-4/Llama-3.1-405B/Grok-2 came back as a search-engine synthesis of Epoch AI's underlying CSV data rather than a direct read of the CSV or a cited Epoch publication page; the FLOP/cost pairing should be treated as approximate pending direct verification against `epoch.ai/data/frontier_ai_models.csv`
- Epoch AI projects that if the ~2.4x/year cost-growth trend continues, the largest training runs will cost more than $1 billion by 2027; 2026 frontier runs (GPT-5/Gemini-Ultra class) are estimated at $200–500 million, with late-2027 frontier runs projected at $1–3 billion — [Epoch AI, "How much does it cost to train frontier AI models?"](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models); 2026/2027 dollar figures specifically via [secondary synthesis](https://deluair.com/consultancy/insights/frontier-ai-training-cost-2026) ⚠️ not independently confirmed against Epoch's own site in this pass
- Gemini Ultra's estimated power requirement for training was ~35 megawatts; extrapolating the trend, Epoch's analysis suggests AI supercomputers will require gigawatt-scale power supply by roughly 2029 — [Epoch AI, "How much does it cost to train frontier AI models?"](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models)
- Cluster sizes used for specific named training runs by year: 2016 NASv3 RL (Google Brain) 800 GPUs; 2017 AlphaZero (DeepMind) 5,064 TPUs; 2019 OpenAI Five 1,536 GPUs, GPT-3 175B ~10,000 GPUs; 2020 Meena (Google Brain) 1,024 TPUs; 2021 Gopher 280B (DeepMind) 4,096 TPUs, GLaM (Google) 1,024 TPUs; 2023 Gemini 1.0 Ultra 57,000 TPUs, GPT-4 ~25,000 GPUs; 2024 Llama 3.1-405B (Meta) 16,384 H100 GPUs, Nemotron-4 340B (NVIDIA) 6,144 GPUs — [Epoch AI, "AI training cluster sizes increased by more than 20x since 2016"](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/training-cluster-size)
- The same Epoch AI cluster-size page reports overall cluster-size growth of more than 20x from 2016 to 2024 — [Epoch AI, same page](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/training-cluster-size)
- Llama 3.1 405B's actual pretraining run on the 16,384-H100 cluster ran 54 days and achieved >90% effective training time (goodput) despite hundreds of interruptions (see Reliability section below) — [search-synthesis of Meta's Llama 3 technical report, cross-checked against Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/faulty-nvidia-h100-gpus-and-hbm3-memory-caused-half-of-the-failures-during-llama-3-training-one-failure-every-three-hours-for-metas-16384-gpu-training-cluster) ⚠️ read via a tech-press summary of the Meta paper, not the primary Llama 3 paper itself, which was not directly fetched in this pass

### Inferences
- The compute-growth rate (~4–5x/year) and cost-growth rate (~2.4–2.6x/year) diverge because each successive GPU generation delivers more FLOP per dollar — a game should let later eras buy more raw "compute units" per dollar even as the absolute dollar cost of a frontier run keeps rising, rather than holding $/compute-unit constant across eras.
- The one data point that gives a search-verified figure for Grok 4 ("200,000 TPUs," 2025, from the Epoch cluster-size page fetch) is almost certainly a data-quality artifact — xAI's Colossus cluster runs NVIDIA GPUs, not TPUs — so treat that specific line item as unreliable; the underlying scale (order 100k–200k accelerators for a 2025 flagship run) is independently corroborated by the Colossus GPU-count figures in the next section, but the "TPU" label for a Grok model should not be used in the game's era-5 data.
- Applying the ~4–5x/year compute-growth rate and ~2.4–2.6x/year cost-growth rate to your five eras gives a rough shape: era 1 (2022–23) ≈ 1e24–1e25 FLOP / tens-of-millions of dollars; era 2 (2024) ≈ 1e25 FLOP / ~$100–200M; era 3 (2025) ≈ mid-1e25 to 1e26 FLOP / ~$200–500M; era 4 (2026–27) ≈ 1e26–1e27+ FLOP / $0.5–3B+; era 5 (2028+) plausibly crosses several billion dollars per run if the trend continues, though Epoch explicitly flags this as an extrapolation, not an observed data point past 2027.

### Gaps
- Training-run *length in days* for GPT-3, GPT-4, Gemini, and Grok 3 specifically (as distinct from Llama 3.1 405B's documented 54 days) was not found in this pass; only Llama 3.1 405B has a directly sourced run-length figure.
- The precise FLOP/cost pairing for Llama 3.1 405B, Grok-2, and Grok 3 needs direct verification against `epoch.ai/data/frontier_ai_models.csv` (only reachable in this pass via search-engine synthesis, not a direct fetch of the CSV).
- 2026 named frontier runs (e.g., whatever GPT-5.x/Gemini 3/Claude Opus-class models trained in 2026) were not individually broken out by FLOP/cost/cluster size in this pass — only aggregate 2026 range estimates (1e26–1e27 FLOP, $200–500M) were found, and those trace to secondary aggregation rather than a primary Epoch table.

## Cluster sizes by year and time to build them

### Takeaway
The largest *documented single training clusters* grew from ~800 GPUs (2016) to ~10,000–25,000 GPUs (2019–2023) to 100,000+ GPUs (2024) to 200,000+ GPU gigawatt-scale campuses (2025–2026); build time for a state-of-the-art 100k-GPU cluster compressed from an industry-typical ~4 years to xAI's widely reported 122 days for the first 100,000-GPU phase of Colossus, though that figure describes facility/power stand-up speed for an unusually aggressive, gas-powered, on-site-generation build, not a typical timeline.

### Cited Findings
- xAI's Colossus: the first 100,000-GPU phase (Memphis, TN) was built in 122 days from empty site to operational supercomputer; NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang said projects of this scale typically take about four years — [multiple sources including Introl Blog, HPCwire, and NVIDIA Newsroom](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/spectrum-x-ethernet-networking-xai-colossus); site-to-150MW timeline specifically dated in [ResistanceZero, "From Empty Field to 150 MW in 122 Days"](https://resistancezero.com/article-23.html)
- Colossus doubled from 100,000 to 200,000 GPUs in a further ~92 days — [HPCwire, "Colossus AI Hits 200,000 GPUs as Musk Ramps Up AI Ambitions"](https://www.hpcwire.com/2025/05/13/colossus-ai-hits-200000-gpus-as-musk-ramps-up-ai-ambitions/)
- As of the fuller Colossus figures already captured in the companion research note (`compute_race.md`, SemiAnalysis-sourced): by mid-September 2026 Colossus carried ~1.29 GW of IT load with roughly 670,000 physical accelerators and ~$48.7 billion of modeled capital, with total planned capacity near 2 GW / 555,000 NVIDIA GPUs — [SemiAnalysis, "xAI's Colossus 2: First Gigawatt Datacenter"](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/xais-colossus-2-first-gigawatt-datacenter) (already cited in the companion note; repeated here for direct comparison against the cluster-size-by-year table above)
- GPT-3 (2019/2020 era) trained on roughly 10,000 GPUs; GPT-4 (2023) on roughly 25,000 GPUs; Gemini 1.0 Ultra (2023) on 57,000 TPUs — [Epoch AI, "AI training cluster sizes increased by more than 20x since 2016"](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/training-cluster-size)
- Meta's Llama 3.1 405B (2024) trained on a single 16,384-H100 cluster; separately, Meta built two 24,576-GPU H100 clusters and stated a 2024 year-end target of an H100-equivalent fleet of ~600,000 GPUs (350,000 physical H100s plus other accelerators) — [Meta Engineering, "Building Meta's GenAI Infrastructure"](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/03/12/data-center-engineering/building-metas-genai-infrastructure/); cross-checked via [SDxCentral](https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/meta-reveals-details-of-two-new-24k-gpu-ai-clusters/)
- Oracle announced a "zettascale" AI supercomputer offering, generally available around 2025, scaling up to 131,072 GPUs in a single cluster — more than 3x the GPU count of the DOE's Frontier supercomputer — [Oracle Cloud Infrastructure blog, "Announcing World's Largest, First Zettascale AI Supercomputer in the Cloud"](https://blogs.oracle.com/cloud-infrastructure/worlds-largest-ai-supercomputer-in-the-cloud)

### Inferences
- Build-time compression (4 years → ~4 months for a 100k-GPU/150MW facility) is the single most game-relevant physical fact for a "gigawatt race" era: it shows the era-4 mechanic should let players choose between a slow, cheap, grid-connected build and a fast, expensive, self-generated-power build, mirroring the real Colossus approach of on-site gas turbines instead of waiting on grid interconnection (see the companion note's Power section for the interconnection-queue numbers).
- Cluster size roughly tracks era boundaries reasonably well: ~10k–25k GPUs fits era 1–2 (2022–24), ~16k–100k+ GPUs fits era 2–3 (2024–25 at frontier labs), and 200k+ GPU / gigawatt-scale campuses fit era 4 (2026–27) — this validates using cluster-size tiers (10k / 25k / 100k / 200k+) as the game's era-gating thresholds for its compute-unit cap.

### Gaps
- A single, consistent, primary-sourced year-by-year table of "largest known cluster in the world" (as opposed to largest cluster used for one named training run) was not found; the figures above mix "largest single training run" and "largest built campus," which are related but distinct metrics a game should probably track separately (peak simultaneous training cluster vs. total campus GPU count, which includes inference and multiple concurrent jobs).
- Grok 3's specific training cluster size and build timeline (as distinct from the later Colossus 100k/200k GPU milestones) were not separately isolated in this pass.

## Conversions: GPUs per MW, cost per GW, capex per GPU, power per rack, PUE

### Takeaway
A modern GB200 NVL72 rack draws roughly 120–132 kW for 72 GPUs (~1.7–1.8 kW/GPU), which works out to roughly 550–650 GPUs per megawatt of IT load; a full 1-gigawatt AI campus costs on the order of $38 billion in up-front capex (about $21 billion of that in servers alone, implying a rough $35,000-per-GPU server capex at GB200-class density), with facility PUE for state-of-the-art liquid-cooled AI campuses now down near 1.05–1.15 versus a stagnant global average of ~1.56.

### Cited Findings
- GB200 NVL72 rack power: NVIDIA's nominal spec is 120 kW per 72-GPU rack (~1.2 kW/GPU by that headline spec); real-world deployed racks draw closer to 130–132 kW under full load once you include the rack's non-GPU components (115 kW liquid-cooled + 17 kW air-cooled per HPE's spec sheet), i.e. closer to ~1.7–1.8 kW per GPU including overhead — [Introl Blog, "GB200 NVL72 Deployment"](https://introl.com/blog/gb200-nvl72-deployment-72-gpu-liquid-cooled); [Sunbird DCIM](https://www.sunbirddcim.com/blog/your-data-center-ready-nvidia-gb200-nvl72); HPE spec detail via [buy.hpe.com](https://buy.hpe.com/us/en/Compute/Rack-Scale-System/Nvidia-NVL-System/Nvidia-NVL-System/NVIDIA-GB200-NVL72-by-HPE/p/1014890104)
- GPUs per MW at GB200 NVL72 density: roughly 8–9 racks fit in 1 MW at 120 kW/rack, i.e. ~576–648 GPUs per MW of IT load — [search-synthesis calculation from the above rack-power sources](https://rax.ae/knowledge-center/articles/nvidia-blackwell-gb200-nvl72-data-center-guide.html) ⚠️ this is a derived/calculated figure from vendor rack-power specs, not a single directly-cited "GPUs per MW" statistic from a primary source
- 1-gigawatt AI data-center total cost of ownership, per Epoch AI's cost-breakdown model (built around NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 server systems): up-front capex ≈ $38 billion, annualized total cost ≈ $8.5 billion/year ($8.5 million per MW per year). Capex breakdown: servers $21.2B, facility $11.4B, network infrastructure $4.9B, land $0.17B, utility works $0.16B. Annualized-cost breakdown: servers $5.0B/yr (60%), facility $1.4B/yr (17%), network $1.2B/yr (14%), energy $0.6B/yr (7%), taxes $0.14B/yr, maintenance $0.12B/yr, labor $0.04B/yr, utility works $0.02B/yr, land $0.013B/yr, water $0.006B/yr. Assumptions: 5-year IT-equipment lifespan, 14-year facility lifespan — [Epoch AI, "Total cost of ownership of a one-gigawatt AI data center"](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-datacenter-cost-breakdown)
- Derived capex-per-GPU (calculation, not a directly cited figure): using the Epoch $21.2B servers-capex figure for 1 GW against the ~576,000–648,000 GPUs/GW implied by GB200 NVL72 rack density above, server capex works out to roughly $33,000–$37,000 per GPU — [derived from the two Epoch AI/vendor sources above; not independently confirmed against a primary per-GPU list price]
- Separately, market commentary (not Epoch) puts total AI-optimized campus cost at $45–55 billion per GW when including a fuller buildout, and cites building costs of $25–40 million per MW of IT load in 2026 (up from $8–12 million/MW in 2020); shell-and-core facility alone runs $15–20 million/MW, an all-in facility with GPUs installed runs $30–40 million/MW; a 200 MVA substation serving 50 MW of load costs $12–16 million; liquid-cooling infrastructure costs $4.5–5.2 million/MW versus ~$1.8 million/MW for air cooling — [search-synthesis of ClusterBid/axis-intelligence/irecruit-family sources](https://clusterbid.com/blog/ai-data-center-building-capex-2026-analysis) ⚠️ these figures are aggregator-level (not traced to a primary construction-cost index or filing) and are noticeably higher than Epoch AI's $38B/GW figure — the two source families disagree by roughly 20–45%, which should be treated as a real range rather than resolved to one number
- Global average data-center PUE has stagnated between 1.55 and 1.59 since 2020, with a 2024 fleet average near 1.56; only 13% of facilities achieve PUE below 1.4 — [search-synthesis, cross-referencing Eaton and Infineon Developer Community summaries](https://community.infineon.com/t5/Blogs/What-is-power-usage-effectiveness-PUE-in-data-centers-Overview-limitations-and/ba-p/1203256) ⚠️ traced to aggregator/vendor-blog summaries, not a primary Uptime Institute survey report directly fetched in this pass
- Liquid cooling adoption drives PUE down toward 1.05–1.15 (immersion cooling to ~1.03); Google's 2025 fleet-wide average PUE is 1.09; Microsoft began fleet-wide direct-to-chip liquid cooling deployment across Azure in July 2025 — [Introl Blog, "Achieving PUE 1.09 in AI Data Centers"](https://introl.com/blog/pue-109-google-data-center-efficiency-strategies); Google's own figure via [Google Data Centers, "Power usage effectiveness"](https://datacenters.google/efficiency/)
- Global data-center electricity consumption reached 415 TWh in 2024 and is projected to exceed 945 TWh by 2030 in this source's framing (compare with IEA's separate 485 TWh 2025 figure already cited in the companion note — the two are not the same year, so they are not directly contradictory, but neither was read from a primary IEA PDF in this pass) — [search-synthesis, Wikipedia "Environmental impact of AI" summary of underlying reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI) ⚠️ Wikipedia-mediated secondary summary, not a primary-source read in this pass

### Inferences
- The prototype's $2/GPU-hour rental rate is well below the median H100 spot price found in the Market Dynamics section below ($3.25–3.44/hour as of September 2026) and closer to the cheap end of the marketplace range ($1.49–$1.99/hour, e.g. Vast.ai/RunPod) — if the game wants $2/hour to represent a "typical/blended" rate rather than a bargain-marketplace rate, it may be worth nudging it toward $2.50–3.00/hour for 2025–26-era realism, while keeping a cheaper $1.50–2.00/hour tier available to represent commodity/older-generation hardware.
- The two capex-per-GW source families (Epoch's ~$38B/GW vs. market aggregators' ~$45–55B/GW) bracket a plausible $40–50B/GW figure for a "build your own gigawatt campus" game mechanic; given Epoch is the more rigorous, methodology-transparent primary source, it is the better anchor, with the aggregator range used only to set an upper bound for cost-overrun events.
- PUE is a good candidate for a simple era-based multiplier on power cost: era 1–2 (air-cooled, ~1.5 PUE) vs. era 4+ (liquid-cooled, ~1.1 PUE) represents roughly a 35% swing in facility power overhead for the same IT load — a meaningful, simple mechanic (e.g., "cooling tech upgrade" reduces effective power cost by ~30%).

### Gaps
- A single authoritative, primary-sourced "GPUs per MW" figure for H100 (as opposed to GB200 NVL72) was not directly found in this pass; only the newer Blackwell-generation rack figure was captured with reasonable confidence. An H100 HGX/DGX 8-GPU node draws roughly 10.2 kW per NVIDIA's own DGX H100 datasheet (not independently re-verified in this pass — flagged as recalled rather than freshly sourced), which would imply a lower ~780 GPUs/MW at the node level before cooling/network overhead; treat this H100 figure as an estimate pending direct datasheet confirmation.
- No primary NVIDIA datasheet (H100, GB200, or newer) was directly fetched in this pass; all rack-power and GPU-density figures trace to reseller/integrator blog posts (Introl, Sunbird, HPE's own product page) rather than NVIDIA's own published spec sheets.
- Newer-generation (Rubin-era, 2026+) rack power density and GPUs-per-MW were not found — this is a meaningful gap for calibrating the game's era-4/5 compute-unit-to-power conversion.

## Lead times: GPU delivery, data-center construction, grid interconnection, gas turbines, transformers, nuclear restarts

### Takeaway
Every stage of the AI-compute build pipeline has its own multi-month-to-multi-year lead time as of 2026, and these have moved in different directions: GPU order-to-delivery lead times for H100-class chips have *shortened* dramatically since 2023 (from 36–52 weeks down to 2–3 months) even as Blackwell-class (B200) lead times remain long (12–18 months for volume orders), while grid-adjacent hardware — power transformers, generator step-up transformers, and gas turbines — has moved the opposite direction, with lead times now measured in years and still lengthening.

### Cited Findings
- H100 GPU lead times: in 2023, lead time from order to delivery for H100-based servers ran 36–52 weeks; by 2024, this improved to roughly 2–3 months for standard configurations ordered directly from NVIDIA or tier-one OEMs, with distribution-channel/in-stock inventory available in 2–8 weeks — [TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/97478/nvidia-h100-ai-gpu-lead-times-improve-4-month-wait-is-now-2-3/index.html); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/nvidias-h100-ai-gpu-shortages-ease-as-lead-times-drop-from-up-to-four-months-to-8-12-weeks)
- B200 (Blackwell) lead times as of 2026: new/non-priority buyers face 12–18 month wait times for volume orders because most early supply was allocated to hyperscalers who placed orders in 2024/early-2025; enterprise buyers with existing OEM relationships see 8–16 weeks (down from 12–24 weeks in Q4 2025), but non-priority buyers report 30+ week waits — [search-synthesis of GPUaaS.com and related trade-press sources](https://gpuaas.com/blog/b200-gpu-availability-report-q2-2026) ⚠️ aggregator-level source, not cross-checked against an NVIDIA or OEM primary statement in this pass
- Power transformers (standard): Wood Mackenzie's Q2 2025 survey found average delivery lead time of 128 weeks (~2.5 years); generator step-up transformers (which connect large generation assets like on-site gas turbines to the grid) average 144 weeks (~2.75 years), with some orders extending to 4 years; high-voltage transformers from Tier-1 manufacturers reached lead times of 60+ months (5+ years) in 2026 by one industry-press account — [IndustrialSage, "Power Transformer Lead Times Hit 128 Weeks in 2026"](https://www.industrialsage.com/power-transformer-lead-times-us-grid-shortage/); HV figure via [GridReadiness](https://www.gridreadiness.com/blog/power-transformer-lead-times-ai-data-center-2026.html) ⚠️ both are trade-press/industry-blog sources; the underlying Wood Mackenzie survey itself was not directly fetched
- Power-transformer list prices have risen 77% since 2019; distribution-transformer prices have risen 78–95% over the same period — [same IndustrialSage source](https://www.industrialsage.com/power-transformer-lead-times-us-grid-shortage/)
- Medium-voltage switchgear for data-center specifications is running close to 2–3 years lead time in early 2026, per contractor-side reporting; standard switchboards run ~52 weeks, power-circuit-breaker switchboards run 84+ weeks — [same IndustrialSage source](https://www.industrialsage.com/power-transformer-lead-times-us-grid-shortage/)
- Bloomberg reported that of roughly 12 GW of US data-center capacity slated to come online in 2026, only about one-third was under active construction as of the report, with transformer/switchgear/battery shortages cited as a major reason — [search-synthesis citing Bloomberg, via LinkedIn/industry-blog repost](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/blueprint-data-centers_datacenters-digitalinfrastructure-supplychain-activity-7449532694079889408-g_1s) ⚠️ the original Bloomberg article was not directly fetched; this is a secondhand citation
- GE Vernova gas-turbine backlog: grew from 100 GW to 116 GW between Q1 and Q2 2026, with the company targeting at least 125 GW backlog by year-end 2026 and a total company order backlog of $176 billion; GE Vernova is on track for 20 GW/year of gas-turbine production by Q3 2026, rising to 24 GW/year by 2028 and a targeted 30 GW/year by 2030 — [Turbomachinery Magazine, "GE Vernova's Gas Turbine Backlog Hits 116 GW"](https://www.turbomachinerymag.com/view/ge-vernova-gas-turbine-backlog-hits-116-gw-as-power-orders-more-than-double); figures cross-checked against [GE Vernova's own SEC 8-K filings for Q1/Q2 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001996810/000199681026000147/gevpressrelease2q26.htm) ⚠️ the SEC filing links were surfaced by search but not individually opened/read end-to-end in this pass — the backlog figures are as reported by Turbomachinery Magazine's citation of them, not independently re-verified against the filing text
- The gas-turbine manufacturing market for large frame turbines is effectively a 3-company oligopoly: GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries; average wait times for gas-turbine delivery have "increased by several years" recently; Mitsubishi Heavy Industries announced plans to double its gas-turbine manufacturing capacity over two years in response to backlog; Siemens Energy is expanding blade/vane manufacturing capacity (announced a 61,000 sq ft facility addition in December 2025) — [Utility Dive, "Gas turbine manufacturers expand capacity, but order backlog could prove stubborn"](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/mitsubishi-gas-turbine-manufacturing-capacity-expansion-supply-demand/759371/)
- Nuclear restarts: Palisades Nuclear Plant (Covert, MI; Holtec International) transitioned from decommissioning to operational status for regulatory purposes on 2025-08-25, following NRC approval of its licensing package in July 2025; it is targeting a 2026 return to service, backed by a $1.52 billion federal loan guarantee (six disbursement rounds already approved as of the source date); the restart has slipped past an original October 2025 target amid ongoing issues — [NucNet](https://www.nucnet.org/news/palisades-nuclear-plant-returns-to-operational-status-and-ready-to-receive-fuel-8-3-2025); slippage noted via [Sierra Club Michigan chapter blog](https://www.sierraclub.org/michigan/blog/2026/03/issues-continue-pile-palisades-nuclear-plant-restart) ⚠️ advocacy-group source for the delay characterization, not a neutral trade-press confirmation

### Inferences
- The lead-time picture is bimodal and this is a strong game mechanic: the *compute* chip itself (H100-class) is now fast to acquire (weeks-to-months), but the *power infrastructure* around it (transformers, turbines, nuclear) takes years — meaning a game's "compute unit" bottleneck should shift, era by era, from "can I get GPUs" (eras 1–3) to "can I get power to run them" (eras 4–5), matching the multi-year transformer/turbine lead times against the multi-week/month GPU lead times.

## Reliability: failure rates, goodput, and checkpointing overhead on large clusters

### Takeaway
Meta's Llama 3 training run on a 16,384-H100 cluster is the best-documented real-world reliability data point available: over a 54-day training period the cluster suffered 466 total job interruptions (about one every 3 hours), 419 of them unexpected (the rest planned maintenance), with GPU-related issues (including HBM3 memory failures) causing the majority of unexpected failures — yet automated recovery kept effective training time (goodput) above 90%, which is the single most useful "mishap frequency vs. recoverability" number for a game's training-run-mishap mechanic.

### Cited Findings
- Meta's 16,384-H100 GPU cluster training Llama 3 (2024) experienced component failures at an average rate of once every 3 hours over the 54-day training period; total job interruptions numbered 466, of which 47 were planned maintenance and 419 were unexpected — [Tom's Hardware, "Faulty Nvidia H100 GPUs and HBM3 memory caused half of failures during Llama 3 training"](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/faulty-nvidia-h100-gpus-and-hbm3-memory-caused-half-of-the-failures-during-llama-3-training-one-failure-every-three-hours-for-metas-16384-gpu-training-cluster) ⚠️ this is a tech-press summary of Meta's own Llama 3 technical report; the primary Meta paper itself was not directly fetched in this pass
- Failure-cause breakdown among the 419 unexpected interruptions: 58.7% were GPU-related (30.1% various GPU failures including NVLink failures, 17.2% HBM3 memory failures specifically, with the remainder of the 58.7% from other GPU-adjacent causes); only 2 CPU failures were recorded across the whole run — [same Tom's Hardware source, corroborated by Data Center Dynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/meta-report-details-hundreds-of-gpu-and-hbm3-related-interruptions-to-llama-3-training-run/)
- Despite the interruption frequency, Meta reported maintaining greater than 90% effective training time (goodput) over the run via automated detection and recovery — [search-synthesis of the Llama 3 technical report's reliability discussion](https://www.moomoo.com/community/feed/meta-reports-that-half-of-the-failures-during-llama-3-112873025896854) ⚠️ same caveat — secondary summary of Meta's primary paper, not independently confirmed by directly reading the paper in this pass
- Llama 3's own report additionally notes at least one interruption per day attributable specifically to routine maintenance (bug fixes, urgent security patches, firmware/driver updates), separate from the unplanned hardware-failure interruptions above — [search-synthesis, same sources]

### Inferences
- "One failure every three hours on a 16k-GPU cluster, ~90% goodput maintained via automated recovery" is directly usable as a game formula: e.g., mishap probability per in-game time-tick scaling with cluster size, with a "checkpointing/recovery tech" upgrade that converts a would-be total-run-loss event into a small goodput tax (the ~10% goodput loss Meta reported) rather than a catastrophic restart.
- The dominance of GPU/HBM hardware failures (not networking, not software) as the leading failure cause is a reasonable basis for tying a game's mishap rate to the *hardware generation and scale* of the compute unit, rather than to unrelated factors — bigger, newer (more failure-prone in aggregate, simply due to more components) clusters should have proportionally more frequent mishaps, offset by better automated recovery in later eras.

### Gaps
- No comparably detailed public reliability data was found for any other named large training run (GPT-4, Gemini, Grok, or any 2025–2026 100k+ GPU run); the Llama 3 disclosure appears to be an unusually transparent outlier rather than an industry norm, so the game's mishap-rate calibration is effectively anchored to a single (very well-documented) data point.
- Specific checkpointing overhead (e.g., % of training time or compute spent on checkpoint writes, or time-to-resume-from-checkpoint) was not found as a standalone quantified figure in this pass — only the aggregate ">90% goodput" outcome, which bundles checkpointing overhead together with all other interruption-recovery costs.
- No data was found in this pass on whether/how failure rates scale with cluster size beyond the single 16,384-GPU data point (i.e., no comparison figure for a 100k+ GPU cluster's failure rate per unit time).

## Depreciation and generations: NVIDIA release cadence, performance per generation, price per GPU-hour over time, useful-life debate

### Takeaway
NVIDIA's data-center GPU generations have shipped roughly every 18–24 months (Ampere/A100 in 2020, Hopper/H100 in 2022, Blackwell/B200 in 2024–25, Rubin targeted for 2026), each delivering a large jump in FLOPS; rental prices for each generation fall sharply as the *next* generation ships and supply eases (H100 rental prices fell from $8–10/hour in 2024 to roughly $1.80–3.50/hour by mid-2026), while a live, unresolved dispute over useful-life/depreciation schedules (already covered in the companion note's Market Dynamics section, via the Michael Burry vs. NVIDIA/hyperscaler argument) means "how fast does last era's compute unit lose value" is a real, contested modeling choice rather than a settled number.

### Cited Findings
- NVIDIA data-center GPU generation timeline: A100 (Ampere) released Q2 2020; H100 (Hopper) launched 2022 on TSMC's 4N process; H200 (Hopper refresh) announced 2024/available 2025, with 141 GB HBM3e and ~4.89 TB/s memory bandwidth, delivering ~241.3 TFLOPS FP16; B200 and B300 (Blackwell, Blackwell Ultra) became available in 2025; Rubin (R100) is scheduled for release around Q3 2026 — [search-synthesis of Civo, IntuitionLabs, and Spheron Blog GPU-comparison pages](https://www.spheron.network/blog/nvidia-rubin-vs-blackwell-vs-hopper/) ⚠️ no single NVIDIA primary datasheet or keynote transcript was directly fetched for these dates in this pass; treat exact launch quarters (especially H200/B200/B300's 2025 "availability" vs. "announcement" dates) as approximate
- Performance figures: H100 delivers up to 1,979 TFLOPS FP16 Tensor throughput and 3,958 TFLOPS INT8; Rubin is described as delivering 50 PFLOPS FP4 with 22 TB/s HBM4 bandwidth — roughly 5x and 2.8x the FP4/bandwidth of B200/B300 respectively; Rubin Ultra (targeted H2 2027) is described as doubling die count per package to reach 100 PFLOPS FP4 — [same search-synthesis source](https://www.thundercompute.com/blog/nvidia-rubin-architecture) ⚠️ same caveat — vendor-comparison blog synthesis, not a primary NVIDIA spec sheet

## Export controls timeline on chips

### Takeaway
US chip-export policy toward China went through several distinct phases in the research window: the initial October 2022 controls, a stricter October 2023 update adding a formal "performance density" threshold to close a loophole (aggregating many smaller chips to match a banned chip's power), a 2025 on-again/off-again H20 saga (halted April 2025, quietly restored July 2025), and a December 2025/January 2026 shift to a case-by-case H200 licensing regime paired with a 25% tariff — a genuinely oscillating policy that a game should model as a periodically-flipping event rather than a fixed rule (this matches the companion note's own conclusion in its Geopolitics section, and is not re-derived independently here beyond the specific 2022–2023 threshold mechanics below).

### Cited Findings
- The initial October 7, 2022 BIS export controls restricted advanced computing and semiconductor-manufacturing-equipment exports to China; in response, chip designers (including NVIDIA) legally developed cut-down chips (e.g., the A800/H800) that fell just below the initial control thresholds — [Covington & Burling](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/10/us-expands-october-7-2022-export-controls-restrictions-on-advanced-computing-and-semiconductor-manufacturing-items)
- The October 17, 2023 BIS update (effective November 17, 2023) closed that loophole by adding a formal "performance density threshold," explicitly designed to prevent circumvention via "simply purchasing a larger number of smaller data center AI chips which, if combined, would be equally powerful as restricted chips" — [CSET/Georgetown, "Explainer: The Commerce Department's October 2023 Export Control Update"](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/bis-2023-update-explainer/)
- The October 2023 rule identifies controlled chips using three combined criteria: total processing performance (TPP), performance density (PD), and whether the chip is "designed or marketed" for datacenter use. Specific numeric thresholds: a chip is controlled if it has TPP ≥ 2400 and < 4800 combined with PD ≥ 1.6 and < 5.92, OR TPP ≥ 1600 combined with PD ≥ 3.2 and < 5.92 (chips exceeding the higher PD band of 5.92 are controlled regardless of TPP tier, per the broader rule structure) — [LBNL Export Control summary of the BIS rule, "A Bigger Yard, A Higher Fence"](https://exportcontrol.lbl.gov/a-bigger-yard-a-higher-fence-understanding-biss-expanded-controls-on-advanced-computing-exports/); official rule text at [BIS.gov](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/bis-updated-public-information-page-export-controls-imposed-advanced-computing-semiconductor) ⚠️ the LBNL and BIS pages were reached via search snippet/summary in this pass, not read end-to-end as full documents
- The Biden-era "AI diffusion rule" (a broader, tiered global framework that would have taken effect May 2025) was rescinded by the Trump administration before it took effect — [IISS, "The US pivot on regulating AI diffusion"](https://www.iiss.org/publications/strategic-comments/2025/12/the-us-pivot-on-regulating-ai-diffusion/) ⚠️ already noted in the companion research note; repeated here for the export-controls-specific timeline
- H20 saga: the Trump administration halted AI-chip exports (including H20) to China in April 2025, then quietly reversed the policy in July 2025, allowing NVIDIA to resume H20 shipments — [Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/trump-lifts-ai-chip-ban-china-nvidia) (already cited in the companion note)
- H200 policy: on December 8, 2025, the administration announced a one-year waiver of export restrictions on H200 chips for AI-datacenter use; the Department of Commerce codified this into a formal rule on January 13, 2026, with case-by-case BIS license review for H200, AMD MI325X, and similar chips, paired with a 25% tariff on chips meeting the same performance thresholds; as of the sources reviewed, State Department review has reportedly stalled actual shipments even after Commerce completed its own analysis — [Introl Blog](https://introl.com/blog/bis-h200-china-export-policy-ai-overwatch-act-2026); tariff detail via [BIS press release](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-revises-license-review-policy-semiconductors-exported-china) (all already cited in the companion note; repeated here for the dedicated export-controls timeline)

### Inferences
- The 2023 "performance density" threshold mechanic (closing the "many small chips = one big chip" loophole) is a nice, concrete rule a game could reference narratively (e.g., an event where a player's workaround chip design gets retroactively banned once regulators notice the aggregate-performance loophole), even though it's a regulatory-drafting detail rather than a physical/economic number per se.
- Combined with the companion note's finding that the "diffusion rule" was rescinded and H20/H200 policy flip-flopped twice in about a year, the overall 2022–2026 export-control pattern is: tighten (Oct 2022) → tighten further, close loophole (Oct 2023) → planned broad tightening rescinded before taking effect (rescinded before May 2025) → halt (Apr 2025) → reverse (Jul 2025) → new conditional-access regime with tariff (Dec 2025/Jan 2026). A game modeling this as roughly one significant policy event every 6–12 months is well supported by the dates found.

### Gaps
- No specific information was found in this research pass (as distinct from the companion note) about a "2026 H200" threshold change beyond the December 2025/January 2026 case-by-case regime already described; the assignment's framing of "2026 H200" as if it were a distinct new threshold event was not corroborated as a separate rule change — it appears to be the same Dec 2025/Jan 2026 rule, not an additional 2026-specific tightening or loosening.
- Full original BIS rule text (Federal Register notices) for either the October 2022 or October 2023 rules was not directly fetched end-to-end in this pass; the specific TPP/PD numeric thresholds above trace to a secondary (LBNL export-control office) summary of the rule rather than the Federal Register text itself.
