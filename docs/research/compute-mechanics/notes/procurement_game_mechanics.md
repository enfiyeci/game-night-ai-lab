# Proven Game Mechanics for Acquiring a Scarce Capacity Resource (Contracts, Auctions, Markets, Construction Lead Times, Suppliers)

## Auctions and allocation: which auction formats suit "limited GPU allocation among rival labs"?

### Takeaway
Board games use at least four distinct auction/allocation formats — English (ascending) bidding on discrete lumpy items, a dynamic buy-price resource market with automatic replenishment, a positional "cheaper the longer it waits" market row, and a menu of different auction types keyed to the item being sold. For a 20-turn, ~1-minute-per-turn game, the dynamic resource market (Power Grid) and the decaying-price offer row (Suburbia) are the best fits for "limited GPU allocation among rivals," because both resolve in a single glance-and-click decision without requiring back-and-forth bidding rounds; a full ascending auction (Power Grid's power-plant auction, Modern Art) is higher-fidelity but costs more real time per resolution.

### Cited Findings
- Power Grid's power-plant auction is a straightforward ascending English auction where players bid against each other for discrete power plants, and a player can deliberately bid up a plant they do not want, purely to drain a rival's cash — a "spite bid" that is a recognized strategic layer on top of plain price discovery. — [BGG: Power Grid game page / Meeple Mountain review, via search synthesis](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/power-grid/)
- Power Grid's resource market (coal, oil, garbage, uranium) is not an auction at all but a priced ladder: each resource type has a fixed track of increasingly expensive slots; buying pulls from the cheapest open slot and pays that slot's listed price; after each round the market is restocked starting from the *most expensive* empty slots downward, so heavy buying by many players drives future prices up while restocking brings them back down — a self-regulating supply/demand curve with no negotiation required. — [BGG "Million Dollar Mechanics" blog post on Power Grid's Resource Market](https://boardgamegeek.com/blogpost/62868/power-grid-resource-market) ⚠️ this specific page returned HTTP 403 on fetch and could not be read in full; the description above is reconstructed from the WebSearch tool's snippet-level synthesis of the page and other search results (Wikipedia, Gamerules.com, UltraBoardGames), not a full read of the primary source.
- Power Grid's overall design is described (by a secondary summary, not the designer directly) as fusing "auction pressure, resource scarcity, map expansion, income pacing, and turn order into one living economic engine" so that it is nominally about power plants and fuel but is "really a game about appetite" — i.e., about reading how much rivals want a thing and taxing that want. — [Opinionated Gamers, "Funkenschlag at Fifteen: The Story of Power Grid"](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/07/11/funkenschlag-at-fifteen-the-story-of-power-grid/) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis only, not fetched and read end to end in this session.
- Suburbia's real-estate tile market is a horizontal row of tiles at fixed price slots ($10 down to free); each turn the oldest tile is removed, all tiles shift down (cheaper), and a new tile enters at the top (most expensive) slot — so the "auction" is really a patience mechanic: wait and a tile you want gets cheaper, but a rival may take it first. — [UltraBoardGames Suburbia rules / Theology of Games review, via search synthesis](https://ultraboardgames.com/suburbia/game-rules.php)
- Modern Art uses a different auction *type* for each card played, determined by the artist symbol on the card: open/free-for-all bidding (anyone can raise, no turn order), once-around (each player bids exactly once in turn order, highest wins), fixed/set-price (the seller names one price and offers it around the table until someone accepts or it fails), and simultaneous/sealed bid (everyone commits a bid in secret and reveals at once). The 1992 game is considered a landmark for pairing five artists' price trajectories with different auction formats in one box, forcing players to learn multiple negotiation postures inside a single session. — [Wikipedia "Modern Art (game)"; corroborated by BGG/CMON summaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Art_(game)) ⚠️ the Wikipedia article itself does not spell out each format's mechanics in detail (confirmed by a full fetch of the page in this session); the mechanical descriptions above rely on secondary WebSearch-synthesized sources (Oink Games, CMON, Bitewing Games "Top Auctioning Board Games"), not a single fully-read primary rulebook.
- Container's foreign-island sale step is a blind (sealed-bid) auction among the *other* players when a ship arrives; the ship's owner then chooses to sell at the winning bid (collecting a matching government subsidy) or to buy in themselves at that price — putting the triggering player in a "should I cash out or double down" decision driven by a price rivals just set. — [Wikipedia "Container (board game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Container_(board_game)) (fetched and read in full in this session).

### Inferences
- For a fast solo-vs-AI game, a static "market row with decaying price" (Suburbia-style) or a "resource ladder with buy-price-and-replenish" (Power Grid-style) resolves a GPU-allocation decision in one click ("buy this batch of compute now at its current price, or wait for it to get cheaper/scarcer") without needing to simulate rival bidding each turn — this matches the 1-minute-per-turn budget far better than a live ascending auction against multiple AI bidders, which needs several bid/pass exchanges to feel real.
- Keying the "auction type" to what is being bought (as Modern Art does) is a way to encode *why* real compute deals differ — e.g., spot GPU rental could use an open/ascending format (price rises visibly as AI rivals compete for the same batch), while a multi-year take-or-pay contract could use a fixed/set-price offer (accept the supplier's number or walk away, no bidding), inside the same game without changing the core loop.
- Container's "rivals set the price, then you decide whether to sell into it or buy into it" step is a strong template for representing export-control-driven scarcity spikes: an AI rival's sudden GPU demand could set a market price the player must react to rather than control.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the primary BGG "Million Dollar Mechanics" article on Power Grid's resource market (HTTP 403), so the fine-grained replenishment rule is sourced from secondary snippets rather than one authoritative primary text; the core mechanic (buy from cheapest open slot, restock from the top down) is corroborated across multiple independent summaries so I treat it as reliable, but flag it per the sourcing rule above.
- I did not find primary source detail on Acquire's mechanics within budget; Acquire is a stock/majority-control game (buying shares in merging hotel chains) rather than a resource-auction game, so it is likely a weaker fit for "GPU allocation" than the games above — this is an inference, not a verified claim, and is a genuine gap.

## Contracts and futures: how do games make "lock in early vs. stay flexible" interesting?

### Takeaway
The clearest, well-documented "lock in early vs. stay flexible" mechanic among the games I could verify is Brass: Birmingham's loan system — taking on debt early is cheap (income is already low, so the income-reduction penalty barely bites) but constrains your income track for the rest of the game, while waiting to take a loan is safer but means missing the early building rush. Container's multi-step "produce → sell to harbor → sell to ship → auction on the island" chain, where different steps must be performed by *different* players, is a strong proven pattern for modeling forward delivery obligations, because a player's goods pass through prices set by rivals at each stage rather than by the producer alone.

### Cited Findings
- In Brass: Birmingham, taking a loan gives an immediate £30 but permanently reduces the player's income track; loans are never repaid, so the only real cost is lower income for the rest of the game — and because income starts low anyway in the early game, an early loan is proportionally the cheapest one to take, creating a genuine "borrow now while it's cheap, or stay flexible and keep full income" tension. — [Wikipedia "Brass (board game)" and Roxley/officialgamerules.org rules summaries, via search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass_(board_game)) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis, not a full read of one primary source in this session.
- Container forces a produced good through four ownership hops — production (by the producer, who sets an initial price of 1–4), purchase into a harbor/dockside store (by a different player, who re-prices it 2–6), shipping, and a blind-auction sale on the foreign island (among yet other players) — meaning no single player controls a good's price end-to-end; profit depends on correctly forecasting what a rival will later pay for goods you cannot yourself re-price once sold onward. — [Wikipedia "Container (board game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Container_(board_game)) (fetched and read in full).
- 18xx games represent forced technology upgrades and delivery obsolescence through "train rusting": each new, more expensive, higher-capacity train type that enters the game causes the oldest train type still in play to be permanently removed ("rust") from every company that holds it, without compensation, converting a healthy company into a suddenly insolvent one if its owner didn't upgrade in time. — [Wikipedia "18XX"; Send More Paramedics blog "18XX: A System of Systems"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX) ⚠️ the Wikipedia article itself gives limited mechanical detail (confirmed by a full fetch in this session — it states the basic rusting rule but does not walk through timing/strategy in depth); the fuller strategic-tension description comes from WebSearch synthesis of BGG threads and the "18XX: A System of Systems" blog, not a single fully-read primary source.

### Inferences
- Brass's loan mechanic is a clean, low-complexity template for "lock in a take-or-pay compute contract": a one-time resource injection (compute now) that permanently taxes a recurring value (income, i.e., cash available for later flexible spot buys) — this maps directly onto "sign a multi-year contract now for guaranteed GPUs, but it eats into the operating budget you'd otherwise have for spot rental or R&D every subsequent turn."
- Container's multi-hop pricing chain is a strong template for representing prepayment-for-priority-allocation: a player who "pre-buys" compute at an early, player-controlled price is protected from the volatility that hits players who wait and must buy at whatever price the market (rivals' bids) has moved to by a later stage.
- Neither source I found gives a clean example of an explicit "futures contract" (pay now, receive later, at a locked price) as a standalone mechanic with a name — this appears to be a gap in board-game precedent, and the closest real analogs (loans, delayed obsolescence, multi-hop pricing) are proxies rather than direct matches.

### Gaps
- I did not find strong sourced material on Age of Steam, Railroad Tycoon, or 18xx "train rush" *as a distinct concept from rusting* within the tool budget; the search results conflated rusting with general train-development pacing. This is a real gap — the "should I buy a train that will rust soon vs. wait for the next tier" decision (as distinct from the rusting *consequence* itself) is under-sourced here.
- I did not investigate EVE Online contracts, Anno supply chains, X4, Victoria 3 markets, or Capitalism Lab within the tool budget for this research pass (time/tool-call constraints); these remain unresearched and should not be treated as covered.

## Construction pipelines and lead times: how do games show a pipeline of capacity arriving later, with risk of delay?

### Takeaway
Frostpunk's building-under-construction state (a placed building sits as an incomplete "scaffold" that can be pausable, and is workforce-limited so throwing more workers at it can speed completion) and Terraforming Mars's two-phase "buy a production upgrade now, only *collect* the extra resource starting next generation" cycle are the two best-documented examples of a visible pipeline where capacity ordered this turn does not arrive until a future turn.

### Cited Findings
- In Frostpunk, buildings under construction exist as a distinct "scaffold" state that consumes wood/steel; construction can be paused when resource storage is near its cap, and canceling a scaffold gives a 100% refund, so the pipeline has an explicit "committed but not yet delivered, and still cancelable" state. Construction speed scales with the number of citizens assigned to work it, so players can trade labor allocation for shorter lead times. — [Frostpunk Wiki "Advanced Tips"; Steam community discussions, via search synthesis](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Advanced_Tips) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis, not fetched and read end to end.
- In Terraforming Mars, resource production and resource collection are separated: players buy/play cards that *increase production* at any time during a generation, but the actual resource gain from that increased production only happens in the once-per-round "Production Phase" that follows, meaning an investment made this turn pays off starting next turn, not immediately — a one-turn lead time baked into the core loop. — [Dized Rules "Terraforming Mars: Production" and "Production Phase"; corroborated by rulebook PDF, via search synthesis](https://rules.dized.com/game/Y2e4M8-fRH-fyWwjwVHsbw/DP-DSHzDS2u5nwrXRHm6Lg/production-phase) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis, not fetched and read end to end.

### Inferences
- The "commit this turn, receive next turn" delay in Terraforming Mars is the simplest possible lead-time mechanic and is likely the most directly portable pattern for a 20-turn/1-minute-per-turn game: "build a data center this turn, it starts producing compute from next turn" requires no extra UI beyond marking an order as pending.
- Frostpunk's pausable/refundable scaffold state suggests a way to represent permit or power delays realistically without punishing the player irrecoverably: a data-center build could be interruptible (paused, not lost) if the player's power supply falls short, then resumed once power catches up — modeling the real dynamic where construction can stall on a bottleneck resource (power, permits) without wasting the investment outright.

### Gaps
- I did not find sourced material on Factorio/Satisfactory power bottlenecks, SimCity/Cities: Skylines power plant lead times, or Ticket to Ride within the tool budget for this pass; these remain unresearched.
- I did not verify whether Frostpunk's construction delay scales in a way that creates a genuine "risk of delay" (e.g., a chance the build stalls or fails) versus simply "slower if under-resourced" — the sources found describe throughput trade-offs, not stochastic delay/failure risk specifically.

## Obsolescence: representing GPU depreciation and new chip generations

### Takeaway
18xx train rusting (assets you own become worthless the instant a rival or the market triggers the next technology tier) and the tycoon-genre hardware lifecycle (a console/product's value or production cost follows a curve — expensive and valuable at launch, cheaper mid-life, then obsolete and low-value again) are two well-documented, contrasting models: rusting is a hard cliff-edge loss, while the tycoon-genre lifecycle is a smoother decay:

### Cited Findings
- 18xx train rusting: when a new train type enters the game (usually triggered by a player purchasing it), the *oldest* train type still in circulation is removed from every company holding it, with no refund — turning previously profitable companies insolvent overnight if their owner had not upgraded ahead of the trigger. Purchasing a new train tier also commonly triggers other simultaneous obsolescence-adjacent effects: new map tiles become available, some private companies close, and non-tile city values increase. — [Wikipedia "18XX"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX) (fetched and read in full — confirms the basic rusting rule and phase-change linkage, though with limited elaboration on strategic timing). Strategic-tension framing ("players must upgrade trains just ahead of a rusting event or to trigger one") is from WebSearch synthesis of secondary sources and is not independently confirmed in the primary text I read. ⚠️
- Mad Games Tycoon: consoles become "obsolete," instantly losing their full star rating (a 5-point quality/desirability score drops to effectively zero), and each hardware product's production cost follows a lifecycle curve — highest at release, declining over time with optimization (down to roughly 80% of initial cost), then rising again as the hardware becomes outdated and harder/more expensive to keep producing. Mad Games Tycoon 2 makes this explicit and time-based: all hardware has a fixed lifespan, and after that window the console is superseded regardless of continued investment, forcing periodic "launch a revised model or an all-new generation" decisions. — [Steam Community guides and discussion threads for Mad Games Tycoon / Mad Games Tycoon 2, via search synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2745388756) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis, not fetched and read end to end; I could not reach a single authoritative primary source (e.g., an official wiki page) describing this mechanic within the tool budget.

### Inferences
- The 18xx rusting model is a better fit for representing GPU-generation shocks than a smooth depreciation curve, because real compute shortages/obsolescence in the prompt's framing move suddenly across a supply chain (chips → packaging → memory → power) rather than gradually — a "hard cliff" trigger (a rival lab or the market crosses a tech threshold, and your older GPU fleet's training throughput/value drops sharply) captures that better than smooth decay.
- The tycoon-genre lifecycle curve (cheap to build mid-life, expensive again near end-of-life) is a plausible way to model the real "next-gen chip shortage" dynamic in reverse: a compute type that is currently scarce (leading edge) becomes cheap and abundant mid-cycle, then becomes comparatively expensive to keep running (power, maintenance, opportunity cost of not upgrading) as it ages — but I could not find a game source that explicitly frames the *late-life* cost rise this way (as opposed to simply "value drops"); this is my inference, not a sourced claim.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced material within budget on Software Inc.'s or Game Dev Tycoon's console-obsolescence mechanics specifically (only Mad Games Tycoon); Game Dev Tycoon is known for a similar "new console generation" dynamic but I could not verify its mechanical details with a source in this pass.
- I did not research crypto-mining simulators within the tool budget for this pass; this remains a genuine gap against the original key question.

## Push-your-luck and supplier failure risk; debt and leverage

### Takeaway
I found solid, verifiable material on debt/leverage (Brass's loans, described above, and Offworld Trading Company's forced-buyout mechanic which is a leverage/majority-control risk rather than debt per se) but did not find strong sourced material specifically on "push-your-luck" supplier-failure mechanics (e.g., a card-flip or die-roll chance that a delivery doesn't arrive) within the tool budget for this research pass.

### Cited Findings
- Offworld Trading Company's endgame victory condition is achieved by buying out a majority of shares in every rival company; shares can be bought one at a time until five remain, at which point the last five must be bought together in a single, telegraphed move — giving the target one last chance to defend by buying its own stock. Each additional share purchased from a rival for buyout purposes costs a 20% premium, which both rewards moving early and discourages multiple players from ganging up simultaneously on the same target (only one player benefits from a given buyout). — [Designer Notes #17, "OTC Designer Notes: Stock Market," Soren Johnson](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/) (fetched and read in full in this session).
- Brass: Birmingham's loan mechanic (see Contracts section above) is the clearest sourced example of debt/leverage: immediate capital in exchange for a permanent income penalty, with the penalty's real cost depending on when in the game the loan is taken. — [Wikipedia "Brass (board game)", via search synthesis] ⚠️ (see caveat above).

### Inferences
- Offworld's buyout-premium/telegraphed-final-move design is a strong template for "aggressive rival lab tries to lock up a scarce supplier's whole capacity": letting the player see a rival's leverage building up (visible share count, visible premium cost) before the final commitment gives the player a chance to react — this could map to an AI rival "cornering" a chip supplier's output, with the player getting one visible warning turn before the corner completes.
- The prompt's specific interest in "push-your-luck / supplier failure risk" (a stochastic chance that a contracted delivery doesn't show up — modeling real export-control or shortage disruption) is not well evidenced by the sources I found in this pass. This is a genuine research gap, not an absence of the mechanic in games generally — push-your-luck mechanics are common in the hobby (e.g., dice-and-bust games), but I did not find one specifically applied to *supplier/delivery* failure within my tool budget, so I am not asserting a match here.

### Gaps
- Did not research classic push-your-luck games (e.g., Can't Stop, Incan Gold) or connect them to a supplier-failure framing within the tool budget — this key question is only partially answered.
- Did not verify Offworld's debt mechanic (the game also reportedly has a debt/loan system distinct from the stock buyout system) — the two Designer Notes pieces I found (via search) focused on the stock market, not on a separate debt mechanic; I did not fetch a source describing OTC debt directly, so I cannot report on it beyond noting it likely exists (mentioned in the original assignment framing) without a confirmed source. ⚠️

## Deckbuilding or card-drafting procurement as a compact UI for "this turn's deal offers"

### Takeaway
Century: Spice Road's shifting merchant-card row (take a card, pay a cost proportional to its position, row refills from the right) is a well-documented, compact template for "this turn's compute deal offers," and it explicitly builds on and extends Splendor's simpler fixed-slot card market.

### Cited Findings
- In Century: Spice Road, merchant cards sit in a row next to the deck; taking a card requires placing one resource cube on every card to its left in the row (the leftmost card is always free to take), and the player also picks up any cubes previously left on the card they take as a bonus; after a card is taken, the row shifts left and a new card is drawn onto the right end. Reviewers describe this as blending "deckbuilding-lite drafting" with resource trading and note it creates strategic tension purely from card *position* rather than from a hidden or auctioned price. — [Nerdist "Why Century: Spice Road Is Splendor 2.0"; Wikipedia "Century: Spice Road", via search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century:_Spice_Road) ⚠️ read via WebSearch synthesis, not fetched and read end to end in this session.
- The comparison sources describe Splendor's card market as a simpler, fixed-slot "collectathon" (cards sit in static slots at set costs, refilled from a deck when taken) against which Century: Spice Road is explicitly positioned as an evolution. — [Nerdist, via search synthesis](https://nerdist.com/article/why-century-spice-road-is-splendor-2-0/) ⚠️ same caveat as above.

### Inferences
- A shifting offer row (Century-style) with position-based cost is a strong, low-friction UI for "this turn's compute deal offers" in a 1-minute-turn game: the player scans a short row of GPU/contract offers, each with a visible price that depends only on its position (cheap-but-taken-later vs. costly-but-available-now), and commits with one click — no bidding, no negotiation overhead, and it naturally creates a "grab it before a rival does" pressure without simulating rival decision-making in detail.
- This pattern could double as the mechanism for representing export-control-driven scarcity moving across the supply chain (chips → packaging → memory → power): different "lanes" of the offer row could represent different bottleneck resources, with cards in a currently-scarce lane costing more or refilling more slowly.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a primary rulebook for Century: Spice Road or Splendor in this session; the mechanical description above (line-cost, leftmost-free, bonus cubes) is corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources found via WebSearch but not independently verified against an official rulebook PDF within the tool budget.

## Designer writing on resource scarcity and meaningful choice

### Takeaway
The one designer-authored primary source I fully read — Soren Johnson's "OTC Designer Notes #17: Stock Market" — makes an explicit, well-articulated case that a resource/economic system's job is to generate a small number of clearly telegraphed, high-stakes decision points (the "rush vs. turtle vs. boom" triangle) rather than to be maximally realistic; scarcity is valuable in a game specifically because it forces those decision points to matter.

### Cited Findings
- Soren Johnson states that Offworld Trading Company's stock market was "the hardest part of the game design to get right" and changed significantly close to release, because it had to serve two purposes at once: a thematic purpose (a modern economic game needs a stock market to reflect success) and a structural purpose (providing a legible victory condition for a game where military conquest isn't available). He frames the resulting system as an accidental discovery of a classic RTS triangle — rush (early buyout attempts, sacrificing growth), turtle (defending your own stock, especially a critical fifth share), and boom (reinvesting in headquarters upgrades and expansion) — and calls out specific moments (buying your own fifth share to block a majority buyout; spending everything on a headquarters upgrade but forgetting to defend your own stock) as the designed "meaningful choice" moments the system is built to produce. — [Soren Johnson, "OTC Designer Notes #17: Stock Market," designer-notes.com](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/) (fetched and read in full in this session).

### Inferences
- Johnson's framing — design the economy backward from a small number of legible, high-stakes decision points, rather than forward from realism — is directly applicable to the 20-turn/1-minute-per-turn compute game: the design brief should identify 2-4 recurring decision archetypes (e.g., "lock in a contract vs. stay liquid for spot," "defend your compute lead vs. reinvest in the next tier," "prepay for priority vs. risk the open market") and build the resource system to make each one visibly costly and visibly telegraphed, the way OTC's share-buyout system telegraphs an opponent's building leverage before the final move.

### Gaps
- I was not able to fetch or fully read the companion piece, Designer Notes #18 (the interview format, distinct from #17's essay), within the tool budget; it may contain additional material specifically on the market/pricing system (as opposed to the stock/buyout system covered in #17) and was not covered here.
- I did not find or read any Friedemann Friese primary-source writing (blog, interview transcript) on Power Grid's resource scarcity within the tool budget; the one scarcity-focused quote I have ("fuses auction pressure, resource scarcity... a game about appetite") is from a secondary retrospective (Opinionated Gamers), not from Friese himself, and is flagged ⚠️ above under the Auctions section. I did not find a GDC talk on economy design within budget either — this is a gap against the original key question.
