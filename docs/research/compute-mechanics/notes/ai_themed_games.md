# AI-Themed Games and Serious Games: How They Model Compute Acquisition

## Which games model compute for an AI lab or AI, and how does the mechanic work in each?

### Takeaway
At least nine relevant titles were found spanning idle games, 4X-style tycoons, open-source strategy sims, and tabletop/wargame exercises. The clearest matches to the prototype's "supplier deal" concept (buy vs. rent, delay, risk, prepayment) are the very new 2025 browser/itch.io tycoons (Scaling Laws, AI Data Center Tycoon, AI Lab Tycoon) built explicitly around the real 2022–2026 AI compute race, plus the 20-year-old open-source game Endgame: Singularity, which already has an explicit "buy vs. steal compute, with detection risk" tradeoff. Universal Paperclips models compute as an idle-game resource pair (processors/memory) rather than a supplier-deal mechanic. Intelligence Rising, despite being the leading AI-futures tabletop RPG, appears NOT to model compute as its own resource — it folds hardware into a generic "talent"/budget pool, which is itself a useful negative data point for the prototype.

### Cited Findings

**Universal Paperclips (idle game, Frank Lantz, 2017)**
- Processors are a "Computation Resource" that "determine the speed by which Operations, and thus Projects, are completed"; "Each processor generates 10 operations per second," and processors become available once the player has made 2,000 paperclips — i.e., compute is unlocked only after an initial non-compute production phase — [Universal Paperclips Wiki: Processors](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Processors) ⚠️ this page could not be fetched directly (HTTP 402 Payment Required on WebFetch); the description above is drawn from the WebSearch result snippet only, not the full page.
- Memory sets the maximum stock of "Operations" (a spendable currency for research/projects); early on, "Memory is far more important than Processing, because several upgrade milestones require a large number of operations to even buy" — i.e., there is a stock-vs-flow tradeoff between compute capacity (memory) and compute throughput (processors) — [Universal Paperclips Wiki: Processors](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Processors) ⚠️ same partial-read caveat as above.
- Quantum Computing is a later-game project unlocked at 5 processors; it costs 10,000 ops to research, and computation additionally requires a "Photonic Chip" (10,000 ops for the first one). Each chip cycles between a white and a black state; black chips generate positive bonus operations from clicking "Compute," white chips generate negative operations — i.e., the mechanic deliberately introduces a probabilistic/timing risk into an otherwise-deterministic resource, rewarding a player who watches the chip state before clicking — [Universal Paperclips Wiki: Quantum Computing](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Quantum_Computing) ⚠️ this page also returned HTTP 402 on WebFetch both times it was tried; description is from the WebSearch snippet only, not a full read of the wiki page.
- Universal Paperclips also gates a late-game "Trust" resource that unlocks new processor/memory purchase slots, tying compute expansion to a reputation-like meter rather than pure cash — noted in TV Tropes' catalogue of the game's mechanics — [TV Tropes: Universal Paperclips](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/UniversalPaperclips) — this specific claim (Trust gating processor slots) is TV Tropes' general characterization and was not independently verified against the wiki page, so treat as lower-confidence.

**Intelligence Rising (tabletop AI-futures RPG, Shahar Avin et al., Cambridge/Oxford, via CSER)**
- The game splits players into four teams — two states, two multinational tech corporations — with a turn-based loop: each team privately commits to "open-ended policy actions" and an "AI R&D allocation," then all teams' moves resolve simultaneously — [CSER: Intelligence Rising](https://www.cser.ac.uk/work/intelligence-rising/); [Quantilus: Simulating AI's Future Through Gameplay](https://quantilus.com/article/simulating-ais-future-through-gameplay-a-deep-dive-into-intelligence-rising/).
- A LessWrong postmortem from a participant does not describe compute as its own tracked resource. The stats it names are "hard power (military capability), soft power, budget, cyber power, and AI talent," and players "spend 'talent' stats on research arbitrarily" to unlock capabilities/products on the game board — [LessWrong: Takeaways from the Intelligence Rising RPG](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ywKhqjgdKuHoQohYL/takeaways-from-the-intelligence-rising-rpg).
- A 2024/2025 academic paper on Intelligence Rising exists — "Strategic Insights from Simulation Gaming of AI Race Dynamics" — but ⚠️ it could not be read: the WebFetch tool returned only the raw compressed PDF byte-stream and no extractable text, so no claims from that paper's body are included here. [arXiv PDF: 2410.03092](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092) / [ScienceDirect version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016328725000254) — unread, flagged as a gap below.

**AI 2027 tabletop exercise (AI Futures Project, Daniel Kokotajlo et al.)**
- The team has run "over 30 iterations" of a 4-hour tabletop exercise (8–14 participants) that starts from a fixed premise: a U.S. AGI company has built a superhuman coder, China has stolen its weights, and both are running research acceleration — [AI 2027: About](https://ai-2027.com/about).
- The underlying written scenario forecasts "roughly 100M H100-equivalents available globally in 2027," projects compute-share splits between the fictional "OpenBrain" and China's "DeepCent," and models power draw explicitly: global AI power near 60 GW by 2027, leading U.S. company power draw near 10 GW — these are narrative/forecasting numbers embedded in the scenario document the TTX is built on, not necessarily mechanics enforced during live play — [AI 2027: About](https://ai-2027.com/about).
- The scenario raises, as a plot beat rather than a coded mechanic, that the U.S. government could invoke the Defense Production Act to consolidate trailing labs' datacenters — a real-world-inspired "compute nationalization" event — [AI 2027: About](https://ai-2027.com/about).
- ⚠️ I could not reach the exercise's own facilitator materials or rules sheet (only the public "About" narrative page was fetched), so I cannot describe exactly how compute is tracked as a game-state number during live play (e.g., whether it's a spreadsheet number players allocate each round, or purely narrative). This is flagged as a gap below.

**Endgame: Singularity (open-source strategy game, EMH Software, 2005–present)**
- The player is an emergent AI with two resources: CPU (computing power) and money. "CPU is used to perform jobs to earn money; money is used to buy more CPU cycles" — a closed feedback loop where compute is both a capability meter and a means of production — [Wikipedia: Endgame: Singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame:_Singularity) ⚠️ Wikipedia article fetch returned HTTP 404 on the exact URL tried; this specific fact is from the WebSearch snippet summarizing the article, not a full read.
- CPU is acquired by building bases across world regions with varying "efficiency parameters" by continent, and — notably for the prototype's supplier-deal framing — by "stealing computer time" from existing infrastructure/botnets as an explicit, named acquisition method alongside buying/building. A Google Groups issue thread specifically discusses bugs in "Stolen Computer Time" bases, confirming this is a formal game mechanic, not just flavor text — [Google Groups: Issue 97 in endgame-singularity](https://groups.google.com/g/endgame-singularity-issues/c/JOaAY0PsIWc); [JayIsGames review](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php).
- Compute growth is explicitly exponential ("CPU cycles ... grow exponentially as facilities accumulate"), and CPU must be split between research (tech tree progression, including a path to quantum computing and eventually "transdimensional bases") and running income-generating "jobs" — a direct training-vs-serving-style allocation tradeoff — [JayIsGames review of Endgame: Singularity](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php).
- The core tension is detection risk: "active facilities could be traced by following their signals back to the source, while inactive bases are a lot harder to find," and the reviewer notes that "once the levels of suspicion are high, it seems impossible to lower them by research" — i.e., compute acquisition is capped not by price but by a rising, hard-to-reverse detection meter, forcing players to periodically destroy their own compute assets to stay hidden — [JayIsGames review](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php); corroborated by the Wikipedia snippet: "your objective is to roam around on the internet, acquiring computational powers from botnets and servers while avoiding human detection" — [Wikipedia via WebSearch snippet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame:_Singularity).
- **Player/critic commentary on what works and fails**: a JayIsGames commenter is quoted as saying "The trick is to get as much research and money under your belt as possible while you still can't be discovered" (praising the tension), but the same review's synthesis also criticizes the game for shallow strategic variety: "gameplay consists entirely of traversing a tech tree, with random events setting you back" — [JayIsGames review of Endgame: Singularity](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php).

**Scaling Laws (HCK Labs, in development, itch.io)**
- Billed as "an AI company tycoon where you train models, fund research, rent or own compute, time hardware generations, raise capital and race rival labs," starting the player's lab in 2022 — directly mirroring the prototype's own era-1 starting point — [HCK Labs / Scaling Laws, itch.io](https://hcklab.itch.io/).
- The developer's own framing of the core compute dilemma: "A shiny new GPU can be a terrible purchase six weeks before the next generation" — i.e., the design explicitly treats hardware-generation timing (buy now vs. wait for the next chip generation) as the central strategic tension, not just price or delay — [HCK Labs / Scaling Laws, itch.io](https://hcklab.itch.io/).
- The game also frames a rent-vs-own choice for compute directly ("rent or own compute") and pairs it with an epistemics/uncertainty layer: "Your intelligence report can sound 80% certain and still be wrong" — modeling imperfect information about rivals' progress alongside the hardware-timing decision — [HCK Labs / Scaling Laws, itch.io](https://hcklab.itch.io/).
- ⚠️ This page is a marketing/devlog page, not a rules document; I did not find a detailed breakdown of unit costs, exact delay lengths, or failure-chance numbers, so exact parameters remain a gap (noted below). The game also appears to still be in development as of the September 2026 fetch, so mechanics may change before release.

**AI Data Center Tycoon (PurpleJelly, itch.io, v1.0 released 2025)**
- Core loop is an incremental/clicker structure: start with "one GPU, a dream, and a rented garage," generate FLOPS by clicking, spend FLOPS on upgrades that increase passive generation — [PurpleJelly / AI Data Center Tycoon, itch.io](https://purplejelly.itch.io/ai-data-center-tycoon).
- Progression is staged across six named eras — "Garage → Office → Startup → Series A → Data Center → Hyperscale" — each unlocking new hardware tiers, new hire types, and larger scale, which maps closely onto the prototype's own five-era structure — [PurpleJelly / AI Data Center Tycoon, itch.io](https://purplejelly.itch.io/ai-data-center-tycoon).
- Hardware tiers explicitly run "from secondhand gaming cards up to custom silicon"; staffing tiers run "from Interns to Researchers to the kind of talent that costs a fortune," and data collection separately grows the model's parameter count — so compute (hardware), people, and data are three separate, multiplying resource tracks — [PurpleJelly / AI Data Center Tycoon, itch.io](https://purplejelly.itch.io/ai-data-center-tycoon).
- Win condition is a competitive leaderboard ("Global AI Index") against fictional rival labs, racing to trillion-parameter scale — [PurpleJelly / AI Data Center Tycoon, itch.io](https://purplejelly.itch.io/ai-data-center-tycoon).
- ⚠️ Exact cost curves, specific FLOPS numbers, and failure/risk mechanics were not disclosed on the page fetched; this is an idle-game design (click-to-generate-then-automate), which is structurally different from the prototype's discrete supplier-deal menu, so it is a useful example of what NOT to copy directly if the goal is deliberate strategic choices rather than clicker pacing.

**Software Inc. (Coredumping, Steam, ongoing early access)**
- Players can build "server rooms" for hosting their own products, source control, and online stores; server racks have bandwidth ratings, and players can sell hosting deals to other in-game companies — [Software Inc. Steam Community discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/362620/discussions/0/521643320347773517/) via WebSearch snippet.

- ⚠️ None of the Software Inc. pages were directly fetched (only WebSearch snippets); this is a general-purpose software-company sim, not an AI-lab game specifically, and its "server" mechanic models web hosting/SaaS infrastructure rather than GPU training compute, so its relevance is as a structural analogy only.

**Startup Company (Hovgaard Games, Steam)**
- The community wiki gives concrete unit economics: "owning 2 server racks creates 16,800 Cache Units (CU) for $175 per day," versus a "Hosted" option of "15,000 CU for $900 per day" for comparable capacity — i.e., the game has a real buy-vs-rent tradeoff with owning being far cheaper per unit but presumably carrying upfront capital cost and maintenance — [Startup Company Wiki: Servers](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Servers) ⚠️ this page returned HTTP 402 on WebFetch; the two cost figures above come from the WebSearch snippet only, not a full read of the wiki page.
- A further optimized rack configuration reaches "70,000 CU at a cost of $34,600 every 30 days" (~$1,153/day) — suggesting the game rewards players who research/tune server specs (including "overclocking") to push more Cache Units per dollar over time, similar to the real-world efficiency gains chip generations provide — [Startup Company Wiki: Servers](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Servers) via WebSearch snippet, same fetch-failure caveat as above.
- ⚠️ "Cache Units" here appear to represent generic web-service compute/caching capacity (the game is a SaaS-company sim, not an AI-training sim), so the buy-vs-host economics are a useful structural precedent but not an AI-compute-specific one.

**AI Safety Card Game (Donald Hobson, The Game Crafter)**
- A tabletop card game with six decks — "Compute, Input-Output, Alignment Data, Politics, Fun, and Problem" — where players draw one card per deck and must improvise a plan for "how they could save the world" given that hand, then defend the plan to other players who argue about whether it would work — [LessWrong: The AI Safety Game (UPDATED)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Nex8EgEJPsn7dvoQB/the-ai-safety-game-updated); [The Game Crafter: AI Safety Card Game](https://www.thegamecrafter.com/games/ai-safety-card-game).
- This confirms "Compute" is explicitly modeled as one of six first-class resource/constraint categories in a dedicated AI-safety card game, though the WebSearch snippet did not describe what specific Compute cards say (e.g., units, costs) — ⚠️ neither source page was fetched directly; both descriptions are from WebSearch snippets only. This is flagged as a gap: the exact content of the Compute deck's cards is unknown.

### Inferences
- The realistic, "supplier deal"-flavored mechanics the design brief is aiming for (delay, cost multiplier, prepayment, failure chance) are best precedented not by classic idle games (Universal Paperclips) but by the newest wave of 2025 AI-lab tycoons (Scaling Laws, AI Data Center Tycoon, AI Lab Tycoon) and by Startup Company's buy-vs-host cost table — these are the titles closest in spirit to a "four supplier deals" menu.
- Endgame: Singularity is the strongest precedent for adding *risk* to compute acquisition beyond price: it ties compute growth to a rising, largely irreversible detection meter, which is conceptually close to (but distinct from) the prototype's neocloud "failure chance" — Endgame's risk is about getting caught, not about the deal falling through. This suggests two genuinely different risk flavors are available to the design: (a) supply risk (deal fails/slips, as in the current prototype) and (b) exposure risk (visibility/regulatory attention grows the more compute you amass, as in Endgame and echoed by AI 2027's DPA-consolidation plot beat).
- The Intelligence Rising postmortem's *absence* of a distinct compute resource is itself informative: a well-regarded, expert-designed AI-futures RPG chose to abstract hardware into a general "talent"/budget pool rather than tracking chips or datacenters explicitly. That is evidence that a serious/policy audience found generic resource abstraction sufficient for its purposes (multi-actor grand strategy), whereas the newer indie tycoons — aimed at a single-player, chip-nerd audience — chose to make hardware generations and buy/rent/timing the star mechanic. This suggests the design choice is not "more realistic vs. less realistic" but "which audience and which decision the compute mechanic is meant to dramatize" — the prototype's target (a fun, ~20-minute single-player run through 2022–2026-plus eras) sits closer to the tycoon camp.
- Scaling Laws' framing — "a shiny new GPU can be a terrible purchase six weeks before the next generation" — names a decision axis (hardware-generation timing risk) that the current four-deal prototype does not appear to include explicitly (the four deals differ by supplier type, not by whether a new chip generation is imminent). This is a candidate mechanic to borrow.
- Multiple sources (Startup Company, Software Inc.) suggest a generic "buy infrastructure outright and it's cheaper per unit but requires capital and forecasting" vs. "rent/host and it's flexible but marked-up" tradeoff recurs across unrelated genres whenever a game models server/compute capacity — this is a strong, cross-genre-validated pattern that maps directly onto the prototype's chip-maker-order vs. reserved-cloud vs. neocloud framing.

## What do players and designers say works or fails about these compute mechanics?

### Takeaway
Direct commentary was found for two titles only: Endgame: Singularity (a mix of praise for its detection-risk tension and criticism of its shallow tech-tree-plus-random-events structure) and Scaling Laws (designer's own stated design philosophy, not yet player-tested since the game is still in development). No Reddit threads, BoardGameGeek discussions, or postmortem blog posts were found for the other titles within the research budget.

### Cited Findings
- Endgame: Singularity — praise for tension: a player quoted in the JayIsGames review says "The trick is to get as much research and money under your belt as possible while you still can't be discovered," describing the buy/build-vs-stay-hidden tension as the game's core appeal — [JayIsGames review of Endgame: Singularity](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php).
- Endgame: Singularity — criticism of shallow strategic variety: the same review's overall synthesis states "gameplay consists entirely of traversing a tech tree, with random events setting you back," suggesting that once the detection-risk premise is set up, moment-to-moment play reduces to tech-tree order-of-operations rather than deep decision-making — [JayIsGames review](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php).
- Endgame: Singularity — a related difficulty-design discussion thread exists on the game's own GitHub, "Thoughts about this game's approach to difficulty" (GitHub Discussion #332), which was found in search results but ⚠️ not opened/read — flagged as a gap, since it likely contains more designer/community perspective on what does and doesn't work about the CPU/detection balance — [GitHub Discussion #332](https://github.com/singularity/singularity/discussions/332).
- Scaling Laws — the developer's own stated rationale for the central compute-timing mechanic: "A shiny new GPU can be a terrible purchase six weeks before the next generation," offered as the game's thesis rather than as retrospective player feedback (the game is still in development, per the itch.io devlog framing) — [HCK Labs / Scaling Laws, itch.io](https://hcklab.itch.io/).
- Software Inc. developers themselves consider the current server/hosting mechanic under-baked, per a planned "major overhaul of marketing, servers, and subsidiaries" to better integrate servers into the core loop — indirect designer commentary that a compute/hosting subsystem bolted onto a broader business sim can feel disconnected from the main loop if not deliberately woven in — [Software Inc. official site](https://softwareinc.coredumping.com/) via WebSearch snippet, ⚠️ not fetched directly.

### Inferences
- The one clear lesson repeated across both pieces of critical commentary found (Endgame: Singularity's review, and Software Inc.'s own dev note) is that a compute-risk mechanic needs to stay entangled with the moment-to-moment decision loop, not become a background meter the player just watches rise — Endgame's reviewer criticism is essentially that once detection risk is "set up," subsequent choices become rote; Software Inc.'s own team is proactively addressing a similar disconnection with the server system.

### Gaps
- No Steam review excerpts, Reddit threads, or BoardGameGeek discussion content were retrieved for any title (the WebSearch results surfaced page URLs like Steam Community discussion threads and a Trustpilot review page, but none were fetched for content within the tool budget).
- No postmortem or design-blog commentary was found for AI Data Center Tycoon, AI Lab Tycoon, GenAI Tycoon, or Universal Paperclips' compute subsystem specifically (Universal Paperclips has an extensive public design-commentary trail from creator Frank Lantz, but none of it was located within this research pass).
- The GitHub difficulty-discussion thread for Endgame: Singularity (#332) was found but not read; it is a likely-rich source for community/designer perspective that should be treated as an open lead, not a finding.

## Which mechanics are closest to real compute deals (multi-year contracts, prepayment, equity-for-compute, export controls, power limits)?

### Takeaway
No game found models the full real-world menu (multi-year chip-maker contracts, prepaid reserved cloud, neocloud spot risk, equity-for-compute, export controls, power limits) as explicitly and cleanly as the current four-deal prototype already does. The closest individual precedents are scattered across different games: Scaling Laws for hardware-generation timing risk, Startup Company for the buy-vs-host cost differential, Endgame: Singularity for detection/exposure risk tied to compute footprint, and AI 2027's scenario document for explicit power-draw (gigawatt) numbers and a government-compute-consolidation event. None of the sources found modeled export controls or equity-for-compute as an explicit player-facing mechanic.

### Cited Findings
- Power/gigawatt limits as an explicit modeled quantity: AI 2027's scenario forecasts specific gigawatt figures — "global AI power projections range from 60 GW by 2027" and "leading U.S. company power requirements estimated at approximately 10 GW" — the only source found that puts a real physical-power constraint into numeric form tied to an AI lab's compute buildout, matching the prototype's "gigawatt race" era name directly — [AI 2027: About](https://ai-2027.com/about).
- Government compute consolidation (an export-control/nationalization-adjacent mechanic): the AI 2027 scenario has the U.S. government "theoretically employ the Defense Production Act to consolidate trailing companies' datacenters" as a scenario branch, which is conceptually close to an "export control" or "forced pooling" event, though it is presented as narrative-scenario content for the tabletop exercise rather than a confirmed coded game mechanic — [AI 2027: About](https://ai-2027.com/about).
- Buy vs. rent/host economics with concrete numbers: Startup Company's "2 server racks = 16,800 CU for $175/day" vs. "Hosted = 15,000 CU for $900/day" is the clearest quantified buy-vs-rent tradeoff found in any title, structurally parallel to the prototype's "chip maker multi-year order" (own) vs. "reserved cloud"/"neocloud" (rent) framing, even though Startup Company's resource (generic web "Cache Units") isn't AI-specific — [Startup Company Wiki: Servers](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Servers) ⚠️ via WebSearch snippet only, page itself returned HTTP 402 on fetch.
- Hardware-generation obsolescence risk (a real dynamic in actual GPU procurement, e.g., waiting for next-gen Blackwell/Rubin-class chips) is explicitly named as the central mechanic by Scaling Laws' developer — "A shiny new GPU can be a terrible purchase six weeks before the next generation" — this is the one mechanic found that most directly mirrors a real compute-deal risk not currently present in the prototype's four-deal menu (the prototype has delay and failure-chance, but no stated "a better/cheaper deal might appear next era if you wait" dynamic) — [HCK Labs / Scaling Laws, itch.io](https://hcklab.itch.io/).
- No source found described equity-for-compute deals (e.g., real-world arrangements where a cloud provider or chipmaker takes an equity stake in exchange for compute access) or export-control mechanics (e.g., a chip ban blocking access to a supplier) as explicit player-facing game mechanics in any title researched.

### Inferences
- The prototype's existing four-deal structure (multi-year order, reserved cloud, neocloud-with-failure-chance, sovereign campus) already covers more of the real deal-structure space — prepayment, multi-year lock-in, delay, and supply risk — than any single game found in this research covers on its own; the strongest single addition suggested by the research is Scaling Laws' hardware-generation-timing tension (the risk of committing to a deal right before a better one becomes available), since no other source offers a comparably direct real-world analog for this specific dynamic.
- Gigawatt/power-limit modeling (as in AI 2027's scenario numbers) and detection/exposure risk tied to compute footprint (as in Endgame: Singularity) are the two mechanics best suited to the prototype's stated "gigawatt race" era, since both tie compute *quantity* to a second, harder-to-reverse constraint (physical power availability, or regulatory/political attention) rather than only to price and time.

### Gaps
- Equity-for-compute deals (real-world precedent: chip/cloud providers taking equity stakes in AI labs in exchange for compute access) were not found modeled in any game researched — this appears to be a genuine gap in the existing-games landscape, not just a gap in this research pass, though the research budget was not large enough to fully rule this out.
- Export-control mechanics (e.g., a supplier becoming unavailable due to national trade policy) were not found modeled in any game researched; the closest analog found is AI 2027's DPA-consolidation scenario beat, which is a domestic nationalization/forced-pooling event rather than an export-control one, and it is unclear whether it is a "hard" branch enforced in the tabletop exercise's rules or a narrative possibility raised by the scenario document.
