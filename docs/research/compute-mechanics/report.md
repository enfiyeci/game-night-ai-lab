# Turn compute deals into binding bets

The compute mechanic that best fits this game is a small set of **binding contracts**. Their size, delivery time and strings change from era to era. The player must then split the compute they own between training, serving users and safety work. Real labs do not buy compute the way you buy goods in a shop. They sign multi-year **take-or-pay** contracts, meaning they pay for reserved capacity whether or not they use it. They prepay to move up a supplier's delivery queue. They accept equity and exclusivity strings, and they watch headline deals shrink when the deals are actually signed. NVIDIA's $100B letter of intent with OpenAI turned into a reported $30B investment. The current prototype (`sim/compute.js` on branch `sim-core`) gets the cast of suppliers right. It has four gaps against reality. First, deal sizes do not scale across eras: a 15-unit deal is 15,000 GPUs, while one gigawatt holds roughly 470,000–650,000 GPUs, about 600 units. Second, it pairs "available now" with "cheap", when in reality immediate capacity is the most expensive kind. Third, contracts are paid once and are not a recurring obligation. Fourth, failure chances are flat per turn even though a turn lasts anywhere from a quarter down to a week. No existing game models the full real menu of deals. The prototype already covers more of it than any single game the researchers found. The best mechanics to borrow are Brass's loans, Power Grid's resource market, Suburbia's discount row, Frostpunk's paused construction and Endgame: Singularity's split of compute between earning and research. The recommended build is Design 1 (contract ladder) plus Design 5 (commit and split), with a light version of Design 4 (power pipeline) in era 4. Together these are roughly 9–14 hours of hackathon work. They also tie compute directly to the AI-risk story, because a lab that is paying for idle compute is under pressure to train and release faster.

## Real labs stack seven deal types, and prepayment buys the queue

By 2026 the largest labs buy compute through at least seven different structures at once, often for the same buildout. The first is on-demand, spot and reserved cloud rental. The second is the multi-year "neocloud" contract. A neocloud is a GPU-only cloud company such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda or Crusoe. The third is buying GPUs outright with a prepayment for allocation. The fourth is a hyperscaler deal that mixes cloud credits and equity; hyperscalers are the giant general clouds such as Microsoft, Google and Amazon. The fifth is a chipmaker equity or warrant deal. A warrant is a right to buy shares at a fixed price. The sixth is off-balance-sheet project financing through a special-purpose vehicle (SPV), a separate company created to own one asset and carry its debt. The seventh is debt secured by the GPUs themselves. The research note's central finding is that OpenAI holds five of these at once: a Microsoft credit-and-equity relationship, CoreWeave take-or-pay contracts, an Oracle cloud contract, NVIDIA and AMD equity deals, and a Broadcom custom-chip deal. A game that makes the player pick one "supplier type" misses this. Real play is stacking.

**The neocloud contract is the backbone.** Customers commit to fixed capacity at fixed rates for 2–5 years and pay regardless of usage. The neocloud then borrows against that contracted revenue to buy the GPUs ([Solvimon](https://www.solvimon.com/blog/neoclouds-owe-customers-years-of-compute)) ⚠️ industry-blog level, not a specific contract. CoreWeave reportedly draws **96% of its revenue from long-term take-or-pay contracts**. Its contracted backlog reportedly reached **$99.4B by 31 March 2026, up from $66.8B at the end of 2025** ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/23/best-gpu-neoclouds-2026/)) ⚠️ an aggregator restating CoreWeave's filings; the filings were not fetched. The most detailed public contract is Microsoft's deal with Nebius. It is worth **at least $17.4B and up to $19.4B over five years, through 2031** ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-08/microsoft-signs-nebius-cloud-deal-for-as-much-as-19-4-billion)). Capacity arrives in **nine tranches (delivery batches) during 2025–2026** from a New Jersey site ([Redmondmag](https://redmondmag.com/articles/2025/09/10/microsoft-strikes-billion-deal-with-nebius.aspx)). The contract includes about **$6.96B of upfront payments, roughly 40% of total value**. Payments are due regardless of use, but Microsoft can claim service credits or cancel individual tranches if Nebius misses delivery or uptime targets ([TheEnergyMag](https://theenergymag.com/news/2026-05-04/microsoft-nebius-ai)) ⚠️ the $6.96B upfront figure comes from a single aggregator and was not cross-checked. That gives a template with three parts: a delivery schedule rather than one lump, an upfront share separate from total value, and remedies for the buyer when the supplier slips.

**Prepayment is the single most consistent lever for jumping the queue.** NVIDIA's CEO publicly describes allocation as first-come, first-served rather than highest-bidder ([Digitimes](https://www.digitimes.com/news/a20260428PD221/nvidia-jensen-huang-gpu-demand-2026.html)). In practice, industry blogs describe "Multi-Year Purchase Agreements" (MYPAs). These reportedly guarantee **15–25% more allocation than standard quotas in exchange for 2–3 year volume commitments and 10–15% upfront prepayment** ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-shortage-2026/)) ⚠️ these percentages trace only to industry-blog aggregators in every research pass; no primary NVIDIA contract or filing describing MYPA terms was found, so treat them as unconfirmed. The same sources describe 2–3 year reserved cloud terms as typically requiring **about 20% of contract value upfront** ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-cluster-reservation-contract-negotiation-2026/)) ⚠️ generic aggregator guidance, not one documented contract. Hyperscalers reportedly placed forward Blackwell orders in 2025 that used up most of NVIDIA's allocation through 2026 and into 2027. Short-term and spot contracts became secondary and could be pre-empted by prepaid long-term ones ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-shortage-2026/)) ⚠️ same aggregator sourcing. Nebius reportedly described its 2026 capacity as sold out, with **more than four customers competing for each GPU tranche** ([Luminix](https://lumienai.com/news/best-gpu-neoclouds-2026-coreweave-nebius-lambda-crusoe-groq-ranked)). There are two layers here. The visible rule is fairness. The rule that actually decides outcomes is who can afford to prepay years ahead. A game can show both.

**Equity-for-compute deals are real, large and circular.** In October 2025 Microsoft and OpenAI restructured their partnership. Microsoft's stake is now **about 27%, valued at roughly $135B**. OpenAI committed to buy **an incremental $250B of Azure services**. In exchange, **Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal on OpenAI's compute**, which had meant Microsoft got the first chance to supply any new compute OpenAI wanted. Microsoft kept exclusivity on OpenAI's API products ([Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2025/10/28/the-next-chapter-of-the-microsoft-openai-partnership/)). The trade is exclusivity for committed spend. AMD gave OpenAI a warrant for up to **160 million AMD shares at $0.01 each**, expiring 5 October 2030. The filing describes the warrant as an inducement for OpenAI's GPU purchase commitment ([AMD 8-K Exhibit 4.1](https://ir.amd.com/financial-information/sec-filings/content/0001193125-25-230895/d28189dex41.htm)). ⚠️ The vesting milestones are redacted in the public filing itself. The reported tranches (first after 1 GW of MI450 deliveries, full vesting at 6 GW plus AMD share-price targets) come only from press statements ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/06/openai-amd-chip-deal-ai.html)). Google agreed to invest **up to $40B in Anthropic** in cash and compute ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/24/google-to-invest-up-to-40b-in-anthropic/)). Separately, the compute side of that relationship was reported at **$200B over five years** ([Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/blog/anthropic-200-billion-google-cloud-five-year-commitment-may-5)) ⚠️ an aggregator figure not checked against a primary statement; whether it is binding take-or-pay, a ceiling or a projection is unknown. For game design, "take investment" and "buy compute" are two separate transactions with separate terms, even between the same two companies.

**Headlines and signed deals are different numbers.** NVIDIA's September 2025 announcement was a non-binding letter of intent. It said NVIDIA would invest **up to $100B progressively as each gigawatt of at least 10 GW is deployed**, starting on Vera Rubin hardware in the second half of 2026. The press release gave no equity terms ([NVIDIA](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/openai-and-nvidia-announce-strategic-partnership-to-deploy-10gw-of-nvidia-systems)). The investment actually finalized in OpenAI's roughly $110B round was reported at **$30B**. NVIDIA's CEO then signaled this was likely the last large equity check into OpenAI or Anthropic, because both were expected to go public ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/04/jensen-huang-says-nvidia-is-pulling-back-from-openai-and-anthropic-but-his-explanation-raises-more-questions-than-it-answers/)) ⚠️ carried over from the earlier notes, sourced to TechCrunch's summary of a Financial Times report, and not re-verified against an NVIDIA filing. MIT Sloan's Michael Cusumano called the arrangement roughly a wash, since the investment and the chip purchases offset each other (same source). Amazon shows a similar gap. Reports say it has invested $8B in Anthropic since 2024, while the headline of Amazon's own release frames a $5B investment plus up to $20B more ([About Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-invests-additional-5-billion-anthropic-ai)) ⚠️ the two framings were not reconciled in research.

**Big builds are financed off the lab's balance sheet.** Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana is funded through a joint venture with Blue Owl. Blue Owl owns 80% and Meta 20%. The package is **$27B of debt plus $2.5B of equity**, arranged through an SPV so the debt stays off Meta's books ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/national/news/data-center-capital-markets/meta-pushes-its-largest-data-center-project-off-its-books-with-27b-joint-venture-131490)). The debt matures in 2049 and is rated A+ ([PE Insights](https://pe-insights.com/blue-owl-and-meta-close-record-30bn-financing-for-ai-data-centre-expansion-in-louisiana/)). For everyday capacity, Meta separately signed a conventional **six-year, $10B+ cloud contract with Google** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/21/google-scores-six-year-meta-cloud-deal-worth-over-10-billion.html)). xAI's reported Colossus 2 financing is a **$20B lease-to-own SPV, about $7.5B equity and $12.5B debt**. The SPV owns the GPUs and leases them to xAI over five years, and NVIDIA reportedly put in up to $2B of the equity ([DataCenterDynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/xai-seeks-12bn-in-debt-to-fund-colossus-2-data-center-with-first-chips-online-in-a-few-weeks/)). Sources disagree on whether this deal had closed by September 2026. Neoclouds finance themselves the same way. CoreWeave's delayed-draw loans (credit lines drawn in pieces as needed) are priced at **SOFR plus 4.5% to 5.5%**. SOFR is the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the benchmark US rate that loans are priced above. The loans require a minimum **1.35x debt-service coverage ratio**, meaning cash flow must be at least 1.35 times the loan payments. They are secured by both the GPUs and the customer contracts ([SEC exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1769628/000176962826000357/ex991pr.htm); [CoreWeave IR](https://investors.coreweave.com/news/news-details/2026/CoreWeave-Closes-Landmark-8-5-Billion-Financing-Facility-Achieving-First-Investment-Grade-Rated-GPU-backed-Financing/default.aspx)) ⚠️ these filings were surfaced by search but not fetched; every rate and covenant figure comes from a search tool's summary. In short, a neocloud's creditworthiness is really a bet on its customers.

**Deals go wrong mostly because the buyer's forecast changes.** In March 2026 Oracle and OpenAI dropped a planned **600 MW expansion of the Abilene, Texas Stargate site**, from 1.2 GW toward about 2.0 GW. Reports blamed a financing dispute and OpenAI's frequently changing demand forecasts. The existing 1.2 GW campus kept running ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-06/oracle-and-openai-end-plans-to-expand-flagship-data-center); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/oracle-and-openai-scrap-planned-600mw-abilene-expansion)) ⚠️ Bloomberg's own article was not fetched; the summary came via Tom's Hardware and DataCenterDynamics. The earlier background note, citing Epoch AI, described the cancelled expansion as 2.1 GW, so the exact size of the cut is uncertain. A year earlier, analysts at TD Cowen reported that Microsoft had walked away from about **2 GW of pending data-center leases** in six months. They tied it largely to a decision not to support more OpenAI training ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/national/news/data-center/microsoft-is-stepping-away-from-more-than-2gw-of-data-center-capacity-128681)). The research found no documented full cancellation of a signed contract. Every case was a partial scope cut or a renegotiation. Analysts at J.P. Morgan warn that suppliers' revenue, debt and equity all trace back to the same few loss-making labs, which concentrates risk ([J.P. Morgan AM](https://am.jpmorgan.com/us/en/asset-management/adv/insights/market-insights/market-updates/on-the-minds-of-investors/does-circularity-in-ai-deals-warn-of-a-bubble/)). The Bank for International Settlements reportedly lists this among its top three financial-stability risks ([Columbia blog](https://blogs.cuit.columbia.edu/gjb2124/circular-financing/)) ⚠️ the BIS statement itself was not fetched; this comes via a secondary academic blog.

**One internal compute promise shows why pledges need teeth.** In July 2023 OpenAI pledged 20% of the compute it had secured to date, over four years, to its Superalignment team ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-superalignment/)) ⚠️ the announcement page was captured via search synthesis, not fetched. Fortune reported that the team's requests for more GPUs were repeatedly refused. The pledge never had clear terms, and the team was disbanded within a year ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/05/21/openai-superalignment-20-compute-commitment-never-fulfilled-sutskever-leike-altman-brockman-murati)) ⚠️ Fortune's article was not fetched directly. The spec already has a safety-compute pledge in `state.promises`, and a Head of Safety who can quit publicly when it is broken. This is the real precedent for that mechanic, and it belongs inside the compute system.

## Physical numbers put one gigawatt at about 600 game units

The prototype's unit is 1,000 top GPUs at $2 per GPU-hour, or $1.46M per unit per month. That unit holds up well against real numbers. The problem is that real fleets grow by about three orders of magnitude across the game's five eras, and the prototype's deal sizes do not grow at all.

**Scale grows about 4–5x per year while cost grows about 2.5x per year.** Epoch AI measured frontier training compute growing **about 4–5x per year**, doubling about every 5.2 months for frontier language models since 2020 ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/training-compute-of-frontier-ai-models-grows-by-4-5x-per-year)). The cost of the final training run grew more slowly, **about 2.4x per year** on amortized hardware or 2.6x per year on cloud-rental pricing. Cloud-rental estimates run about twice the amortized ones ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models)). Dividing the two growth rates gives roughly 1.7–1.9x more compute per dollar each year. So each era's unit should buy more capability than the last, even as absolute spending climbs. Epoch projects the largest runs will pass **$1B by 2027** (same source). Named cluster sizes from Epoch are GPT-3 at about 10,000 GPUs, GPT-4 at about 25,000 GPUs, Gemini 1.0 Ultra at 57,000 TPUs and Llama 3.1 405B at 16,384 H100s ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/training-cluster-size)). GPT-4's run is estimated at about $80M and Llama 3.1 405B at about $170M ([Epoch data](https://epoch.ai/data/frontier_ai_models.csv)) ⚠️ both dollar figures come from a search engine's summary of Epoch's dataset, not a direct read. Runs in 2026 are estimated at $200–500M and 1e26–1e27 FLOP ([deluair](https://deluair.com/consultancy/insights/frontier-ai-training-cost-2026)) ⚠️ a secondary aggregator, not confirmed against Epoch.

**One gigawatt costs about $38B to build and about $8.5B a year to own.** Epoch's model of a 1 GW campus built on GB200 NVL72 racks puts **upfront capital at about $38B**. Servers are $21.2B of that, the facility $11.4B and networking $4.9B. It puts **total cost of ownership at about $8.5B per year**. Servers are 60% of the annual cost and **energy only 7%** ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-datacenter-cost-breakdown)). Other estimates run from Bernstein's $35B up to NVIDIA's own $50–60B per gigawatt ([Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/how-much-does-a-gw-of-data-center-capacity-actually-cost-4314046)). A GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 GPUs and draws about **120–132 kW** in practice ([Introl](https://introl.com/blog/gb200-nvl72-deployment-72-gpu-liquid-cooled)). That works out to roughly **576–648 GPUs per megawatt** of computing load, or about **470,000–650,000 GPUs per gigawatt** ([rax.ae](https://rax.ae/knowledge-center/articles/nvidia-blackwell-gb200-nvl72-data-center-guide.html); [amcompute](https://www.amcompute.com/blog/the-power-budget-of-an-ai-data-center)) ⚠️ both are calculations from vendor rack specs in blogs, not company-disclosed figures. In game units, **one gigawatt is about 470–650 units, call it 600**. One unit draws about 1.5–2 MW. xAI's Colossus is the real reference point. By mid-September 2026 it carried **about 1.29 GW of computing load across roughly 670,000 accelerators**, with about **$48.7B** of modeled capital ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/xais-colossus-2-first-gigawatt-datacenter)). That is about 670 game units.

**The $2 base price is the cost of owning, not renting.** Dividing Epoch's $8.5B yearly cost by 470,000–650,000 GPUs gives an owner's cost of about **$1.50–2.07 per GPU-hour**. That is our derivation from the figures above, not a published number. Rental prices sit well above it. The median H100 on-demand price across about 40 providers was **$3.44 per GPU-hour on 25 September 2026, with a range of $1.49–$6.98** ([GPUPerHour](https://gpuperhour.com/gpu-price-index)) ⚠️ search synthesis of a price index. Another snapshot put the median at $3.25, with CoreWeave at $4.25 and hyperscalers up to $6.98 ([Shattered.io](https://shattered.io/h100-h200-b200-cloud-gpu-pricing-2026/)) ⚠️ not re-verified; aggregator level. H100 rental fell from **$8–10 per hour in 2024 to $1.80–3.50 by mid-2026**, helped by a 44% AWS price cut in June 2025 ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/is-the-ai-chip-shortage-over-in-2026-gpu-pricing-and-what-comes-next)) ⚠️ aggregator level, not checked against AWS's pricing page. B200 ranges from **$2.12 on spot and $2.25 on a 36-month reservation, through $3.99–5.50 on-demand at specialists, to about $14.24 at AWS list price** ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/data-center-gpu-pricing-2026)) ⚠️ aggregator level. The spread reflects contract type, not data error. The pattern is that locking in is cheap and getting capacity immediately is expensive. The prototype inverts this. Its CoreFlame neocloud is available immediately at 0.9x the base price, and its Azuria reserved cloud costs 1.25x. In reality, a reserved B200 costs about 1.1x the owner's cost, while on-demand costs 2–3.5x.

**Lead times split into fast chips and very slow power.** H100 server lead times were **36–52 weeks in 2023** and fell to **2–3 months in 2024** ([TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/97478/nvidia-h100-ai-gpu-lead-times-improve-4-month-wait-is-now-2-3/index.html); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/nvidias-h100-ai-gpu-shortages-ease-as-lead-times-drop-from-up-to-four-months-to-8-12-weeks)). In 2026, new or non-priority buyers of B200 reportedly waited **12–18 months** for volume orders, while buyers with existing supplier relationships waited 8–16 weeks ([GPUaaS](https://gpuaas.com/blog/b200-gpu-availability-report-q2-2026)) ⚠️ aggregator level. The packaging and memory layer sits beneath the chips. CoWoS is TSMC's advanced packaging, which bonds the GPU die and its memory stacks onto one base. CoWoS-packaged parts ran **52–78 week lead times in mid-2026**, with NVIDIA holding about 60% of capacity ([ClusterBid](https://clusterbid.com/blog/gpu-supply-chain-2026-tsmc-cowos-nvidia-allocation-lead-times)). HBM is high-bandwidth memory, the stacked memory chips mounted next to the GPU. It was **sold out through 2026** at SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung ([Astute Group](https://www.astutegroup.com/news/general/sk-hynix-holds-62-of-hbm-micron-overtakes-samsung-2026-battle-pivots-to-hbm4/)). SemiAnalysis reports the main 2026 constraint moved again, to TSMC's 3nm wafers ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/the-great-ai-silicon-shortage)). Power equipment is far slower. Large power transformers averaged **128 weeks** and generator step-up transformers **144 weeks** ([IndustrialSage](https://www.industrialsage.com/power-transformer-lead-times-us-grid-shortage/)) ⚠️ trade press; the underlying Wood Mackenzie survey was not fetched. The median time to connect a new project to the US grid has doubled to **more than five years** ([ATK Energy](https://atkenergygroup.com/blog/grid-interconnection-data-centers/)). ERCOT, the Texas grid operator, held about **238 GW** of large-load requests and converted only a low single-digit percentage of them into operating projects ([EnkiAI](https://enkiai.com/data-center/ai-data-center-energy-2026-2600-gw-queue-pjm-plan/)). GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog grew **from 100 GW to 116 GW** in one quarter of 2026 ([Turbomachinery](https://www.turbomachinerymag.com/view/ge-vernova-gas-turbine-backlog-hits-116-gw-as-power-orders-more-than-double)) ⚠️ trade-press citation of GE Vernova's filings, not a direct read. The fastest real build is xAI's first Colossus phase: **100,000 GPUs in 122 days**, where NVIDIA's CEO said such projects typically take about four years ([NVIDIA](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/spectrum-x-ethernet-networking-xai-colossus)). It then doubled to 200,000 GPUs in about 92 more days ([HPCwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/2025/05/13/colossus-ai-hits-200000-gpus-as-musk-ramps-up-ai-ambitions/)). xAI did it with on-site gas turbines instead of waiting for the grid.

**Local politics is now a second constraint on power.** A tracker counts **$64B** of US data-center projects blocked or delayed, **$68B more in Q2 2026 alone**, and 843 opposition groups ([Data Center Watch](https://www.datacenterwatch.org/report)). One Stargate site faced documented opposition. Another town banned future data centers ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/openai-stargate-where-the-us-sites-stand)). Because energy is only about 7% of yearly cost, the power-source choice in a game should mostly trade **speed against politics**, not against price.

**Reliability, efficiency and serving round out the picture.** Meta's Llama 3 run on 16,384 H100s had **466 interruptions in 54 days, about one every three hours; 419 were unexpected** and most were GPU- or HBM-related. Automated recovery still kept **goodput above 90%**. Goodput is the share of cluster time spent doing useful training after failures and restarts ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/faulty-nvidia-h100-gpus-and-hbm3-memory-caused-half-of-the-failures-during-llama-3-training-one-failure-every-three-hours-for-metas-16384-gpu-training-cluster)) ⚠️ a tech-press summary of Meta's paper; the paper itself was not fetched. PUE is power usage effectiveness: total facility power divided by the power that reaches the computers, where 1.0 means no overhead. The global average PUE has stayed near **1.56** ([Infineon](https://community.infineon.com/t5/Blogs/What-is-power-usage-effectiveness-PUE-in-data-centers-Overview-limitations-and/ba-p/1203256)) ⚠️ vendor-blog summary, not the primary Uptime Institute survey. Google's fleet runs at **1.09** ([Google](https://datacenters.google/efficiency/)). Inference (serving users) reportedly grew from **about one third of AI compute in 2023 to about half in 2025 and two thirds in 2026** ([AgentMarketCap citing Deloitte](https://agentmarketcap.ai/blog/2026/04/17/ai-inference-overtakes-training-two-thirds-2026-compute)) ⚠️ an analyst projection relayed by an aggregator; this is the weakest-sourced number in the whole research. OpenAI's 2024 compute reportedly split into **about $3B training, $1.8B inference and $1B research**, out of roughly $7B in total ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/openai-compute-spend)) ⚠️ search synthesis of an Epoch page, not fetched directly. That split implies the final training run of shipped models is a minority of what a lab spends on compute.

**Export policy flips every 6–12 months.** The United States restricted China chip exports in October 2022. In October 2023 it tightened the rules with a "performance density" test that stopped buyers from combining many smaller chips to match a banned one ([CSET](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/bis-2023-update-explainer/)). H20 exports were halted in April 2025 and quietly allowed again in July 2025 ([Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/trump-lifts-ai-chip-ban-china-nvidia)). A January 2026 rule then allowed case-by-case H200 licenses with a 25% tariff ([BIS](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-revises-license-review-policy-semiconductors-exported-china)). Gulf buyers depend on US-brokered approval. Stargate UAE targets 1 GW, with the first 200 MW online in 2026 ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/ai-arrives-in-the-middle-east-us-strikes-a-deal-with-uae-and-ksa)). Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN has committed about $100B across 2.2 GW ([explainx](https://www.explainx.ai/blog/uae-saudi-arabia-ai-landscape-g42-humain-2026)). The prototype's Gulf deal costs US favor. In reality, access is **gated by** US favor. The deal needs a license the US can grant or revoke.

## No game models the full deal menu, but six mechanics transfer cleanly

The researchers found about a dozen AI-themed games and serious games. None of them models the full real menu of multi-year contracts, prepayment, equity-for-compute, export controls and power limits. The prototype's four suppliers already cover more of that menu than any single title. No game was found that treats equity-for-compute or export controls as a player-facing mechanic. That is a gap in existing games, and something this game can own.

**AI-lab tycoons dramatize timing and rent-versus-own.** Scaling Laws is an itch.io tycoon still in development. It starts in 2022 and lets you rent or own compute and time hardware generations. Its developer's thesis names the tension the prototype lacks: "A shiny new GPU can be a terrible purchase six weeks before the next generation" ([HCK Labs](https://hcklab.itch.io/)). ⚠️ This is a marketing and devlog page with no unit numbers, and the game may change before release. AI Data Center Tycoon is a clicker with six eras running from garage to hyperscale. Its hardware ranges from secondhand gaming cards to custom silicon ([PurpleJelly](https://purplejelly.itch.io/ai-data-center-tycoon)). It shows an era structure close to ours, but its click-to-generate loop is what to avoid if the goal is deliberate choices. AI Lab Tycoon separates designing chips from securing compute ([aitycoon.org](https://aitycoon.org/)) ⚠️ only its one-line search description could be read; the page did not render for the fetch tool. Startup Company gives the clearest buy-versus-host numbers found in any game. Two owned racks produce 16,800 capacity units for $175 a day, while hosted capacity costs $900 a day for 15,000 units ([Startup Company Wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Servers)) ⚠️ the wiki returned HTTP 402; figures are from a search snippet. Software Inc. adds cooling limits to server rooms, but its own developers plan to overhaul servers because they sit apart from the core loop ([Software Inc.](https://softwareinc.coredumping.com/)) ⚠️ snippet only.

**Two older games show that compute risk must stay in the decision loop.** In Endgame: Singularity the player is an emergent AI. CPU earns money and money buys CPU ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame:_Singularity)) ⚠️ the article returned 404; this comes from a search snippet. CPU must be split between research and income-earning jobs, and one acquisition route is stealing computer time. The real limit is a detection meter that is hard to lower once it rises. The JayIsGames reviewer praised the race to build up before discovery. The same review faulted the game as a tech tree with random setbacks once the premise is set ([JayIsGames](https://jayisgames.com/review/endgame-singularity.php)). Universal Paperclips pairs processors, which set how fast operations accumulate, with memory, which sets how many can be stored. Its late "Trust" resource gates new compute slots ([Paperclips Wiki](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Processors); [TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/UniversalPaperclips)) ⚠️ the wiki returned HTTP 402 and the Trust claim is TV Tropes' lower-confidence summary. The lesson from both games is that a compute risk becomes dull when it turns into a background meter the player only watches.

**Serious games mostly abstract compute away.** In Intelligence Rising, a participant's write-up lists hard power, soft power, budget, cyber and AI talent, but no compute resource ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ywKhqjgdKuHoQohYL/takeaways-from-the-intelligence-rising-rpg)). ⚠️ The academic paper on the game could not be read because the PDF did not extract. The AI 2027 scenario behind the AI Futures tabletop exercise gives the most concrete numbers. It projects about 100M H100-equivalents globally and about 60 GW of AI power in 2027, about 10 GW for the leading US company, and a possible Defense Production Act consolidation of trailing labs' data centers ([AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/about)). ⚠️ The exercise's own rules were not found, so it is unknown whether compute is a live number during play. The Defense Production Act consolidation fits era 5 directly.

**Board games supply the mechanics themselves.** Power Grid's resource market is a price ladder. Buyers take the cheapest open slot, and the market refills from the most expensive empty slots downward, so heavy demand raises future prices without any bidding ([BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/blogpost/62868/power-grid-resource-market)) ⚠️ the page returned HTTP 403; the rule is reconstructed from several search snippets. Power Grid also allows spite bids that drain a rival's cash ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/power-grid/)). Suburbia's tile row slides each tile one slot cheaper every turn, so waiting saves money but a rival may take the tile first ([UltraBoardGames](https://ultraboardgames.com/suburbia/game-rules.php)) ⚠️ search synthesis only. Century: Spice Road charges by position in a row and pays bonuses on cards others passed over ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century:_Spice_Road)) ⚠️ search synthesis only. Brass: Birmingham's loans give £30 now and permanently lower income, so a loan taken early, when income is low anyway, costs the least ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass_(board_game))) ⚠️ search synthesis only. This is the cleanest analogue for a take-or-pay contract. In 18xx games, buying a new train type makes the oldest type disappear from every company that holds it, with no refund ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX)) ⚠️ the article states the basic rule; the strategic framing comes from secondary sources. Frostpunk keeps unfinished buildings as a scaffold that can be paused and refunded ([Frostpunk Wiki](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Advanced_Tips)) ⚠️ search synthesis only. Terraforming Mars pays out production gains in the phase after you buy them ([Dized](https://rules.dized.com/game/Y2e4M8-fRH-fyWwjwVHsbw/DP-DSHzDS2u5nwrXRHm6Lg/production-phase)) ⚠️ search synthesis only. Container runs goods through blind auctions whose prices other players set ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Container_(board_game))). Offworld Trading Company makes the final five-share buyout a single visible move, which gives the target one turn to defend ([Designer Notes](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)). The one designer source read in full is Soren Johnson's note on that system. He argues that an economy exists to produce a few clearly signalled, high-stakes choices, not to be maximally realistic. The lesson for this game is to build compute around two to four recurring dilemmas and make each one visible and costly.

## Realism comes from obligations, queues and moving bottlenecks, not more suppliers

Six changes would make the prototype's compute system match reality without adding much user interface. Each one rests on the evidence above.

**Scale deal sizes by era.** Frontier clusters grew from about 25 units (GPT-4) to about 670 units (Colossus in 2026). A fixed 6–15 unit menu makes era 4's "gigawatt race" a label rather than a decision. Deals should scale roughly 3–5x per era. Grid-connected power sites should become a card type in era 4 and should have been ordered in era 2 or 3.

**Make contracts recurring and binding.** Take-or-pay means monthly charges continue whether the compute is used or not. Real contract terms run 2–6 years, and the whole game spans only about 33 months (24 in eras 1–2, 8 in eras 3–4, 1 in era 5). So a contract signed in era 3 or later can simply run to the end of the game. The upfront share should be a separate number. Real values run from about 10–15% for chip orders, through about 20% for reserved cloud, to about 40% in the Nebius case, all ⚠️ aggregator-sourced as noted above. The prototype's three months prepaid on a two-year Verde order comes to about 12% of term value. That falls inside the real range.

**Put the price on speed, not on the supplier's name.** On the real price ladder, owning costs about 1.0x the base, a long reservation about 1.1x, neocloud on-demand about 2x and hyperscaler on-demand up to about 3.5x, with list prices higher still. The prototype's `spotPremium: 1.5` is on the low side. CoreFlame's "available now at 0.9x" should become either "reserved at 1.0x, arrives next turn" or "now at 2x".

**Let prepayment buy queue position, and let the bottleneck move.** Real bottlenecks moved from chips to packaging to wafers and HBM, and then to power. That matches the spec's bottleneck cards almost exactly. The bottleneck card should change which deals are slow and which can be pre-empted, not just add a fixed delay to all of them (`BOTTLENECK_DELAY` currently does the latter).

**Scale failure chances to turn length.** A flat 6% chance per turn equals about 2% per month in quarter-length eras, 6% per month in month-length eras and about 23% per month in week-length era 5. Chances should be stored per month and converted to each era's turn length. The research found no base rate for neocloud failure. The realistic trigger is the neocloud's own customers or its debt covenants breaking, so failure is best tied to events rather than a flat roll.

**Model the gap between headline and signed deals, and the strings.** An announcement card could deliver 30–100% of its headline value. The NVIDIA case landed at 30%. Equity deals should show a lower "true net value" because the money comes back to the investor. Exclusivity should block other suppliers until it is bought out, as Microsoft's right of first refusal did until 2025.

The AI-risk link runs through all six. Take-or-pay turns money pressure into race pressure: idle compute costs cash, so the player is pushed to train and ship sooner. The safety-compute pledge is the one commitment with no contract behind it. That is why it is tempting to break, and why breaking it should be visible. Export-control flips and Defense Production Act-style pooling put governments inside the compute market, which is where compute governance actually operates.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which supplier is best" to "which obligation can you afford to carry." Real compute is gathered through commitments that outlast the reason they were signed. Microsoft's 2 GW walk-back and the Abilene cut show that the costly failure is usually the buyer misjudging its own demand, not the supplier vanishing. That is good news for a 20-minute game. The drama comes from the player's own forecasts, and the player can see and act on them. A random supplier-failure roll, by contrast, is out of the player's hands.

The second insight is about scale and time. Real fleets grow roughly 25-fold between GPT-4 and a gigawatt campus, while the game's clock shrinks turns from quarters to weeks. Together these mean that by era 5 nothing new can be built in time. The last era's compute question is therefore no longer about buying. It is about who controls the capacity that already exists. That is exactly where the Defense Production Act pooling beat, pacing deals and the safety pledge belong, and it ties the compute system to the game's AI-risk endings without any new resource.

### Design 1: contract ladder (upgrade the current menu)

**How a turn plays.** The player uses one action slot to sign one contract from four or five supplier cards. Each card shows five things: size, arrival turn, upfront cost, monthly charge and one string. Signed contracts appear in a "commitments" strip that shows the total monthly obligation. Charges continue from arrival to the end of the term whether the compute is used or not. Exiting early forfeits the upfront payment and costs a relationship penalty. **Real dynamics.** Take-or-pay, a separate upfront share, delivery tranches, hyperscaler exclusivity (Microsoft's right of first refusal), neoclouds that are cheap but fragile, sovereign capacity gated by the US, and equity deals that come back to the investor. **Borrowed from.** Brass: Birmingham's loans, where a commitment permanently taxes income, and Startup Company's own-versus-host prices. **What makes it fun.** Every card is readable in two seconds. The tension is visible: big cheap commitments now against flexibility later. A late-game "commitments" bar the player cannot shrink is the Brass feeling in AI clothing. **Build cost.** About 3–5 hours: extend `SUPPLIERS` with per-era size, term and string fields, add recurring charges and an exit cost, and add one card component.

### Design 2: allocation queue (prepay to move up)

**How a turn plays.** Each era, the bottleneck supplier releases a fixed number of units per turn into a visible queue. The player and three rival labs each hold a tier. Tier A (prepaid) gets first claim plus a bonus. Tier B (standard) shares pro rata. Spot buyers take leftovers and can be bumped. The player's move is to hold, prepay to move up a tier, or buy spot at the current ladder price. Buying from the ladder raises the next turn's price, and the ladder refills from the top each turn. A rival's prepayment is shown one turn before it lands. **Real dynamics.** First-come, first-served as the public rule, with the reality that prepayment wins. MYPA-style terms of 10–15% prepaid for 15–25% extra allocation ⚠️ unconfirmed. More than four customers per Nebius tranche. Hyperscalers locking up Blackwell supply through 2027. Spot being pre-empted. **Borrowed from.** Power Grid's resource ladder ⚠️ rule reconstructed from snippets, and Offworld Trading Company's signalled final move. **What makes it fun.** The player reads rivals' appetite and can get squeezed or outbid in plain view. It also feeds race heat, because every rival grab is visible. **Build cost.** About 7–10 hours: rival demand logic, queue resolution, the ladder and a new UI panel. This is the riskiest design for 36 hours unless rival logic already exists.

### Design 3: deal row (offers that get cheaper, until a rival takes them)

**How a turn plays.** Five deal cards sit in a row with price slots of 1.3x, 1.15x, 1.0x, 0.9x and 0.8x. Each turn, unsigned cards slide one slot cheaper. A rival takes one card (two in the bottleneck-heavy eras 3 and 5), and a new card enters at the expensive end. A "next generation" warning card appears one turn before a new chip generation. When the generation arrives, older-generation cards drop about 40% in price, and older owned units count for less capability. Letter-of-intent cards show a headline that resolves to 30–100% when signed. **Real dynamics.** Sold-out tranches. H100 rental falling from $8–10 to $1.80–3.50 after Blackwell ⚠️. Hardware-generation timing. Headline versus signed deals. **Borrowed from.** Suburbia and Century: Spice Road ⚠️ both from snippets, Scaling Laws' timing thesis, and a soft version of 18xx rusting. **What makes it fun.** It is a wait-or-grab gamble every turn, with high replay value because the card draw changes each run. It is also the most natural user interface for Design 1's cards. **Build cost.** About 4–6 hours on top of Design 1's card data: a row, a slide step, a rival pick and a generation event.

### Design 4: power pipeline (era 4 layer, seeded in eras 2–3)

**How a turn plays.** From era 2 the player can "reserve a site", which is cheap and slow. In era 4, sites turn into construction projects shown as scaffolds. Each site picks a power source. Grid power is cheapest and cleanest but takes about 6 or more turns, so it must be started in era 2 or 3. On-site gas takes 4 turns and costs public trust, and it raises the chance of local opposition. A nuclear restart is slow and prone to slipping, but reliable, and it gains trust. The Gulf campus is fast but depends on US favor. Opposition events can pause a scaffold, with no refund lost while it waits, or cut about 30% of a site's scope, echoing Abilene. Every site shows its announced capacity next to what is actually online. **Real dynamics.** Transformer lead times of 128–144 weeks ⚠️. Grid connection over five years. Colossus's 122-day gas build. $64–68B of projects blocked. Nuclear restarts slipping ⚠️, with Palisades' delay characterized by an advocacy group ([NucNet](https://www.nucnet.org/news/palisades-nuclear-plant-returns-to-operational-status-and-ready-to-receive-fuel-8-3-2025); [Sierra Club](https://www.sierraclub.org/michigan/blog/2026/03/issues-continue-pile-palisades-nuclear-plant-restart)). Energy is only about 7% of yearly cost, so the power choice trades speed against politics. **Borrowed from.** Frostpunk's pausable scaffold ⚠️ from snippets, and Terraforming Mars' delayed payout ⚠️ from snippets. **What makes it fun.** Early foresight pays off late, and era 4 feels physically different from eras 1–3. It also delivers the spec's "local data-center opposition" event and the feed reactions. **Build cost.** About 3–4 hours for a light version (three site types, one opposition event, a scaffold state) and about 6–8 hours for the full version with sites reserved in era 2.

### Design 5: commit and split (forecast, serve, and keep the safety pledge)

**How a turn plays.** A three-way slider splits online compute between training, serving and safety or evaluations. Serving has a floor set by the player's user base and the era. If the floor is not met, users see outages and the player must buy spot at 2–3.5x. Unused committed compute is still billed. The player can resell it on spot at a 30–50% loss, or walk away from a contract by forfeiting its prepayment. If the player has made a safety-compute pledge, the safety share is checked against it every turn. Breaking it is silent at first. Then the spec's warning-then-card flow brings staff questions, and finally the Head of Safety may quit publicly. In era 5 a government "pooling" card offers to take a share of the player's compute in exchange for favor and pacing credit, modeled on the Defense Production Act beat in AI 2027. **Real dynamics.** Inference rising from one third to two thirds of compute ⚠️. OpenAI's 2024 split of roughly $3B training, $1.8B inference and $1B research ⚠️. The Superalignment pledge that went unmet ⚠️. Microsoft's walk-back and the Abilene cut, both driven by buyers' changing forecasts. **Borrowed from.** Endgame: Singularity's split of CPU between earning jobs and research, and Universal Paperclips' pairing of flow and capacity. **What makes it fun and relevant.** The player feels the squeeze a real lab feels. A hit model starves the next training run, and the cheapest compute to cut is the safety share. This is the design that most directly serves the 40% AI-risk judging weight. **Build cost.** About 3–5 hours. It reuses the budget screen and `state.promises`, and adds the slider, the serving floor and resale.

| 2 | 40% | same | 70% | investor asks you to drop the pledge |
| 3 | 50% | same | 60% | agent launch doubles serving demand for 2 turns |
| 4 | 65% | same | 50% | humanoid line adds serving load |

### Recommended combination

Build Design 1 as the spine, because it is mostly an upgrade of code that already exists. Add Design 5 next, because it is cheap and does the most for AI-risk relevance. If time allows, add Design 4 light for era 4. Together these come to roughly 9–14 hours by my estimate.
