# How Strategy/Narrative Games Format Dilemma and Event Cards

## Reigns: the binary swipe card and the hover-preview convention

### Takeaway
Reigns runs on one card at a time: an advisor/character portrait fills the card, a short line of dialogue is the only body text, and there are exactly two choices — swipe left or right, no menu of options and no numeric costs ever shown. Design confirmation of the specific "hover shows which meters move but not by how much" mechanic could not be pulled from a primary developer source in this pass (see Gaps); it is corroborated only by secondary game-info summaries, so it is flagged as an inference-supported claim rather than a directly verified one.

### Cited Findings
- Reigns has four reputation meters (Church, People, Army/Military, Money) shown as bars at the top of the screen, and these bars fill or deplete depending on the swipe decisions made — [MMOCulture summary of Reigns mechanics](https://mmoculture.com/2026/07/reigns-beyond-pre-registration-news/) ⚠️ (WebSearch-tool synthesis of multiple secondary pages, not a single primary source fetched and read end to end).
- Every choice in Reigns is made by swiping left or right, with no other input method, and each swipe carries a unique consequence for the run — [MMOCulture / general Reigns mechanic summaries via WebSearch](https://mmoculture.com/2026/07/reigns-beyond-pre-registration-news/) ⚠️ (same caveat as above).
- The original design brief behind Reigns explicitly borrowed Tinder's swipe-and-judge interaction and treated it as "a toy," not a dating mechanic, expanding it from simple binary choices into "pleasantly surprising scenarios" — Game Developer (formerly Gamasutra) interview with François Alliot, fetched and read: [Reigns: Her Majesty's Francois Alliot on making a more complex swipe-em-up](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/-i-reigns-her-majesty-s-i-francois-alliot-on-making-a-more-complex-swipe-em-up). This source was fetched successfully and read, but the retrieved text did not contain implementation-level detail on the hover-preview mechanic, meter display, or exact card layout — the interview is about creative/design philosophy, not UI specifics.
- A GDC Vault talk exists specifically on this system — "The Casual (but Regal) Swipe: Creating Game Mechanics in 'Reigns'" — in which Alliot describes the team giving itself a small set of constraints around the swipe system and the tone of the game — [GDC Vault listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024278/The-Casual-(but-Regal)-Swipe). This talk is paywalled/session-gated; only the listing/description was seen, not the talk content, so anything beyond the title and one-line description is a gap, not a finding.

### Inferences
- Because Reigns shows only four bars and no numbers, and because the swipe is the entire input vocabulary, the "hover shows direction but not magnitude" convention widely attributed to Reigns is consistent with the game's minimalist meter design (four dumb bars, no tooltips, no numeric readouts) — but this is my inference from the surrounding design facts, not a directly cited mechanic description.
- The card format (single character portrait + one line of dialogue + two silent choices) generalizes well to "Game Night: AI Lab" only if the team accepts a hard 2-choice cap and drops any on-card cost text entirely, replacing it with per-meter visual feedback after the choice resolves (a "bar moved" animation) rather than a preview.

### Gaps
- Could not verify, from a primary source, the exact interaction of "hovering/dragging toward a side shows a preview icon of which meter will move, but not the amount" — this is a commonly repeated player-facing description of Reigns online, but the two sources reachable in this pass (Game Developer interview, GDC Vault listing) did not confirm it in verifiable text. Treat this specific claim (from the assignment's own key question) as unverified from primary sourcing in this note; it should be checked against direct gameplay footage or the itch.io/YouTube "Casual (but Regal) Swipe" talk if precision is required before committing to the exact mechanic.
- Could not confirm from a primary source whether Reigns has any time limit on a decision (my working assumption, uncorroborated here, is no — it is a self-paced swipe, not a timed pop-up).

## Frostpunk: event pop-ups vs. the Book of Laws

### Takeaway
Frostpunk splits its dilemmas into two distinct formats: reactive event pop-ups (short, triggered by a crisis, often nudging the player toward a specific law) and the deliberate, player-initiated Book of Laws (a paused menu of branching, mutually exclusive law trees). Both communicate consequences through relative, hedged language ("Hope will increase slightly," "Discontent will rise moderately") rather than exact numbers, and the moral weight of a choice is deliberately decoupled from its strategic necessity in the late game.

### Cited Findings
- When a negative event occurs (running out of food, a lack of working hands), a pop-up window appears suggesting a specific law be signed, which steers the player toward believing that particular choice is the available solution — [Frostpunk Wiki, "Events" page, via WebSearch synthesis](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Events) ⚠️ (direct fetch of this page returned HTTP 402 twice; this fact is from the WebSearch tool's summary of the page, not a full read of the source).
- The Book of Laws is composed of two law trees, Adaptation and Purpose, and choosing one law in a pair permanently forecloses its alternative (no going back) — [Frostpunk Wiki, "Book of Laws" page, via WebSearch synthesis](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Book_of_laws) ⚠️ (direct fetch also returned HTTP 402; this is WebSearch-tool synthesis, not a full read).
- Every law signed carries a Discontent or Hope penalty/boost, i.e., a soft, named-meter consequence attached to the choice rather than a hidden number — [Frostpunk Wiki, "Book of Laws," via WebSearch synthesis](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Book_of_laws) ⚠️ (same caveat).
- Rather than showing raw numeric feedback, Frostpunk communicates consequences through relative, hedged phrasing such as "Hope will increase slightly" or "Discontent will rise moderately," and building/law costs remain opaque until after a law is actually passed — Gabriel Chauri, "Frostpunk – Player's Decisions in the Book of Laws," fetched and read in full: [gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions](https://www.gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions/).
- The design deliberately avoids "obviously right" choices by ensuring no two laws produce the same outcome, and by making the moral weight of choices increase over the course of the game while their strategic necessity decreases — for example, the endgame Purpose-branch laws "New Order" (authoritarian) and "New Faith" (religious-fanatic) each eliminate the Hope bar entirely, so the choice is framed as being about the player's values rather than about optimization, since by that point in the game neither branch's mechanical bonus is strictly needed — Gabriel Chauri, fetched and read in full: [gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions](https://www.gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions/).
- The game is composed of two law trees ("horizon of intent" framing) and event pop-ups exist specifically to nudge players toward laws during a crisis, which is a distinct interaction from the deliberate, self-paced Book of Laws menu — Gabriel Chauri, fetched and read in full: [gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions](https://www.gabrielchauri.com/frostpunk-decisions/).
- Frostpunk's developers (11 bit studios) have discussed the "Book of Laws" and its role in provoking moral reflection in a PC Gamer interview, but the article's design-commentary body could not be retrieved in this pass — the fetch returned only the site's membership/paywall shell with the actual article content marked "truncated due to length" — [PC Gamer, "Frostpunk developers on hope, misery, and the ultimately terrifying book of laws"](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/) ⚠️ fetch failed to return the article body; only the search-result snippet (not independently re-verified) suggested the developers frame it as "How would you behave?" — this specific quote/framing is unverified in this note.

### Inferences
- The pairing of "reactive pop-up nudges toward a law" + "deliberate paused menu to actually enact it" maps closely onto the game-jam project's own two-tier structure (a warning card first, then a full card next turn if ignored) — Frostpunk's version is the inverse sequencing (crisis pop-up first, considered law-book decision second) but the same principle applies: don't force the weightiest decision into the same fast, reactive moment as the warning.
- "Hope will increase slightly / Discontent will rise moderately" is a directly reusable pattern for a hidden-stat game: convert exact deltas into a small fixed vocabulary of magnitude words (slightly / moderately / significantly) attached to a named, in-fiction meter, rather than a number or a raw icon count.

### Gaps

This should be verified directly against the wiki or in-game footage before being treated as authoritative.
- Could not verify the exact number of choices per Frostpunk event pop-up (my recollection, unverified here, is typically 2, occasionally 3) or whether pop-ups can be dismissed/deferred and what expiry penalty (if any) applies — no source in this pass addressed dismissal/expiry mechanics for the pop-up format specifically (only the law-book's permanence was confirmed).
- Could not verify presentation details of "miracle"/saga quest events (e.g., the New Faith questline sequence) beyond the fact that "New Faith" is a law that then triggers a follow-on story arc starting when the Execution Platform completes — [Frostpunk Wiki, "The New Faith (Arc)," via WebSearch snippet only, not fetched](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_New_Faith_(Arc)) ⚠️ — no detail retrieved on how the arc's individual event windows are laid out.

## FTL: Faster Than Light — the text event box and blue options

### Takeaway
FTL's events are plain modal text boxes: an italicized narrative paragraph followed by a vertical list of unstyled choice lines, with "blue options" (choices gated behind ship equipment/crew/upgrades) marked in blue and often but not always more favorable. Outcomes are not previewed numerically anywhere in the base UI — the player commits blind and then reads a follow-up text/resource summary — and community mod tools exist specifically because the base game hides outcomes entirely.

### Cited Findings
- Blue Options are choices within random encounters/events that are selectable only when the ship has the required equipment, crew, or system upgrade level, and they often (not always) lead to more favorable outcomes than the non-blue alternatives — [FTL Wiki, "Blue Options," via WebSearch synthesis](https://ftl.fandom.com/wiki/Blue_Options) ⚠️ (direct fetch attempted twice, both returned HTTP 402; this is WebSearch-tool synthesis of the page, not a full read).
- The wiki's own style guide for transcribing events states that event introduction text is written in italics exactly as it appears in the game, plain choice lines are listed without formatting, and blue options are marked with a dedicated template — [FTL Wiki, "Meta:Event Conventions," via WebSearch synthesis](https://ftl.fandom.com/wiki/Meta:Event_Conventions) ⚠️ (direct fetch returned HTTP 402; this is WebSearch-tool synthesis, not a full read).
- A player-made mod, "FTLEventTags," exists specifically to tag roughly 1,000 lines of in-game event/choice text with their possible outcomes, and its author states "no information is displayed that can't otherwise be found in the game files, or from Googling dialogue" — [GitHub: thrnz/FTLEventTags1.6](https://github.com/thrnz/FTLEventTags1.6) ⚠️ (seen via WebSearch snippet only, not independently fetched and read).
- FTL has a player-configurable option to add a "brief delay" before an event's choices become clickable, specifically to prevent accidentally selecting a numbered response before reading it — [Steam Community discussion, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/212680/discussions/0/358415738195367202/) ⚠️ (WebSearch synthesis only, not independently fetched).

### Inferences
- The existence of a delay-before-clickable option, rather than a true timer, implies the base game event box is not time-limited in the sense of an expiring countdown — the ship's other systems (enemy fire, oxygen, fires) keep running in real time in the background, but the event dialog itself does not auto-resolve or expire on its own on a clock. This is my inference from the "brief delay to prevent misclicks" setting existing at all (a misclick-prevention feature would be pointless if the box already timed out), not a directly cited statement that events never expire.
- FTL's near-total refusal to preview numeric outcomes (hence a mod being needed to leak them) is the most aggressive point on the "hide everything" end of the spectrum among the games surveyed here — useful as the extreme reference point opposite CK3's tooltip-preview approach.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch the FTL Wiki's own event/blue-option pages directly (both returned HTTP 402 from the fetch tool on two separate URLs); the wiki-sourced claims above rest on WebSearch synthesis, not a full read, and should be spot-checked against actual event screenshots before being treated as exact.
- Could not confirm the number of choices typically offered per FTL event (2–4 is the commonly cited range from player knowledge, but no source in this pass stated a number) or whether a one-line reward/resource summary is shown post-choice versus a full text follow-up.

## Crusader Kings 3: event windows, portraits, and the notification queue

### Takeaway
CK3 events are pop-up windows with up to five configurable character-portrait slots (left, right, and three lower positions), any number of dialog options (including zero, for pure-notification events), and option tooltips that can show a custom preview of effects. Multiple pending events queue in an on-screen outliner/notification area rather than blocking the game; an event left unanswered for four months on the same in-game date has its first listed option auto-selected for the player.

### Cited Findings
- CK3 events "occur throughout gameplay... appearing as pop-up notifications on the player's screen that may present choices, or may simply inform the player of the consequences and require they acknowledge the event has occurred" — CK3 Wiki, "Events" page, fetched and read directly: [ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Events](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Events). (Note: the wiki page itself is flagged by Paradox's own wiki as an incomplete stub needing expansion, so absence of detail on some sub-points reflects the source's own gap, not a missed read.)
- "If the player has not chosen an option after four months on the same date, the first choice gets autoselected" — CK3 Wiki, "Events" page, fetched and read directly: [ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Events](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Events).
- CK3's event scripting format supports five distinct portrait positions in an event window: `left_portrait`, `right_portrait`, `lower_left_portrait`, `lower_center_portrait`, and `lower_right_portrait`, each bound to a character and an optional animation — CK3 Wiki, "Event modding" page, fetched and read directly: [ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding).
- Event types in CK3's scripting model include `character_event`, `letter_event`, `duel_event`, characterless events (type `none`), and an `empty` event type needed as a technical prerequisite for characterless events to trigger — CK3 Wiki, "Event modding," fetched and read directly: [ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding).
- Events in CK3 "can have any number of options, including none at all," and options support attached flavor text shown in the option's own tooltip — CK3 Wiki, "Event modding," fetched and read directly: [ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Event_modding). (The page did not, in the retrieved text, document a practical maximum option count or the mechanism for a full numeric-effect preview tooltip, e.g. `custom_tooltip`/`show_as_tooltip` scripting commands — see Gaps.)
- CK3's interface includes event notifications that pop up both on the game map and in a dedicated outliner area, a feed in the bottom-right corner, a "current situation" menu at top-center, and emergency indicators, and hotkeys exist to open and resolve pending notifications from that queue — [CK3 Wiki, "Interface" page, via WebSearch synthesis](https://ck3.paradoxwikis.com/Interface) ⚠️ (this page itself was not independently fetched; the claim is from WebSearch's synthesis across the Interface page and a Paradox forum thread titled "Queued events outliner," so treat the specific queue/outliner mechanic description as secondary-sourced, not a direct primary-source read).

### Inferences
- The four-month auto-select-the-first-option rule is the clearest "if ignored, a default happens" model among the surveyed games, and it specifically defaults to whichever option is listed first in the event's script — for a game-jam project, this suggests a simple, legible rule ("if you never resolve it, the first/most-conservative listed choice happens") is a well-tested pattern rather than a novel risk.
- The five-portrait-slot system (one or two "speaker" portraits plus up to three "lower" reaction portraits) is a way to stage a multi-character scene diegetically without extra UI chrome — directly relevant to the owner's preference for diegetic presentation, since CK3 achieves "who is involved" purely through character art placement in the same window, not through separate panels.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm from a fetched primary source exactly how CK3's option tooltips preview numeric or directional effects (e.g., the `show_effects`/`custom_tooltip` scripting mechanism referenced in community knowledge) — the Event modding page's retrieved text mentioned only flavor-text tooltips, not effect-preview tooltips specifically, so the assignment's premise that "option tooltips preview effects" is plausible and widely known from playing the game but not verified word-for-word against a fetched source in this pass.
- Could not confirm the precise behavior of the "notification stack" (how many can be pending simultaneously, whether they visually stack as cards, or whether the game can be advanced with events still pending) beyond WebSearch's synthesis that notifications appear in an outliner and a bottom-right feed. This needs a direct read of the CK3 Wiki "Interface" page or in-game screenshots to state precisely.

## Suzerain: political dilemmas without exposed numbers

### Takeaway
Suzerain frames its decisions as narrative "decision prompts" inside a heavily illustrated, text-driven political-sim interface: visible stats (Economic Development, Government Budget) are shown, but key consequence-tracking variables (Alignment, Unrest, Opinion) are explicitly kept hidden from the player, and the game leans on narrative reflection (advisors, news, character dialogue reacting to your choices) rather than numeric readouts to communicate what a choice did. Independent design commentary notes that this opacity is partly a practical necessity of branching-narrative budgets (some choices are built to have no real effect, and hiding that from the player is what makes "perfect" play impossible) as well as a deliberate design choice.

### Cited Findings
- Suzerain's decisions "alter potential events in the future and affect visible statistics (Economical Development, Government Budget) and hidden statistics (Alignment, Unrest, Opinion)," and "no single action brings the country to its knees" because major consequences depend on a string of choices rather than one card — [Steam Community app page / aggregated design summary via WebSearch](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1207650) ⚠️ (WebSearch-tool synthesis across multiple pages, not a single primary source fetched and read in full).
- "The more options given to players, the more work developers must do, and as a result, some choices are sculpted to secretly have no meaningful effect on the game," and hiding the exact consequences of actions from the player is what makes "perfect decision-making" impossible — this is presented by the search tool as drawn from academic/analytical discussion of the game (an arXiv paper on interactive storytelling for media literacy appeared among the sources), but the specific arXiv source was not independently fetched and read in this pass, so it is flagged rather than cited to one named document ⚠️ (WebSearch synthesis, source paper not opened directly: candidates were arxiv.org/pdf/2508.11059 and arxiv.org/pdf/2208.14037, neither fetched).
- The game's "Country Overview" screen serves as a communication tool that reflects the player's choices back through in-fiction dialogue/reporting rather than through raw hidden-stat numbers — Articy showcase page for Suzerain, fetched and read (though the retrieved text was thin on this specific point): [articy.com/en/showcase/suzerain](https://www.articy.com/en/showcase/suzerain/).
- A dedicated player-written "Decision Prompts Guide and List" exists on Neoseeker, indicating the game's key dilemma moments are formally named/catalogued as a distinct UI element called a "decision prompt," separate from ordinary branching dialogue — [Neoseeker, "Decision Prompts Guide and List"](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/guides/Decision_Prompts_Guide_and_List) ⚠️ fetch attempt returned HTTP 403 Forbidden; only the page's existence and title were confirmed via search-result listing, not its content.

### Inferences
- Suzerain's split between a few visible, named numeric stats (budget, economic development) and several explicitly hidden tracking variables (alignment, unrest, opinion) is close to the exact shape the owner wants for "Game Night: AI Lab" (some numbers can be shown, the sensitive ones — alignment debt, rival capability — stay hidden) — Suzerain's solution is to report the hidden variables' movement only through narrative consequence (a character's reaction, a news brief, a later event becoming available or unavailable), never through a number or a bar tied to that specific hidden stat.
- Because some choices in a branching narrative game are (per the inference above) built to be inert, and the game does not tell the player which ones, the player's uncertainty about "did that even matter" is itself functioning as a diegetic stand-in for the hidden stat — an approach that only works if the game is narratively rich enough to sell the illusion, which is a real constraint worth flagging for a game-jam scope.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the actual layout of a Suzerain "decision prompt" screen (who speaks, portrait presence, body length, number of choices, whether there is a time limit) from any source reachable in this pass — the two most promising sources (Neoseeker's Decision Prompts guide, a dedicated catalogue page) returned HTTP 403, and general Steam/interview pages did not describe the screen's visual layout. This is the weakest-covered part of the Suzerain section and should be treated as a gap requiring direct gameplay footage or screenshots if precise layout detail is needed.
- Could not confirm whether Suzerain decisions can be deferred/postponed or whether they force an immediate choice with no time pressure (my working assumption, unverified, is untimed — consistent with it being a text-heavy visual novel-adjacent political sim — but no source confirmed this explicitly).

## Cross-cutting design writing on dilemma/choice design

### Takeaway
The clearest, most citable design-theory source found in this pass is Soren Johnson's "When Choice is Bad" (Designer Notes), which argues choice is not free: every added decision point costs the player time, adds complexity, and — counterintuitively — can produce more repetitive play if players converge on a dominant strategy once they've solved the choice space. Sid Meier's "games are a series of interesting decisions" framing (from a 2012 GDC talk, sourced only via a secondary summary in this pass) supplies the classic definition of what makes a choice interesting: a genuine tradeoff (e get a bigger sword for 500 gold; a faster car with worse handling) where the player understands the scope of what's at stake.

### Cited Findings
- Soren Johnson's essay proposes the working formula "(total fun) = (meaningful decisions) / (time played)" — i.e., adding choices is a tradeoff against play time, not a pure benefit — Designer Notes, "GD Column 25: When Choice is Bad," fetched and read directly: [designer-notes.com/gd-column-25-when-choice-is-bad](https://www.designer-notes.com/gd-column-25-when-choice-is-bad/).
- Johnson identifies three distinct costs of adding choice: (1) too much time (e.g., Risk's army-placement phase lengthens play without guaranteeing the added choice is meaningful, contrasted with the streamlined Dice Wars), (2) too much complexity (cognitive overload — choosing among fifty technology options feels categorically different from choosing among five, which is part of why MOBAs succeeded by having players control one hero instead of an army), and (3) too much repetition (paradoxically, unlimited choice can reduce experienced variety because players converge on an optimal strategy — Atom Zombie Smasher counters this by randomly restricting which weapons are available each round, forcing adaptation) — Designer Notes, fetched and read directly: [designer-notes.com/gd-column-25-when-choice-is-bad](https://www.designer-notes.com/gd-column-25-when-choice-is-bad/).
- The essay's retrieved text did not explicitly address how to show or hide the consequences of a choice from the player (it is about whether to offer a choice at all and how many options to offer, not about numeric transparency) — noted here as an explicit scope boundary of this source, not a gap in retrieval.
- Sid Meier's 2012 GDC talk framed games as "a series of interesting decisions," where an interesting decision characteristically involves a tradeoff (a big sword costs 500 gold; the fastest car has poorer handling), and where making a decision meaningful requires the player to understand the full scope of what the choice affects — [Game Developer, "GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions," via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions) ⚠️ (WebSearch-tool synthesis of the article, not independently fetched and read in full in this pass).
- A separate, dedicated GDC talk exists specifically on this topic — "Decisions That Matter: Meaningful Choice in Game and Level Design" (GDC 2014) — described as addressing why meaningful choice matters psychologically and how it ties to player agency and the "space of possibility," but its content beyond the title/abstract was not retrieved in this pass — [GDC Vault listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020570/Level-Design-in-a-Day) and [worch.com recap](https://www.worch.com/2014/03/23/decisions-that-matter/) ⚠️ neither was fetched and read directly; only titles/one-line descriptions were seen via WebSearch.

### Inferences
- Johnson's "too much repetition" argument (unlimited choice converging on a dominant strategy) is directly relevant to the game-jam project's card design: if every event card always offers "sacrifice safety for progress" as one of 2–3 options, players will quickly learn to always pick (or always avoid) that option, and the fix Johnson documents elsewhere in games (Atom Zombie Smasher's random restriction of available choices) suggests varying which specific costed options are even on offer, rather than only varying the flavor text around a fixed option shape.
- None of the cross-cutting sources reached in this pass gave a first-party, explicit rule for "how to hide a hidden stat's movement from the player while still making the tradeoff legible" — the strongest concrete pattern for that specific problem, among all games surveyed, remains Frostpunk's relative-magnitude phrasing ("slightly / moderately") and Suzerain's narrative-reflection approach (a character or news item reacts to the choice instead of a number appearing), both cited above.
