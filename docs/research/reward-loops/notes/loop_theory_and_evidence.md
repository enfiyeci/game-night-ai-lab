# Core loops, compulsion loops, reward schedules, and the evidence behind "dopamine loop" claims (for Game Night AI Lab)

Reading-quality legend. Plain claims were read from the source text itself in this session. A claim marked ⚠️ rests on less than the whole document (an abstract, a partial read, a search-result summary, or a tool summary). The full accounting is in the Coverage section at the end. Quotes are under 15 words.

Note for the report writer: the playtest comment ("so many decisions it overwhelmed me, then weeks when nothing happened") describes a pacing-rhythm problem (clusters then gaps). The literature below is much stronger on rhythm and on the limits of "dopamine" claims than on any number of decisions per minute. No source I found gives a decisions-per-minute figure.

## 1. How practitioners define core loop, compulsion loop, nested loops, and the appointment/session loop, and who coined what

### Takeaway
There is no single agreed vocabulary. The behavioral-schedule foundation comes from Hopson (2001), who never uses the phrase "compulsion loop"; the phrase itself has an unclear origin and a definition that bakes in a "neurochemical reward" claim that scientists dispute (see section 2). Nested timescales are described by Cook ("fractal" loops, 2012) and Collier (short, medium, long loops, 2011). "Appointment" play is documented as a named dark pattern by Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013). I found no primary definition of a "session loop" or "meta loop" as such.

### Cited Findings
- Hopson's article "Behavioral Game Design" (Gamasutra, April 27, 2001) defines reinforcer, contingency (the rule for when rewards are given) and response, and describes fixed and variable ratio and interval schedules. The author later became head of user research at Bungie. The word "compulsion" does not appear anywhere in the article text; it uses "behavioral momentum" for the tendency to keep going. — [Hopson, Game Developer (archived Gamasutra)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)
- Hopson on schedules: "variable ratio schedules produce the highest overall rates of activity", and he adds this "doesn't necessarily mean they're the best". Fixed ratios produce a long pause then a burst; fixed intervals produce a pause that gradually shortens; variable intervals give steady but slower activity. — [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)
- Hopson's design advice: keep several activities available at once so that when one runs dry another takes the slack; avoid sharp drops in reward ("reducing the level of reinforcement is a very punishing thing"). He also lists avoidance schedules (Ultima Online house decay) as a cheap way to create behavioral momentum, since developers need not supply new rewards. He closes by saying the goal is to design games to bring out "the kind of player we want". — [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)
- Origin of "compulsion loop" is unclear. Joseph Kim (2014) says the concept was "introduced as early as 2001 by John Hopson" and gives the definition he adapts from Adam Crowe: a "habitual, designed chain of activities" repeated to gain a "neurochemical reward". Kim also writes that compulsion-loop theorists believe free will does not exist, and that the point of the loop is to induce a biological response such as dopamine release. Kim's attribution to Hopson is Kim's reading; Hopson's own text does not use the term. — [Kim, "The Compulsion Loop Explained", Game Developer, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-compulsion-loop-explained); Hopson's text checked at [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)
- Pete Collier (Dec 2011): the term is "recently coined", he is "unsure of its exact origin", and there is "no official definition of it". His own definition: a construct that keeps someone engaged by marrying their actions with an appropriate level of reward. — [Collier, Game Developer / #altdevblogaday](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/opinion-compulsion-loops-in-the-short-medium-and-long-term)
- Nested timescales (Collier): three compulsion loops should run at once. Short term = preoccupation and instant gratification; medium term = construction and deferred gratification (short actions framed as building blocks toward something); long term = legacy and reflective gratification, a loop that "never fulfills its loop". He says the short-to-medium transition is the critical juncture where attachment can "shatter". This is one designer's opinion, with no empirical test. — [Collier](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/opinion-compulsion-loops-in-the-short-medium-and-long-term)
- Core loop vs compulsion loop (Kim): the core loop is the chain of activities in the primary user flow ("what is the user primarily doing over and over"); he says the two terms are often used interchangeably and he separates them by design of the phases. He splits a loop into Anticipation, Action, Reward. He claims Skinner-style research focused on the Reward phase and that mobile games innovated in the Anticipation phase (aspirational neighbors) and the Action phase (variable-ratio actions). He names "appointment mechanics" (Game of War timers) as a retention tactic he sees as excessive. — [Kim 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-compulsion-loop-explained)
- Cook, "Loops and Arcs" (Lostgarden, April 30, 2012): a loop is a cycle where the player's mental model prompts an action on the system, the system returns feedback, and the model updates. "These loops are fractal and occur at multiple levels and frequencies", and "nested, dependent loops" yield complex dynamics. Loops build "wisdom" (a holistic understanding of a system). "An arc is a broken loop you exit immediately" (a one-time experience like a film). A cheap test: ask "What repeats and what does not?" Cook notes in-app-purchase economics reward keeping players engaged long term ("a fit game is one that you play forever"). The article mentions skill atoms only in passing and does not define them; it links to a separate post. — [Cook, Lostgarden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/04/30/loops-and-arcs/)
- "Playing by appointment" and "grinding" are named temporal dark patterns. Appointment: the game, not the player, sets when to play, and a "withering" mechanic (crops lost if not harvested within about 24 hours in FarmVille) turns this into an obligation. Grinding: repetitive tasks that emphasize time invested over skill. The authors frame dark patterns as design elements "against a player's best interests". — [Zagal, Björk & Lewis, "Dark Patterns in the Design of Games", FDG 2013 (full text PDF)](https://gup.ub.gu.se/file/101018) ⚠️ read the abstract and the appointment and grinding sections only, not the whole paper.
- Machinations (Joris Dormans) is described by the publisher and a sample-chapter listing as a framework to build, document, simulate and test a game's internal economy (resources and the actions that produce, consume and exchange them), in Adams & Dormans, *Game Mechanics: Advanced Game Design* (New Riders, 2012). — [InformIT listing](https://www.informit.com/store/game-mechanics-advanced-game-design-9780321820273) ⚠️ publisher and search-result descriptions only; I did not read the book or the Machinations documentation.
- Hodent, *The Gamer's Brain* (CRC Press, paperback Aug 2017) lists motivation, emotion, attention, memory, learning and "neuromyths" as topics. — [listing at Video Game Library](https://thevideogamelibrary.org/book/the-gamer-s-brain-how-neuroscience-and-ux-can-impact-video-game-design) ⚠️ listing only; I could not confirm what the book says about loops, SDT or dopamine, and I did not read the book.

### Inferences
- For Game Night, a useful working vocabulary that is traceable: Cook's loop (act, feedback, mental-model update) is the unit; Collier's three timescales and Kim's Anticipation-Action-Reward are two ways to stack loops. Neither is a validated model, so treat them as design checklists.
- Kim's definition of "compulsion loop" quietly assumes the neurochemical claim and a "free will does not exist" framing. Because the lab is a teaching game, it is safer to describe its loops as feedback/learning loops (Cook) and to avoid the compulsion framing.
- Hopson's avoidance-schedule recipe (decay, upkeep) is exactly what Zagal et al. flag when it becomes a "withering" obligation. Any upkeep mechanic in the lab (compute bills, staff morale decay) should be checked against that.

### Gaps
- The original Crowe & Buchanan "Compulsion Loops: Compulsive Behavior as Mass Media" and Beresford's article (both cited by Kim) were not located; the "who coined compulsion loop" question stays open. Kim's attribution to Hopson is secondhand.
- Katkoff's "Mid-Core Success Part 1: Core Loops" (cited by Kim) was not reached.
- Cook's "The Chemistry of Game Design" (skill atoms) did not load (the page returned almost no text), so I cannot report its definition.
- No primary source found that defines a "session loop" or "meta loop" explicitly; the nearest are Collier's medium/long loops and Zagal's appointment pattern.
- Dormans/Adams and Hodent were not read first-hand (see ⚠️ above).

## 2. Neuroscience: reward prediction error, wanting vs liking, Koepp 1998, anticipation vs receipt, and where "dopamine hit" claims overreach

### Takeaway
Dopamine neurons in monkeys signal prediction errors and, separately, uncertainty during the wait for a reward; Berridge's work shows dopamine drives "wanting" (motivation), not "liking" (pleasure). The Koepp 1998 PET study shows dopamine release during a video game but does not show pleasure. "Dopamine is the pleasure chemical" is rejected by the scientist whose work is most often cited for the opposite, and by a peer-reviewed game-industry blog and a DiGRA paper.

### Cited Findings
- Schultz, Dayan & Montague (Science, 1997): dopaminergic neurons in primates have "fluctuating output" that "apparently signals changes or errors in the predictions" of rewards; the findings fit quantitative theories of adaptive control. — [Europe PMC record, PMID 9054347](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9054347) (complete abstract read; the article itself was not).
- Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz (Science, 2003): the phasic dopamine response varies with reward probability (consistent with a prediction-error signal), and a separate response "covaried with uncertainty": a gradual ramp up until the possible time of reward. Uncertainty is maximal at probability 0.5. — [Europe PMC search record](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22Discrete%20coding%20of%20reward%20probability%20and%20uncertainty%20by%20dopamine%20neurons%22) ⚠️ abstract only; monkeys.
- Koepp et al. (Nature, 1998): PET with 11C-raclopride showed reduced binding in the human striatum during a video game, "consistent with increased release" of dopamine. The reduction correlated positively with task performance and was greatest in the ventral striatum. The abstract claims behavioural conditions under which dopamine is released in humans; it says nothing about enjoyment ratings. — [Europe PMC record, PMID 9607763](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9607763) ⚠️ abstract only; the abstract does not state the sample size and I did not verify it.
- Berridge & Robinson (American Psychologist, 2016): liking and wanting are dissociable. Wanting ("incentive salience") is produced by "large and robust" systems including mesolimbic dopamine; liking is produced by small, fragile hedonic hotspots and is "not dependent on dopamine". Rats with nearly all brain dopamine depleted still showed normal liking reactions to sugar but lost motivation to eat. Dopamine stimulation even inside hedonic hotspots "always fail[s] to enhance liking". They state that it is now rare to find an affective neuroscientist who still asserts that dopamine mediates pleasure; in their view dopamine mediates "desire rather than pleasure". Wanting is triggered in pulses by cues and can be amplified by stress or excitement and can run against what a person consciously wants. The incentive-sensitization theory of addiction concerns drugs; its extension to behavioral addictions is described as "emerging", with mechanisms "not yet understood", and as applying to highly susceptible individuals. — [Berridge & Robinson 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207/)
- Berridge (2012): incentive salience takes learned reward cues plus current physiological state as inputs, so cue-triggered motivation can drift from the learned value of the reward. — [Eur J Neurosci 2012, DOI 10.1111/j.1460-9568.2012.07990.x via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22From%20prediction%20error%20to%20incentive%20salience%22) ⚠️ abstract only.
- Anselme (2013): animals prefer uncertain rewards over certain ones even when the net gain is lower, and respond more to reward cues in uncertain situations; Anselme argues unpredictability and deprivation both raise motivation. — [Behav Brain Res 2013 via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22Dopamine%2C%20motivation%2C%20and%20the%20evolutionary%20significance%20of%20gambling-like%20behaviour%22) ⚠️ abstract only; animal work.
- Game-industry critique with academic peer review: Lewis-Evans (Game Developer, Aug 27, 2013; acknowledges peer review by clinical neuropsychologists at the University of Groningen) argues dopamine is mostly about wanting, and that "dopamine is probably not the cause" of the pleasure of play. He notes dopamine-lacking mice still like sugar water; a leading researcher joked that the best answer to "what does dopamine do" is "confuse neuroscientists"; Parkinson's patients on dopamine drugs show compulsions without more pleasure. He says dopamine is also released during repetitive motor movements, which "may or may not have anything to do with rewards" (citing Egerton et al. 2009). He warns that if the public learns dopamine is about wanting rather than liking, the message "designed to maximise dopamine" shifts from "fun" to "want to play it, even if, perhaps, you don't like" it. He urges designers to "be neuroskeptical" and says watching behavior in playtests is more useful than neuroscience for design. — [Lewis-Evans, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/dopamine-and-games-liking-learning-or-wanting-to-play-)
- Nielsen & Blom (DiGRA 2017 extended abstract) argue "dopamine is not the cause of pleasure in games" and that commonly drawn links between dopamine, games and addiction are not valid. — [ITU record](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/crimes-against-pok%C3%A9mon-go-why-dopamine-does-not-explain-the-pleas/) ⚠️ abstract page only; the extended abstract PDF was not opened.
- Practitioner side: Kim's article presents dopamine release as the purpose of a compulsion loop and cites Wikipedia for dopamine being released in response to "rewarding experiences". — [Kim 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-compulsion-loop-explained). Conflict: Berridge & Robinson and Lewis-Evans above.

### Inferences
- The anticipation phase is where dopamine-linked wanting and the uncertainty ramp show up in the animal data, not the receipt phase; so the waiting time before the review-score reveal is the part with the strongest scientific support for "pull", and it is also where honest design matters most.
- Wanting without liking is the scientific core of why "maximise the dopamine" is not a defensible design goal for a teaching game: it describes a state the player may not endorse. This is Lewis-Evans's own ethical point.
- Koepp 1998 is correctly cited for "dopamine is released during skilled video-game play and tracks performance", not for "games feel good because of dopamine".
- Almost all the mechanistic data are from monkeys, rats and mice or from drug and gambling studies; transfer to a 25-minute management sim is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Schultz 1997, Koepp 1998, Fiorillo 2003, Berridge 2012 or Anselme 2013 papers beyond their abstracts. The Koepp sample size and any game-specific subjective ratings are unchecked.
- Dean Burnett's and Tommy Blanchard's critiques ("science garnish") appeared only in a search summary and were not opened, so I do not rely on them.
- Not found: a controlled study measuring dopamine response to a specific commercial game feature (loot drop, level up) in humans.

## 3. Variable-ratio schedules, near misses, uncertainty, and loot-box/gambling research

### Takeaway
The operant-conditioning facts Hopson cites (steady, high response rates under variable ratio) come from lab animals and simple tasks, and human adults behave differently from animals on at least some schedules. Near misses increase wanting to play in gambling tasks but only when the player has control, and loot-box spending correlates with problem gambling in surveys, with direction unknown. No source shows a variable-reward mechanic in a no-stakes management game causes harm.

### Cited Findings
- Hopson says behavioral findings are "species-independent" and apply to novel domains such as games. — [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design). Complicating evidence: in a study of two infants under one year old on fixed-interval schedules, behavior closely resembled that of animals (scalloped responding) but "differed markedly" from older children and adults, which the authors take as support for the idea that verbal behavior explains the difference between adults and animals. — [Lowe, Beasty & Bentall 1983](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22infant%20performance%20on%20fixed-interval%20schedules%22) ⚠️ abstract only; this concerns fixed-interval schedules, not variable ratio.
- Clark et al. (Neuron, 2009): in a slot-machine task, near misses were rated less pleasant than full misses yet "increased desire to play"; the effect appeared only when the subject had personal control over arranging the gamble. Near misses activated ventral striatum and anterior insula, regions that also respond to wins; insula response correlated with a gambling-propensity questionnaire. The authors conclude near misses "invigorate gambling through the anomalous recruitment of reward circuitry". They cite earlier work suggesting near misses at roughly 30% frequency encourage persistence. The fMRI group was 15 volunteers. They interpret the near miss as a prediction error (expected win, then omission). — [Clark et al. 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658737/) ⚠️ read abstract, introduction opening and the first part of the discussion, not the methods and results.
- Reid (1986), the classic near-miss paper named in the brief, was not opened; Clark et al. cite the earlier near-miss literature.
- Zendle & Cairns (PLOS ONE, 2018): survey of 7,422 gamers found loot-box spending related to problem-gambling severity (η² = 0.054), much stronger than the relationship between problem gambling and other in-game purchases (η² = 0.004). The mean loot-box spending category for problem gamblers was $20-$30 to $30-$40 (score 6.47) versus $1-$5 to $5-$10 for non-problem gamblers (score 2.41); the survey used spending bands, not exact dollar amounts. The study is correlational, cannot say which way causation runs, and the sample was unblinded. — [Zendle & Cairns 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206767) ⚠️ read the abstract, introduction opening and discussion, not the methods.
- Meta-level loot-box evidence: a systematic review (Spicer et al., New Media & Society, 2022) reports 12 of 13 publications finding a positive relationship, mean r = 0.27, with causality undetermined; a separate meta-analysis (Garea et al.) reports r about 0.26. — [Spicer et al. record](https://pureportal.bcu.ac.uk/en/publications/loot-boxes-problem-gambling-and-problem-video-gaming-a-systematic/) ⚠️ search-result summary only; neither paper was opened.
- Population context: Przybylski, Weinstein & Murayama (Am J Psychiatry, 2017; four surveys, N = 18,932): more than 2 in 3 players reported no symptoms of Internet gaming disorder, between 0.3% and 1.0% might meet criteria for a potential diagnosis, and Internet games "may be significantly less addictive than gambling". Links to health outcomes were "decidedly mixed". — [Europe PMC record, PMID 27809571](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27809571) (complete abstract read; the article not).
- Design-side caution: Hopson notes that a variable ratio is not automatically "best", and that sudden reward drops provoke frustration (pigeon and chimpanzee anecdotes, which are unsourced in the article). — [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)

### Inferences
- The Game Night event cards and review-score reveal are uncertain outcomes, but nothing is bought with real money and the player is not betting; the loot-box literature is about paid randomized rewards and is not direct evidence about them.
- Clark's near-miss effect needs personal control and gambling stakes, and it is a measured increase in wish-to-continue paired with lower pleasure. Presenting a score as "so close to the top tier" repeatedly would borrow that mechanism; for an honest teaching design, show the score's components instead.
- Hopson's "multiple activities available" advice is the most directly useful behavioral rule for the "nothing happens" weeks: a flat gap is a pause in motivation, and parallel low-stakes activities (reviewing a feed, tuning a run) fill it.

### Gaps
- No source here tests variable-ratio schedules in a management or strategy game; the evidence is from animals, slots and surveys.
- Reid 1986, Skinner primary texts, and the 2019 Zendle replication were not opened.
- Przybylski et al. is about problem gaming prevalence; it does not address how much a no-money game's design contributes.

## 4. Complementary models: Self-Determination Theory, flow and difficulty adjustment, peak-end rule, Koster's theory of fun, Schell's interest curves

### Takeaway
Self-Determination Theory has direct game evidence (autonomy, competence and relatedness predict enjoyment) and fits a teaching game better than schedule-based theory. Flow and the peak-end rule are widely cited, but the game-specific evidence is thinner than their popularity (dynamic difficulty adjustment shows mixed results; peak-end comes from pain studies). Koster's "learning is fun" and Schell's interest curve are designer frameworks, not tested theories.

### Cited Findings
- Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (Motivation and Emotion, 2006): four studies. Studies 1-3 (people assigned to play 1, 2 and 4 games) found perceived in-game autonomy and competence associated with enjoyment, preference and changes in well-being; intuitive controls related to presence. Study 4 (n = 730, online multiplayer community) found autonomy, competence and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play. For enjoyment, the three needs jointly explained R² = .45 (autonomy β = .49, competence .24, relatedness .12); for future play, R² = .10. Yee's achievement motive was negatively related to post-play mood. Authors list limitations including self-selected play and self-report. — [Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf) ⚠️ read the abstract, introduction opening and general discussion; DOI https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8.
- Sailer et al. (Computers in Human Behavior, 2017) report in an SDT-based randomized study that particular elements affect particular needs (badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised competence and perceived meaningfulness; avatars, story and teammates related to relatedness) and that decision freedom did not move as predicted. — [Sailer et al. record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/53202) ⚠️ search-result summary only.
- Flow: Chen (CACM, 2007) reproduces Csikszentmihalyi's eight components (challenging activity requiring skill, clear goals, direct immediate feedback, sense of control, and others) and the Flow Zone between anxiety (too hard) and boredom (too easy), noting a "fuzzy safe zone" tolerating temporary lack of stimulation if the player has "hope that more is on the way". Chen warns that too many choices "overwhelm the user" and that requiring frequent choices can hurt flow. It is a viewpoint article, not a study. — [Chen 2007 PDF](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/~lieber/courses/csu670/f08/materials/p31-chen-flow-in-games.pdf) ⚠️ read roughly the first 7,500 of 18,600 characters.
- Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA): a review summary says DDA's effect on player experience has "yielded mixed results" and critiques its heavy reliance on flow theory. — [Guo, Thawonmas & Ren as summarized in search results](https://www.universityxp.com/research/2026/5/11/rethinking-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-for-video-game-design) ⚠️ search-result summary only; the paper was not opened.
- Peak-end rule: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (Psychological Science, 1993): after a 60 s cold-water trial and a 90 s trial whose last 30 s was slightly warmer, a significant majority chose to repeat the longer one; the authors argue duration matters little in retrospective judgment, which is dominated by the worst and final moments. — [Psychological Science abstract page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x/) ⚠️ abstract only, via a tool summary. Philosopher John Broome's 1996 commentary reports Kahneman found the rule works for pleasant episodes such as concerts too. — [Broome, Analysis 1996 (PDF)](https://www.utilitarianism.com/pain.pdf) ⚠️ read about half; it is a commentary, not Kahneman's data.
- Koster's *A Theory of Fun*: in his 10-years-later slides he says "games are systems built to help us learn patterns" and "fun is a neurochemical reward to encourage us to keep trying", quoting his book: "With games, learning is the drug." The slides note boredom follows when patterns are mastered; Koster recounts that the idea "came out of cognitive science and evolutionary psychology". — [Koster, Theory of Fun 10 Years Later (slides)](https://www.raphkoster.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Theory-of-Fun-10-Years-Later.pdf) ⚠️ read about the first 6,000 of roughly 42,000 characters of slide text.
- Schell's interest curve: described by a secondary summary as an initial peak, then a gradual climb with smaller peaks and valleys to a final climax. — [Notes by Lex](https://notesbylex.com/interest-curve) ⚠️ secondary summary only; I could not verify Schell's own wording or chapter.
- Pacing evidence from production: Valve's Mike Booth (GDC 2009 deck) says Counter-Strike's natural pacing is "spiky", "constant, unchanging combat is fatiguing", and "long periods of inactivity are boring"; Left 4 Dead's AI Director estimates each player's "emotional intensity" and, if intensity is too high, removes major threats for a while. Mobs spawn at randomized 90-180 s intervals; boss events are dealt from a shuffled set with no repeats ("structured unpredictability"). This is a designer's account of practice, not a controlled test. — [Booth, The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)

### Inferences
- SDT gives a plain check for the lab: does each decision feel chosen (autonomy), is feedback informative about skill (competence), and is there a social tie (the board, the feed) (relatedness). Note the effect on future play is small in Ryan's data (R² = .10).
- The peak-end rule suggests the end of each era and of the 20-30 minute run should deliver the strongest, clearest moment (for example a model-release reveal) and a legible wrap-up; this extends a lab finding about pain to game sessions, which I did not find tested directly.
- Booth's "intensity governor" is a close structural match to the playtest complaint (overload then emptiness); the Director's randomized intervals also show how to get variety without clumping.

### Gaps
- No source found testing the peak-end rule on game sessions.
- Schell, Hodent and Csikszentmihalyi's primary texts were not read; Koster only in slide form.
- The DDA and gamification papers were seen only through search summaries.

## 5. Rules of thumb for a 20-30 minute real-time run: decisions per minute, overload and decision fatigue

### Takeaway
No source gives a number for "good" decisions per minute. The best-supported cautions are (a) people hold only about four chunks in working memory, (b) "choice overload" averages near zero across studies and depends on conditions, (c) "ego depletion" decision fatigue largely failed a large replication, and (d) production designers report both constant intensity and long lulls as bad. The sensible move is to shape the rhythm and measure it in playtests, not to pick a decisions-per-minute figure from the literature.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan (Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2001): evidence points to a single central capacity limit averaging about four chunks, rather than Miller's seven. — [Cowan 2001 via Europe PMC, PMID 11515286](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11515286) ⚠️ abstract only.
- Iyengar & Lepper (2000): people were more likely to buy jam or chocolate or do an optional essay when offered 6 options than 24 or 30, and reported greater satisfaction with limited sets. — [Europe PMC search record](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22When%20choice%20is%20demotivating%22) ⚠️ abstract only.
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (J Consumer Research, 2010): meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) found the mean effect of choice overload "virtually zero" with "considerable variance between studies", and "potentially important preconditions" but no "sufficient conditions". — [Geneva archive record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76440) ⚠️ abstract only via a tool summary; DOI 10.1086/651235.
- Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (J Consumer Psychology, 2015), as summarized in search results: with 99 observations (N = 7,202) they identify four moderators (choice-set complexity, decision-task difficulty, preference uncertainty, decision goal) and say that taking moderators into account the overall effect of assortment size is significant, in contrast to the earlier meta-analysis. — [Kellogg listing](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual) ⚠️ search-result summary only. Conflict between this and the 2010 mean-near-zero result is about moderators, not necessarily about the data.
- Hagger & Chatzisarantis et al. (Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2016): 23 labs, N = 2,141, pooled ego-depletion effect d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15] (includes zero), after earlier meta-analyses had questioned the effect. — [Registered Replication Report abstract, Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22multilab%20preregistered%20replication%20of%20the%20ego-depletion%22) (complete abstract read; DOI 10.1177/1745691616652873). This casts doubt on the strong claim that making many decisions drains a limited willpower resource; it does not show decision overload is harmless.
- Production observations: Booth's deck (see section 4) calls constant combat fatiguing and long inactivity boring; Chen's viewpoint says frequent forced choices can undermine flow. — [Booth](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf); [Chen](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/~lieber/courses/csu670/f08/materials/p31-chen-flow-in-games.pdf) ⚠️ partial read.
- Behavioral side: Hopson says activity level depends on how soon the participant expects a reward, so long flat stretches lower motivation, and recommends having multiple activities available. — [Hopson](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design)

### Inferences
These are my reasoning, labelled as such. None is a tested number.
1. Treat the complaint as a rhythm problem. Because event cards already stop the clock, the issue is not reaction speed but how many open decisions arrive close together. A pacing governor like the Director's (estimate load, delay new cards when load is high, schedule quiet after a cluster) addresses both halves of the complaint.
2. Cap how many unresolved choices the player must hold at once at about three or four, in line with the working-memory estimate. This is an extrapolation from lab memory tasks, not a game finding.
3. Keep option lists per card short but do not rely on "six beats 24": the choice-overload literature says the effect depends on complexity, difficulty and preference uncertainty, and the average is near zero. The relevant risk for the lab is difficult, preference-unclear choices clustering, not menu length.
4. Do not justify design on "decision fatigue" as depleted willpower; that mechanism did not replicate. Justify it on attention and working memory, and on playtest feedback.
5. Fill quiet stretches with visible progress and optional low-stakes activity (training-run progress, feed items) rather than with extra forced decisions; this follows Hopson's "multiple activities" advice and Booth's "long periods of inactivity are boring".
6. Make the model release and the era close the designed peaks, and end the run on a clear, positive, informative note (peak-end rule, extrapolated from pain studies).
7. Measure instead of guessing: log time-stamped decisions in playtests, compute decisions per 60-second window, and set a target for the maximum window and for the longest zero-decision gap; then compare the playtest reports. No literature figure replaces this.
8. For honesty in a teaching game: avoid the Zagal et al. patterns (appointment pressure, grinding, withering/decay obligations), and avoid near-miss framing on scores. Keep the review-score reveal explanatory.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies a comfortable decisions-per-minute range for a real-time management game; I found none for pausable real-time games specifically.
- The Danziger "judges and lunch" study (a frequently cited decision-fatigue example) was not examined.
- Interruption and task-switching cost literature was not covered.
- Chernev et al. 2015 and Guo et al. were seen only as search summaries.
