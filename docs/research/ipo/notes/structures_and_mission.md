# Corporate structures and the mission-driven AI lab after an IPO

Research note for the Game Night AI Lab IPO arc. Compiled 2026-10-27. This is research, not game design; the only design material is the marked-as-inference section near the end.

Conventions: "⚠️" marks a claim that rests on a source I could read only in part (or not at all). "News-only" marks a claim whose only source is journalism. "Not verified" means I looked and could not confirm it.

---

## Summary (the most game-relevant findings)

1. **A real AI-lab IPO filing now exists.** Anthropic, PBC filed a public Form S-1 (the registration statement a company files with the SEC before an IPO) on **October 26, 2026**, to list Class A stock on Nasdaq under "ANT." It shows a three-layer control design: a financially disinterested trust (the Long-Term Benefit Trust) elects 4 of 7 directors through a special Class T share; a founders' LLC holds a single Class F share carrying **50.1% of all general votes**; employees get a narrow-purpose Class E vote; public Class A holders "generally will not be able to affect the outcome" of votes ([S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm), ⚠️ partial read, see coverage).
2. **The trust can be switched off, but only by stacked supermajorities.** The trust and its Class T share can be amended or redeemed (bought back for nothing) by 80% of the board plus 80% of trustees, or, without the trustees, by founders plus employees plus public holders at set thresholds, or by 85% of Class A shares once the founders' class is gone (same S-1).
3. **OpenAI's nonprofit keeps control through a special share and state attorneys general.** Since **October 28, 2025**, the OpenAI Foundation (the nonprofit) appoints and removes every director of OpenAI Group PBC, holds about 26% of its equity (Microsoft about 27%), and its Safety and Security Committee can halt model releases even when risk thresholds would allow them. California's agreement ties this control to the foundation keeping its "Class N Common Stock" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/our-structure/); [Delaware AG](https://news.delaware.gov/2025/10/28/ag-jennings-completes-review-of-openai-recapitalization/); [California MOU](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/Final%20Executed%20MOU%20Between%20OpenAI%20and%20California%20AG%20re%20Notice%20of%20Conditions%20of%20Non-Objection%20%2810.27.2025%29%20%28Signed%20by%20OpenAI%29%20%28Signed%20by%20CA%20DOJ%29.pdf)).
4. **OpenAI filed confidentially but its CEO called an IPO "ill-advised" on safety grounds.** OpenAI said on June 8, 2026 that it had submitted a confidential S-1; on September 12, 2026 Sam Altman said an IPO now would be "ill-advised," the same day Anthropic's CEO published an essay urging labs to pace capability gains (news-only: [CNBC Jun 8](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/08/openai-confidentially-files-for-ipo-prepping-wall-street-for-ai-debut.html), [CNBC Sep 12](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/12/anthropics-amodei-proposes-plan-to-slow-the-pace-of-advancing-ai-capabilities.html)).
5. **Public benefit corporation (PBC) status alone is a weak lock.** Since July 16, 2020, Delaware lets shareholders drop PBC status by simple majority; only holders of 2% (or $2 million for listed firms) can sue over the "balancing" duty; and as of 2025 there were no reported court opinions on PBC governance. Allbirds, a listed PBC, removed its PBC status on **June 15, 2026** while pivoting to AI infrastructure ([8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1653909/000119312526273417/d178962d8k.htm)). xAI quietly dropped its Nevada benefit-corporation status by May 9, 2024 (news-only).
6. **Control has to be built before listing day.** Nasdaq Rule 5640 bars a listed company from reducing existing shareholders' voting rights, including by issuing new super-voting stock. A lab that lists without its control devices cannot easily add them later ([Nasdaq rulebook](https://listingcenter.nasdaq.com/rulebook/nasdaq/rules/nasdaq-5600-series), ⚠️ partial).
7. **Mission owners do intervene in listed companies.** In November 2025 the Novo Nordisk Foundation and Novo Holdings replaced Novo Nordisk's chair, vice chair and five other directors at an extraordinary general meeting ([EGM notice](https://www.novonordisk.com/content/dam/nncorp/global/en/investors/irmaterial/egm/novo-nordisk-egm-2025-notice.pdf), ⚠️ partial).
8. **Benefit sharing has moved from proposal to filing.** The 2020 "Windfall Clause" proposal (donate a rising share of extreme profits) now has a listed-company cousin: Anthropic's board approved issuing up to 2% more stock to its own Public Benefit Fund each time the share price rises 50% above the prior milestone (S-1).

## 1. Dual-class and multi-class shares

**How it works.** A company issues two or more classes of common stock with equal economic rights but unequal votes. Insiders keep high-vote shares; the public buys low-vote or no-vote shares.

- **Google (2004).** Google's original S-1, filed **April 29, 2004**, set Class A at one vote per share and Class B at ten. The founders' letter said the structure "will make it harder for outside parties to take over or influence Google" and that new investors "will have less influence over its strategic decisions than they would at most public companies" ([Google S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1288776/000119312504073639/ds1.htm), ⚠️ read only the cover page and the founders' letter section "Corporate Structure").
- **Facebook/Meta (2012).** The IPO prospectus (filed May 18, 2012) gave Class B ten votes; Class B holders held about 96.0% of the vote and Mark Zuckerberg controlled about 55.9%. Facebook declared itself a "controlled company" and skipped an independent nominating committee ([Facebook 424B4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000119312512240111/d287954d424b4.htm), ⚠️ cover page only).
- **Snap (2017).** The prospectus dated **March 1, 2017** offered **non-voting** Class A stock to the public; founders' Class C stock (ten votes) held about 88.5% of voting power ([Snap 424B4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1564408/000119312517068848/d270216d424b4.htm), ⚠️ cover page only).
- **SpaceX (2026), which now owns xAI.** SpaceX, a Texas corporation, priced its Nasdaq IPO at $135 per share in a prospectus dated **June 11, 2026**. Class B carries ten votes and elects a majority of the board; Elon Musk held about 82.4% of voting power after the offering, and SpaceX relies on the controlled-company exemptions. Under a 2025 Texas statute its bylaws require a shareholder group to own **3% of outstanding shares (about $53 billion at the IPO price)** to bring a derivative suit (a suit on the company's behalf against its own directors) ([SpaceX 424B4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026042639/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm), ⚠️ read the cover and the derivative-suit risk factor only). CNBC reports SpaceX acquired xAI earlier in 2026 (news-only, [CNBC Sep 12](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/12/anthropics-amodei-proposes-plan-to-slow-the-pace-of-advancing-ai-capabilities.html)).

**Sunset clauses.** A sunset ends the extra votes after a set time or event. Bebchuk and Kastiel argue that the benefits of dual-class stock "tend to recede" and the costs "tend to rise" as time passes from the IPO, that controllers have "perverse incentives" to keep the structure, and propose sunsets after a fixed period such as ten or fifteen years unless unaffiliated shareholders vote to extend ([Virginia Law Review abstract](https://virginialawreview.org/articles/untenable-case-perpetual-dual-class-stock/), ⚠️ abstract only; the full article was not opened). Anthropic's founder class has an event-based sunset: it ends 450 days after fewer than three "Continuing Founders" remain for 90 days (Section 4 below).

**Index providers' responses.**
- **S&P, July 31, 2017:** companies with multiple share classes became ineligible for new addition to the S&P Composite 1500 (including the S&P 500); existing members were grandfathered. FTSE Russell proposed on July 26, 2017 a rule requiring more than 5% of voting rights to be held by free-float shareholders ([Davis Polk via Harvard Law Forum, Aug 2, 2017](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2017/08/02/snap-decision-leading-index-providers-nix-multi-class-shares/); a law-firm summary, not the S&P notice itself).
- **S&P reversal, April 17, 2023:** "all companies with multiple share class structures will be considered eligible candidates" again; tracking stocks stay excluded ([S&P DJI press release](https://press.spglobal.com/2023-04-17-S-P-Dow-Jones-Indices-Announces-Results-of-S-P-Composite-1500-Index-Consultation-on-Share-Class-Eligibility-Rules)).
- **FTSE Russell's 5% rule (current, v2.4 April 2026):** developed-market companies need "greater than 5%" of total voting rights, counting unlisted high-vote shares, in free-float hands. It applied to new entrants from the September/October 2017 reviews (the FAQ gives both); grandfathering ended September 2022 (Russell US from the June 2023 reconstitution). Failing companies are removed at the June review and can be re-tested after one year ([FTSE Russell FAQ](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/policy-documents/minimum-voting-rights-hurdle-faq.pdf)). Anthropic's S-1 warns its "multi-class capital structure may make us ineligible for inclusion in indices."

**"Controlled company" exemptions.** Under Nasdaq Rule 5615(c), a company is "controlled" when more than 50% of the voting power for electing directors is held by an individual, group or another company. It is then exempt from the rules requiring a majority-independent board and fully independent compensation and nominating committees (audit-committee rules still apply) ([Nasdaq 5600 series](https://listingcenter.nasdaq.com/rulebook/nasdaq/rules/nasdaq-5600-series), ⚠️ read Rule 5615 and Rule 5640 only). Anthropic qualifies because the trust elects a board majority, but says it will use the exemption only for its nominating and governance committee. The parallel NYSE rule (Section 303A.00) is **not verified**: the NYSE manual page returned a 404.

**Evidence on performance and governance.**
- Cremers, Lauterbach and Pajuste (Review of Corporate Finance Studies 13(2):459–493) study U.S. firms 1980–2019: dual-class firms have higher mean valuations at IPO, possibly from founders' "unique vision," but the premium "tends to dissipate" as firms age, possibly because the gap between insiders' votes and cash-flow rights widens ([ECGI abstract page](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/paper-publications/the-life-cycle-of-dual-class-firm-valuation), ⚠️ abstract only; the full paper PDF was blocked. A secondary claim that the premium turns into a discount about six years after IPO came from a search snippet and is **not verified**).

## 2. Public benefit corporations (PBCs)

**The Delaware statute** (Title 8, Chapter 1, Subchapter XV, §§361–368; [Delaware Code](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc15/index.html), read in full):
- **§362, §365(a) — the balancing duty.** A PBC is a for-profit corporation whose board must manage it "in a manner that balances the pecuniary interests of the stockholders, the best interests of those materially affected by the corporation's conduct, and the specific public benefit or public benefits identified in its certificate of incorporation."
- **§365(b) — weak enforcement by design.** Directors owe no duty to anyone because of that person's interest in the public benefit, and a balancing decision satisfies their duties if it is "informed and disinterested and not such that no person of ordinary, sound judgment would approve."
- **§365(c).** Absent a conflict of interest, failing to balance is not bad faith or a loyalty breach for exculpation and indemnification purposes, unless the charter says otherwise.
- **§366 — the biennial benefit report.** At least every two years the PBC must give stockholders a statement of its objectives, its measurement standards, factual information against those standards, and an assessment. Making it public or using a third-party standard is optional unless the charter or bylaws require it.
- **§367 — who can sue.** A suit to enforce the balancing duty needs plaintiffs owning at least 2% of outstanding shares, or for a listed company the lesser of 2% or $2,000,000 of market value.
- **§362(c).** The usual notice that shares are PBC shares is not needed for SEC-registered offerings.

**The 2020 amendments (effective July 16, 2020).** Conversion into or out of PBC status originally needed 90% of outstanding stock, cut to two-thirds in 2015. The 2020 amendments deleted those rules, so conversions now default to a simple majority under §§242(b) and 251; they also removed appraisal rights for conversions and made exculpation of balancing failures the default ([Ropes & Gray via Harvard Law Forum, Aug 31, 2020](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2020/08/31/delaware-public-benefit-corporations-recent-developments), read in full). Fisch and Solomon note the same amendment (House Bill 341, signed July 16, 2020).

**Do public PBCs resist shareholder pressure? Evidence and cases.**
- **Allbirds (2026).** Allbirds agreed on March 29, 2026 to sell its brand assets for an assumed $39.0 million and pivot to an "Electronics Infrastructure Business." Its proxy asked shareholders to remove the PBC language because the new business "would be less focused on the public benefit of environmental conservation," and warned of "derivative claims or other disputes relating to our change in ... public benefit orientation" ([DEFM14A, May 8, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1653909/000119312526213226/d39753ddefm14a.htm), ⚠️ read the notice, the purpose section and the PBC risk factor only). Shareholders approved on June 3; the charter amendment took effect June 15, 2026; the company became Smartbird, Inc., hired a CEO with AI-infrastructure experience, and cut the stockholder-meeting quorum from a majority to one-third ([8-K, Jun 17, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1653909/000119312526273417/d178962d8k.htm), read in full). This is the clearest verified case of a listed PBC abandoning its benefit purpose.
- **Etsy (2015–2017).** Not a PBC but a certified B Corp that had said it intended to become one. After weak results, the board replaced the CEO, an activist (Black-and-White Capital) took a board seat, and the company dropped its B Corp certification. Fisch and Solomon call this "real evidence that the current PBC form can readily be usurped by market forces" ([Fisch & Solomon 2021](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/fischsolomonfinal_1.pdf), read in full).
- **Ben & Jerry's (2000).** A listed, mission-driven (non-PBC) company sold to Unilever; the founders lacked majority control and "could not block a takeover" (same source).
- **Scholarly view.** Fisch and Solomon conclude that the PBC "preserves the shareholder primacy norm": shareholders alone elect directors, approve mergers, can convert out by simple majority, and alone can sue; purpose statements of the large PBCs are "too vague and aspirational to be legally significant." Dorff (2025 symposium reply, read in full) agrees enforcement is weak but argues the form reinforces mission culture. He reports "the complete absence of any reported judicial opinions involving the governance of BCs or PBCs," that most recently listed PBCs trade below their IPO price, that Zymergen and AppHarvest went bankrupt within a few years of listing, and that about a quarter of the public PBCs he listed have faced federal securities class actions ([Dorff, Southwestern Law Review](https://www.swlaw.edu/sites/default/files/2026-04/A%20Strong%20First%20Step-%20Public%20Benefit%20Corporations%20And%20Governance%20Reform.pdf)).
- **Activist attack on a listed PBC's mission:** I found no verified case beyond Allbirds' own board-led exit. **Not verified.**

**xAI.** xAI was formed as a Nevada benefit corporation in 2023; Nevada records obtained by the group LASST show the status gone as of May 9, 2024, and the X/xAI merger articles dated March 28, 2025 had no benefit-corporation structure. Musk's lawyer still described xAI as a public benefit corporation in a May 2025 court filing (news-only: [CNBC, Aug 25, 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/25/elon-musk-xai-dropped-public-benefit-corp-status-while-fighting-openai.html); I did not open the Nevada records). Its parent SpaceX listed in June 2026 as an ordinary Texas corporation (Section 1).

## 3. OpenAI's structures

All dates below are from OpenAI's own posts unless marked.

- **2015 — nonprofit.** "OpenAI was founded in 2015 as a nonprofit" ([our-structure](https://openai.com/our-structure/)). The December 2024 post adds that donations included $137M in cash ("less than a third of which was from Elon") ([Dec 27, 2024](https://openai.com/index/why-our-structure-must-evolve-to-advance-our-mission/)).
- **March 11, 2019 — OpenAI LP, "capped-profit."** Investors and employees get "a capped return," and "any returns beyond that amount ... are owned by the original OpenAI Nonprofit entity." "Returns for our first round of investors are capped at 100x their investment," with lower multiples expected later; caps were "negotiated in advance on a per-limited partner basis." Controls: the LP's "primary fiduciary obligation is to advance the aims of the OpenAI Charter"; all investors and employees signed agreements that the Charter "always comes first, even at the expense of some or all of their financial stake"; only a minority of board members could hold financial stakes, and only those without stakes could vote where investor interests and the mission might conflict, including on payouts ([OpenAI LP](https://openai.com/index/openai-lp/)).
- **November 17, 2023 — board crisis.** The nonprofit board (Ilya Sutskever, Adam D'Angelo, Tasha McCauley, Helen Toner) removed Sam Altman, saying he "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board"; Mira Murati became interim CEO ([OpenAI, Nov 17, 2023](https://openai.com/index/openai-announces-leadership-transition/)). The post dated **November 29, 2023** announced Altman's return and a "new initial board" of Bret Taylor (chair), Larry Summers and Adam D'Angelo, a non-voting Microsoft observer, and an independent review (law firm WilmerHale named Dec 8, 2023). Altman wrote that Microsoft was "ready to welcome all of us if we couldn't achieve our primary goal" ([OpenAI, Nov 29, 2023](https://openai.com/index/sam-altman-returns-as-ceo-openai-has-a-new-initial-board/)). The exact date the return was agreed (reported as Nov 21–22) is **not verified** from a primary source.
- **December 27, 2024 — plan to convert.** Plan: turn the for-profit into a Delaware PBC "with ordinary shares of stock"; the nonprofit's interest would become PBC shares "at a fair valuation"; "Investors want to back us but ... need conventional equity and less structural bespokeness." The PBC would "run and control OpenAI's operations and business" ([Dec 27, 2024](https://openai.com/index/why-our-structure-must-evolve-to-advance-our-mission/)). Delaware's AG says she opened her review on **October 9, 2024**, and that OpenAI's first plan "would remove the current nonprofit entity from overseeing the for-profit."
- **May 5, 2025 — reversal.** The nonprofit "will continue to be overseen and controlled by that nonprofit," decided "after hearing from civic leaders and ... the Attorney General of Delaware and the Attorney General of California." Altman: the capped-profit design "made sense when it looked like there might be one dominant AGI effort but doesn't in a world of many great AGI companies" ([May 5, 2025](https://openai.com/index/evolving-our-structure/)).
- **October 28, 2025 — recapitalization.** The nonprofit became the **OpenAI Foundation**; the for-profit became **OpenAI Group PBC**. The Foundation "appoints all members of the board of directors of OpenAI Group and can replace directors at any time" through special voting rights; it holds a **26%** stake (about $130B) plus a warrant paying "significant additional equity" if the share price rises more than tenfold after 15 years; **Microsoft holds roughly 27%**; the remaining 47% is held by current and former employees and investors. The Safety and Security Committee (SSC) stays a Foundation committee; its chair, Zico Kolter, sits only on the Foundation board and observes the Group board ([our-structure](https://openai.com/our-structure/); [Built to benefit everyone](https://openai.com/index/built-to-benefit-everyone/)). The Foundation's initial focus is a $25B commitment to health and "AI resilience."

**The attorneys general's conditions.**
- **Delaware** (Statement of No Objection, Oct 28, 2025; read in full): the nonprofit keeps "the sole power and authority to appoint" and remove PBC directors; PBC directors "will be required to consider only the mission (and may not consider the pecuniary interests of stockholders or any other interest) with respect to safety and security issues"; the SSC can "require mitigation measures—up to and including halting the release of models or AI systems—even where the applicable risk thresholds would otherwise permit release"; within one year at least two Foundation directors (including the SSC chair) will not sit on the PBC board; the AG gets "advance notice of significant changes in corporate governance." Delaware's independent financial adviser was Moelis & Company ([Delaware AG](https://news.delaware.gov/2025/10/28/ag-jennings-completes-review-of-openai-recapitalization/)).
- **California** (MOU dated Oct 27, 2025; [press release](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-issues-statement-openai%E2%80%99s-recapitalization-plan) and MOU read in full): control holds "**so long as the NFP holds Class N Common Stock**," and "the decision whether to relinquish the Class N Common Stock, if ever, is in the control of the NFP Board." While it holds Class N, the nonprofit's prior written approval is needed to change the PBC's public-benefit article, sell the company or material assets, issue more Class N, or create any new class of stock that votes for directors (except Class B stock in acquisitions). The SSC gets "an effective approval right" over PBC safety and security actions by contract. The PBC board must have a majority of independent directors and fully independent audit, compensation and nominating committees. The nonprofit must give the AG **21 days' notice** before consenting to a change of control, a mission change, a charter change that removes the Class N appointment right, or moving headquarters out of California. The PBC must publish annual public mission-progress reports; senior PBC staff meet the AG's office quarterly.

**2026: IPO preparations (news-only unless stated).**
- OpenAI said it "recently submitted a confidential S-1. ... We have not decided on timing yet; it may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company" (quoted by [CNBC, June 8, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/08/openai-confidentially-files-for-ipo-prepping-wall-street-for-ai-debut.html)). CNBC also reports a $852 billion post-money valuation, work with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, a planned employee tender offer, and that a jury in Musk's suit found his claims time-barred, with the judge adopting the verdict.
- On September 12, 2026 Altman told Fortune an IPO now would be "ill-advised," citing growing AI-safety concerns; CFO Sarah Friar had told employees in August the company would likely list in 2027 or sooner ([CNBC, Sep 12, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/12/anthropics-amodei-proposes-plan-to-slow-the-pace-of-advancing-ai-capabilities.html)). I did not open the Fortune interview.
- EDGAR (the SEC's filing database) shows no public registrant named "OpenAI Group PBC" as of this search (command output, Oct 27, 2026). Confidential draft filings are not public, so this does not prove there is no filing.
- **How Foundation control would survive a listing:** no public OpenAI prospectus exists, so **not verified**. What is verified is that the California MOU makes control depend on the Foundation's Class N stock, forbids new director-voting classes without the Foundation's consent, and requires notice before any change of control. Inference: if listed, OpenAI would likely look like Anthropic's or Meta's model (public holds low-vote or non-director-voting shares) and would be a Nasdaq/NYSE "controlled company."

## 4. Anthropic and the Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT)

**Before the IPO (Anthropic's own post, Sept 19, 2023, read in full):** Anthropic has been a Delaware PBC with the purpose "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." At its Series C it created **Class T** stock held only by the Trust, which elects a number of directors that phases in "according to time- and funding-based milestones; in any event, the Trust will elect a majority of the board within 4 years." The Trust is a Delaware common-law "purpose trust" of five financially disinterested trustees. "Failsafe" provisions allow changes "without the consent of the Trustees if sufficiently large supermajorities of the stockholders agree," with thresholds rising as the Trust's power phases in ([Anthropic LTBT post](https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-long-term-benefit-trust)).

**Published critique.** Zach Stein-Perlman (May 27, 2024) argued that because the Trust Agreement and thresholds were unpublished, "Public information is consistent with the Trust being quite subordinate to stockholders," and that a few large investors (possibly Amazon and Google) might easily reach a supermajority; he asked Anthropic to publish the agreement ([EA Forum mirror](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/JARcd9wKraDeuaFu5/maybe-anthropic-s-long-term-benefit-trust-is-powerless), post read in full; comments only partly). The S-1 now publishes the post-IPO thresholds (below), which answers part of this critique for the listed company; the Trust Agreement text itself is still **not verified** as public.

**IPO preparations (EDGAR, primary).** Draft registration statement (DRS) filed June 1, 2026; amendments July 13, Aug 10, Sept 4, Oct 1 and Oct 9, 2026; **public S-1 on October 26, 2026** (EDGAR filing index, command output). Proposed listing: Nasdaq Global Select Market, symbol "ANT." Lead underwriters on the cover: Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, J.P. Morgan, Citigroup, and others. Price range and share count are blank. Lawyers: Wilson Sonsini (company) and Freshfields (underwriters).

**How the LTBT works once listed** (all from the [S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm); ⚠️ I read the cover, the table of contents, the "Risks Related to our Governance Structure" risk factors, the whole "Our Governance Structure and Its Goals" section, parts of "Management," and the share-class comparison table, about 110,000 of 1.56 million characters; I did not read "Description of Capital Stock" in full):

- **Share classes.** Class A: one vote, publicly traded. Class N: same economics, no vote, held mainly by strategic partners, converts to Class A on sale to an unaffiliated buyer. Class T: held solely by the LTBT, no economic rights, elects the "Class T directors." Class F: a single share held by a founders' LLC, no economic rights, carries **50.1% of total voting power** on general matters, undiluted by future issuances. Class E: issued to non-founder employees (one share per six months of service, plus shares matched to charitable pledges), no economic rights, votes only on (a) the "Specified Class T Director" when the nominating committee disagrees with the Trust and (b) amendments to or redemption of the Trust and Class T.
- **Board.** Seven seats after the IPO: four Class T directors (Reed Hastings, Vas Narasimhan, Christopher Liddell, and one vacancy) elected and removable by the Trust; three elected by Class A and Class F voting together (Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Yasmin Razavi). Before the IPO, the Trust had designated three of six directors. One Class T seat (Liddell, the "Specified Class T Director") needs the nominating committee's concurrence, failing which Class E employees vote.
- **Who can replace the CEO.** The board, a majority of which the Trust elects. The founders' LLC elects the other three seats and decides "all matters submitted to a vote of our stockholders generally (including the approval of any merger, sale of our company ...) for the foreseeable future."
- **Who can force a sale.** Nobody outside: a merger needs board approval (Trust-majority board) and a stockholder vote (founders' 50.1%). The S-1 says the structure "is likely to have the effect of limiting the likelihood of an unsolicited merger proposal, unsolicited tender offer, or proxy contest."
- **The failsafe, now with numbers ("Trust Amendment Threshold").** The Trust's terms and Class T rights can be amended, or Class T redeemed for no consideration, by: (1) if trustees agree, 80% of the board and 80% of trustees; (2) if trustees object and Class F exists: in the first three years, unanimous Continuing Founders plus a majority of Class E votes cast plus a majority of Class A votes cast; at any time, two-thirds of Continuing Founders plus 75% of Class E votes cast plus a majority of Class A votes cast; (3) if Class F is gone and trustees object, **85% of outstanding Class A shares**. Class T can also be transferred, without restriction, on the same approvals. The "Class T Sunset Time" never happens automatically.
- **Founder sunset.** A "Class F Transition Period" of 450 days starts when fewer than three Continuing Founders remain for 90 consecutive days; then Class F and Class E are redeemed for nothing. A founder drops out on resignation, death or incapacity, removal for cause (including specified competitive activities), or selling down below 2% of their IPO-date Class A holdings. After the founder sunset, the board becomes staggered (three classes, three-year terms) and some charter changes need two-thirds of Class A.
- **Trustees.** Five seats, one vacant: Neil "Buddy" Shah (chair since June 2023), Richard Fontaine (since April 2025), Ben Bernanke (since June 2026), Christopher Stubbs (since October 2026). Trustees pick their own successors, serve annual terms, are paid $400,000 a year (chair +$25,000), may not buy company stock, and their pay may not depend on company profits. Anthropic funds the Trust: $2,716,750 (2023), $0 (2024), $906,089 (2025), $8,425,213 (first nine months of 2026). Anthropic gave $450,000 to CNAS, which Fontaine leads, in August 2026.
- **Discrepancies to flag.** Anthropic's LTBT web page says Fontaine joined in May 2025 and Bernanke in July 2026, and that Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar joined in January 2026; the S-1 says April 2025 and June 2026 and does not list Cuéllar. I could not find why; **not verified**.
- **Safety commitments the S-1 says may cost money.** The S-1 discloses that Anthropic forgoes uses for "fully autonomous weapons" or "mass surveillance," released its "Mythos" model only as a gated research preview, and chose not to build image and video generation, and warns these "may not maximize stockholder value." It adds a new safety and risk committee of two independent directors overseeing model safety and security.
- **Benefit sharing.** Anthropic contributed 846,840,960 Class N shares (5% of fully diluted shares as of June 30, 2026) to a wholly owned Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, and approved a framework to issue up to 2% more each time the share price rises 50% above the prior milestone. Each co-founder pledged 80% of personal equity to philanthropy.
- **Controlled company.** Yes, because the Trust elects a board majority; the only exemption it expects to use is for the nominating and governance committee.

**Pre-IPO news.** CNBC reports Anthropic closed a round at a $965 billion valuation before announcing its confidential filing in early June 2026 (news-only, [CNBC Jun 8](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/08/openai-confidentially-files-for-ipo-prepping-wall-street-for-ai-debut.html)).

## 5. Other mission-locking or control devices

- **Golden shares (UK).** Rolls-Royce's articles give a single £1 non-voting "Special Share" that may be held only by a Secretary of State or Crown nominee. Its written consent is needed to change key articles (including the "United Kingdom Control" article), to dispose of a material part of the nuclear-propulsion business or the group, or to wind the company up. The articles cap any foreign person's interest at 15% of votes, describing UK control as "a cardinal principle" ([Rolls-Royce articles](https://www.rolls-royce.com/~/media/Files/R/Rolls-Royce/documents/annual-report/2022/rr-holdings-plc-articles-of-association.pdf), ⚠️ read Article 18 and the start of Article 54 of a 77-page document; filed with the 2022 annual report, so current wording is not verified). The BAE Systems equivalent page was blocked by bot protection (**not verified**).
- **Foundation-controlled listed companies (Danish model).**
  - *Novo Nordisk.* Two share classes: each DKK 0.01 of A capital carries 10 votes and of B capital 1 vote (A capital DKK 107,487,200; B DKK 339,012,800). The notice calls the Novo Nordisk Foundation "the ultimate controlling shareholder" and Novo Holdings "the main shareholder." At the **November 14, 2025** extraordinary general meeting, convened after the company's announcement of October 21, 2025, the Foundation and Novo Holdings proposed every candidate; the chair, vice chair and five other directors did not stand; former CEO and Foundation chair Lars Rebien Sørensen was proposed as chair ([EGM notice](https://www.novonordisk.com/content/dam/nncorp/global/en/investors/irmaterial/egm/novo-nordisk-egm-2025-notice.pdf), ⚠️ read the first ~24,000 of ~45,000 characters; the rest is candidate biographies). Election results, and whether Novo Holdings holds all A shares, are **not verified**. My arithmetic: A shares carry about 76% of all votes.
  - *Carlsberg.* The Carlsberg Foundation's charter requires its board to "ensure that the foundation controls the majority of the votes in Carlsberg A/S." The charter required 51% of share capital from 1970; in 2007 it moved to more than 25% of capital plus at least 51% of votes, and in 2013 to at least 51% of votes only, so Carlsberg could raise equity. The founder's "Golden Words" ask that the brewery be run "regardless of immediate profit" ([Carlsberg Foundation](https://carlsbergfondet.dk/en/about-the-foundation/governance/ownership-of-carlsberg-as/), read in full).
  - *Bosch (unlisted).* The charitable Robert Bosch Stiftung holds about 94% of the capital but "has no influence on the strategic or business orientation"; about 93% of votes sit with Robert Bosch Industrietreuhand KG and about 7% with the founder's descendants ([Bosch](https://www.bosch.us/our-company/bosch-group-worldwide/), read in full). This split of money from votes is the "stewardship ownership" pattern.
- **Stewardship ownership (Patagonia, 2022).** On September 14, 2022, 100% of voting stock went to the Patagonia Purpose Trust and 100% of non-voting stock to the Holdfast Collective, which receives the profits. Founder Yvon Chouinard on going public: "What a disaster that would have been. Even public companies with good intentions are under too much pressure to create short-term gain" ([Patagonia](https://www.patagonia.com/ownership/), letter read in full).
- **Poison pills and staggered boards.** A poison pill (shareholder rights plan) dilutes any buyer who crosses an ownership threshold; a staggered board elects only a third of directors each year, so a hostile buyer needs two annual meetings to win a majority. I did not open a primary source on pill mechanics (the casebook page I tried returned nothing), so the general description is **not verified** here. Verified use: Anthropic's charter lets 80% of the board create "undesignated preferred stock," with Class F consent (that such "blank-check" preferred stock is the usual tool for a pill is general background, not sourced here), and switches to a staggered board after the founder sunset (S-1). Google's founders' letter likewise framed dual-class as takeover defense.
- **Chinese AI labs and state devices.**
  - *VIEs.* A variable interest entity is a structure in which a foreign shell company (often in the Cayman Islands) lists abroad and controls a China-based operating company only through contracts, not ownership. On July 30, 2021 SEC Chair Gensler required such issuers to disclose that investors "are not buying shares of a China-based operating company," and to disclose Chinese government permission to list ([SEC statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/gensler-2021-07-30), read in full).
  - *State "golden shares."* An op-ed by HKU law professor Angela Zhang reports that Weibo sold a 1% stake in April 2020 to an entity owned by the China Internet Investment Fund (set up by the Cyberspace Administration of China and the Ministry of Finance), which has taken board seats at ByteDance and Weibo; she notes such a stake "would normally enable the state investor to appoint a director," yet Weibo was still fined repeatedly ([Project Syndicate via Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2087306), read in full; publication date not shown on the page). The ByteDance 1% stake (reported as April 2021) is from news snippets only; **not verified**.
  - *Listed Chinese labs.* Zhipu AI (Knowledge Atlas Technology Joint Stock) began trading in Hong Kong on **January 8, 2026** as the first large-language-model developer to list; MiniMax was due to list the next day (news: [Yicai](https://www.yicaiglobal.com/news/zhipu-ai-soars-in-hong-kong-stock-market-debut-as-chinese-startup-becomes-worlds-first-llm-firm-to-go-public), read in full). The name "Joint Stock" suggests a PRC-incorporated company listing H shares rather than a VIE (inference; I did not open the prospectus). Whether either has a state golden share is **not verified**.

## 6. Capped-profit and benefit-sharing ideas

- **OpenAI's cap (2019–2025).** First-round investors were capped at 100x; caps were set per investor, with any excess going to the nonprofit (Section 3). OpenAI dropped it in 2025 for "a normal capital structure where everyone has stock." Its replacement is the Foundation's 26% stake plus a warrant that pays more if the share price rises over tenfold in 15 years, so the public-benefit claim now grows with success rather than kicking in above a cap.
- **The Windfall Clause (Centre for the Governance of AI, arXiv Dec 25, 2019, revised Jan 24, 2020).** An "ex ante commitment by AI firms to donate a significant amount of any eventual extremely large profits." The proposed schedule works like an income tax on profits measured as a share of gross world product (GWP): 0% below 0.1% of GWP, 1% of marginal profits from 0.1% to 1%, 20% from 1% to 10%, 50% above 10%. The authors estimate the expected present cost as only about 60% more than leading corporate philanthropy and argue it is legal under the business judgment rule. Objections they address: windfall "should just be taxed," and "firms will find a way to circumvent their commitments" ([O'Keefe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.11595); ⚠️ read the 6-page AIES short version in full, not the longer report).
- **Anthropic's Public Benefit Issuances (2026)** put a price-triggered version on a listed company's books: 5% to its Public Benefit Fund at the start, then up to 2% more per 50% share-price gain, at the board's discretion (S-1). The S-1 warns these issuances "may not result in any direct or measurable benefit to our business or our stockholders."
- **Independent critiques of the Windfall Clause** beyond the authors' own: I did not find or open one. **Not verified.**

## 7. How each structure limits or exposes a mission-driven lab once public

**Securities-law disclosure.** A prospectus must describe the structure and its risks. Anthropic devotes a risk-factor subsection and a dedicated chapter to it, and states that public Class A holders generally cannot affect any vote. A controlled company must disclose that it relies on the exemptions (Nasdaq 5615(c)). PBC meeting notices must say the company is a PBC (§366(a)). China-based VIE issuers must disclose that buyers hold a shell. Inference: once a lab's charter and trust thresholds are in an S-1, any later change becomes a disclosed, litigable event. The S-1 notes an exclusive Delaware forum for such disputes.

**Comparison table**

| Structure | Who controls | What it protects | What it exposes once public | Real example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary single-class corporation | Majority of shareholders | Nothing beyond directors' judgment | Activists, proxy fights, hostile bids; CEO replaceable by any coalition with a majority | Etsy (CEO replaced, B Corp dropped) |
| Dual-class / founder super-vote | Founders' high-vote class | Founder can block sales and keep the CEO | Valuation premium tends to fade; index exclusion risk (FTSE 5% rule); mission depends on founders' own choices | Google 2004, Meta 2012, Snap 2017, SpaceX 2026 |
| Delaware PBC alone | Shareholders (simple majority can exit since 2020) | Legal cover for directors who trade profit for purpose | Only 2%/$2M holders can sue; no reported cases; purpose can be voted away | Allbirds dropped PBC Jun 15, 2026 |
| PBC + dual-class | Founders | Mission as long as founders want it | Founders can themselves drop the mission | Allbirds (98.4% Class B at IPO), Veeva, Warby Parker |
| PBC + independent trust electing board majority (+ founder vote) | Trust (board majority) and founders (stockholder votes) | Board seats insulated from investors; takeover nearly impossible | Trust can be amended or redeemed by stacked supermajorities; investors with no voice may discount the stock; founder sunset changes the balance | Anthropic S-1, Oct 26, 2026 |
| Nonprofit parent with special share + AG oversight | Nonprofit board (appoints all PBC directors) | Mission-only standard on safety; safety committee can halt releases; AG notice before change of control | Control lasts only while the nonprofit keeps its Class N stock and its board chooses to; nonprofit and PBC boards overlap heavily | OpenAI Foundation / OpenAI Group PBC since Oct 28, 2025 |
| Capped profit | Nonprofit general partner | Excess returns flow to the mission | Caps proved hard to fund at scale and were dropped | OpenAI LP 2019–2025 |
| Industrial foundation majority-vote owner | Foundation board | Long-term strategy; no hostile takeover | Foundation can overrule the listed company's board | Novo Nordisk Nov 2025 EGM; Carlsberg |
| Stewardship ownership (votes held by purpose trust) | Purpose trust | Mission and independence | Not listed, so no public capital | Patagonia 2022; Bosch |
| State golden share | Government | National-security veto over sales and foreign control | Political interference; foreign-ownership caps limit investors | Rolls-Royce Special Share; Chinese CIIF 1% stakes |
| Benefit-sharing pledge (windfall clause, public benefit issuances) | Board discretion or contract | Shares the upside with the public | Dilution; enforcement depends on design | GovAI 2020 proposal; Anthropic Public Benefit Fund 2026 |

## What this suggests for a game (inference, not research)

- **The IPO is the last chance to build the lock.** Exchange rules (Nasdaq 5640) make adding super-votes after listing hard, so a pre-IPO "charter design" phase where the player chooses devices, each with a price, is realistic.
- **Each device has a matching weakness the board or market can attack:** PBC status (a simple-majority vote can remove it, as at Allbirds); a trust (stacked supermajorities can redeem it); a founder class (it sunsets when founders leave or sell); a nonprofit special share (it lasts only while the parent keeps it, under attorney-general watch).
- **Index inclusion and the stock price can be the cost of insulation.** More control lowers investor appeal (Anthropic's own risk factor; the fading dual-class premium).
- **New stakeholders become voting blocs.** Anthropic gives employees a narrow vote over trust changes; that maps to a seven-member board game with an employee faction.
- **Safety-driven delay is a real IPO event.** Altman's "ill-advised" remark, and Anthropic's disclosed forgone revenue (weapons, surveillance, a gated model release), are real precedents for "slow down, take the stock hit."
- **Benefit sharing tied to share-price milestones** (2% per +50%) is a ready-made mechanic that is both real and simple.
