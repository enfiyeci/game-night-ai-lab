# How games model IPOs, share issues and public markets

Research note for Game Night AI Lab, 2026-10-27. Scope: how strategy, tycoon, management and board games handle going public, issuing shares and living with a public market, and which mechanics turn the IPO into an arc rather than a cash button.

## Summary

The strongest finding is that almost no video game in this survey treats the IPO itself as an event. Where an IPO exists it is one action that raises money (Capitalism Lab's whole IPO page is three sentences: it raises funds, usually lifts the valuation, and can be reversed by buying back 100% ([Capitalism Lab, IPO](https://www.capitalismlab.com/subsidiary-dlc/initial-public-offering-ipo/))). The drama in these games lives after the listing: a share price that rivals can buy into, a board or chairman that can fire you, and dilution that slowly costs you control. Offworld Trading Company is the best model of the long tail: your own shares work as hit points, rivals buy them to eliminate you, and the developers spent months making that threat visible and fair after players complained of "surprise" buyouts ([Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)). Railroad Tycoon II is the best model of accountability: investors judge a five-year return and "may fire the chairman" if the stock price declines for long enough ([RT2 manual, ch. 8](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/http://vztimg.exent.com/Prem/products/172350/manual.pdf)). The board games (1830, Chicago Express, Acquire) model the clearest trade: selling shares is the only way to raise capital, and every share sold gives away part of the control and the profits. The most common failure is a share-price formula that the player can pump with their own trades or cash, which produced a "money cheat" in Sid Meier's Railroads! ([Gamewatcher review](https://www.gamewatcher.com/sid-meiers-railroads/review)), an infinite-money exploit in OpenTTD ([issue #7691](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/issues/7691)), and a confession from the Software Inc. developer that "making stuff up and patching exploits with weird hacks probably wasn't the best approach" ([Alpha 11.5.1 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3893864668621694055)). Of the AI-lab games, only Scaling Laws has a real funding model (valuation driven by frontier position, revenue and a market-sentiment cycle, with a down-round penalty), and none of them was verified to have an IPO.

## Terms used in this note

- **IPO (initial public offering):** the first sale of a company's shares to the public. After it, anyone with money can buy the shares.
- **Dilution:** issuing new shares makes each existing share a smaller slice of the company. Your percentage drops even though you sold nothing.
- **Buyback:** the company spends its own cash to buy its shares back, which shrinks the number of shares and raises each remaining holder's percentage.
- **Float:** the shares available to the public. In 18xx board games, "floating" a company means enough shares have been sold for it to start operating.
- **Tender offer:** an offer to buy shares directly from a holder, usually above the market price.
- **Hostile takeover:** a rival gains control by buying a majority of your shares without your agreement.
- **Dividend:** cash paid from company profits to shareholders.
- **Buying on margin:** borrowing money to buy shares, using the shares you already hold as collateral. A **margin call** forces you to sell when the collateral loses value.
- **Short selling:** selling borrowed shares, betting you can buy them back cheaper later.
- **Par value:** in 18xx games, the starting price per share the company's founder picks.
- **Poison pill:** any defence that makes a takeover too expensive or impossible.
- **Cap table:** the list of who owns what percentage of a company.
- **Down round:** raising money at a lower valuation than the previous round, which costs the founders extra ownership.

## Game by game

Each entry answers the same seven questions where the sources allow: what triggers public status; what the player decides; what moves the share price; what the market can do to the player; what the player can do back; how long it lasts in a session; and what players and critics say.

### Capitalism II and Capitalism Lab (Enlight)

**Trigger.** In Capitalism II the stock market is a new-game setting: "Allow Stock Investment" lets your corporation invest in others "but it will also be open to possible takeover and merger moves from other corporations" ([Capitalism II manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf), ⚠️ read only the setup, winning/losing, headquarters, Chapter 6 "Stock and Finances" and management-personality sections, not all 73 pages; the PDF's two-column layout also extracts with some interleaving). Capitalism Lab adds private companies: a company you found with the Billionaire Life DLC is "a private company that you own outright — no outside shareholders, no risk of a takeover until you take it public", and you "can hold an IPO down the road to raise outside money (and, of course, take on takeover risk again)" ([Playing Without a Company](https://www.capitalismlab.com/playing-without-company/)). The IPO page itself says only that an IPO raises funds, "in most cases" gives a higher valuation, and can be reversed by buying back 100% ([IPO](https://www.capitalismlab.com/subsidiary-dlc/initial-public-offering-ipo/); [Privatization](https://www.capitalismlab.com/subsidiary-dlc/privatization/)). With the Banking and Finance DLC, venture capitalists appear who "invest only in pre-IPO rounds" and "commit for the long term" ([Venture Capitalists](https://www.capitalismlab.com/banking-dlc/venture-capitalists/)).

**Decisions.** When issuing new shares you pick an issue price (it must be at or below the market price) and a quantity. The maximum quantity depends on profitability, the issue price, and the expected receipts, and "new shares cannot be issued too frequently. You must give the market time to absorb the new shares" ([manual, ch. 6](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)). You set a dividend payout ratio (it starts at zero and needs a Financial Department in your headquarters), your own CEO salary (capped by last year's profit), and spending on an Investor Relations Department that "help[s] improve your corporation's stock attractiveness" (same manual). The Banking DLC adds bonds, stock splits and special dividends ([Banking and Finance DLC](https://www.capitalismlab.com/?p=2241); [Special Dividends](https://www.capitalismlab.com/banking-dlc/special-dividends/)).

**Price.** The manual says "the market determines the stock price" and shows equity per share, earnings per share (based on operating earnings "because of game design considerations", so a company's own stock holdings don't distort it), price/earnings ratio, dividend yield and total return ([manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)). The exact formula is not given there (not verified). Capitalism Lab adds an "Alternative Stock Sim" option that makes prices ignore the economic state and bans trading between your company and its subsidiaries; "together they make it considerably harder to speculate for quick profits — which is the point of the option" ([Stock Market Enhancement](https://www.capitalismlab.com/new-content/stock-market-enhancement/)). A stock below $1.00 triggers an automatic reverse split (same page).

**What the market can do to you.** More than 50% ownership by anyone gives them control; at 75% they can merge your company out of existence. In a single-player game you lose immediately when someone acquires 50% or more ([manual, "Winning or Losing"](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)). AI CEOs have personality ratings, including "Corporation Takeover and Merger": "A high number in this area warns that your corporation would be at risk if the person is interested in your corporation" (same manual). The manual warns that issuing shares "could open your company to a hostile takeover". In Capitalism Lab's Billionaire Life DLC a takeover no longer ends the game: you can stay on as a salaried CEO, the new chairman reviews your results, sends one warning ("turn the company around by my next review, or I will replace you as CEO"), and fires you at the next review if results are still poor. An aggressive owner reviews more often; "you always get at least one full review cycle between the warning and any firing" ([Playing Without a Company](https://www.capitalismlab.com/playing-without-company/)).

**What you can do back.** Buy back shares, which "strengthens the owner's control of the corporation and reduces the risk of being taken over"; make tender offers (some holders "will refuse to sell their shares no matter how much you offer them because their main interest is in controlling the corporation"); and pay for a merger in cash or new shares, though paying in shares can, rarely, hand the target's holders control of your company ([manual, ch. 6](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)). Anti-manipulation rules stop you from using controlled corporations to buy stocks you or your corporation already own (same). After a takeover in Capitalism Lab, control returns to you if the acquirer sells below 50%, because "if nobody does [hold a majority], control falls to the company's Chief Executive — and that's you" ([Playing Without a Company](https://www.capitalismlab.com/playing-without-company/)).

**Length.** Not verified for a typical session.

**Player reports.** A Steam guide for Capitalism 2 describes a deliberate cycle: buy back your own shares early near the $10 starting price until you own 75–80%, then issue about 10% of the company at a time once the price is high (its example issues 181,000 shares at $208), and buy back again when cash is idle ("It's better to lose a little on the premium of issuance … than to miss the opportunity to raise a lot of cash in the future") ([Master in Capitalism 2](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2969285443), player report; ⚠️ read only the stock-related passages of a ~14,000-word guide; the page as served carried a notice that the guide had been removed from the community and was "only visible to you"). The same guide notes an AI competitor holding 22.6% of the player's company and buys control of that competitor to recover the shares. This shows the share system is a real lever, and also that a skilled player can turn issuing and buying back into a repeatable money pump.

### Offworld Trading Company (Mohawk Games)

**Trigger.** Every company is public from founding. Each company has ten shares, and players start owning two or three depending on player count ([OTC wiki, Stock](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock), community wiki built largely from a forum post by "Blues"). Founding later reduces your starting debt and raises your starting share price, so the moment you found works like choosing when to list ([OTC wiki, Debt](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Debt)).

**Decisions.** Buy rivals' shares to attack, buy your own shares to defend, or invest in the economy instead. The designer describes this as rush / turtle / boom, a rock-paper-scissors loop: buying a rival's stock early (rush) beats a player who poured money into growth (boom), buying your own "all-important fifth share" (turtle) beats the rush, and growth beats turtling ([Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)).

**Price.** Per the community wiki, the price trends toward total value divided by 100,000, where total value is headquarters value plus cash and resources plus structures, minus debt multiplied by five at standard difficulty; each share bought by others adds 5% (and the tenth adds much more), and shares you hold in others add to your own value; the wiki admits "the reasons for this are currently unclear" for diminishing returns at high values ([OTC wiki, Stock](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock)). Interest on debt depends on a bond rating set by the debt-to-value ratio ([OTC wiki, Debt](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Debt)). Black-market and patent options touch this layer: "Cook the Books" improves your bond rating by one step, and the "Financial Instruments" patent pays you 25% of rivals' interest ([Cook the Books](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Cook_the_Books); [Financial Instruments](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Financial_Instruments)).

**What the market can do to you.** If rivals together own six of your ten shares you are eliminated in a "majority buyout" and become a subsidiary: an AI that runs your company and pays its profits to the shareholders ([Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/); [Dev Diary: Taking Stock](https://forums.sorcererking.com/480133/dev-diary-taking-stock)). Shares you own in yourself can be bought from you one at a time at double price until five remain; the last five must be bought as one block, with a 20% surcharge per share owned by a third party ([Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)).

**What you can do back.** Buy your own shares (five self-owned shares block a majority buyout), pay down debt to raise your price (the wiki calculates when this beats buying stock), and attack the rival's income ([OTC wiki, Stock](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock); [Debt](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Debt)).

**Length.** Not verified for a typical session.

**Design history and reception.** The designer calls the stock market "the hardest part of the game design to get right", changed "only a few months before release". The first version, where a buyout gave you the loser's whole base, caused snowballing and a late-game standoff where everyone saved cash for the final buyout. The subsidiary system fixed both. A late change made each self-owned share act "the equivalent of hit points", so players "could easily see how close the game was to ending as they lost control of their own stock, one share at a time" ([Designer Notes #17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)). Stardock's dev diary says that in early access stock "led to a combination of issues involving snowballing … and standoffs in the late game" and that the majority and destroy buyout modes improved "both the middle and late stages of the game" ([Dev Diary](https://forums.sorcererking.com/480133/dev-diary-taking-stock); ⚠️ the diary text contains a "[[..]]" placeholder where some original text appears to be elided). Player reports from February 2015 early access describe losing "5th game in a row to a massive zerg rush stock buyout", going "from 8 unbought slots to whammo you lose in 15 seconds", and ask for a 30–60 second purchase cooldown; a poster named DeftMunky, writing as a team member (status not verified), replied that "the AI will buy your stock at a slower rate" in the next version and that the team would make the problem "more transparent" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/271240/discussions/0/618453594754245349/), player reports; ⚠️ read the first page of the thread as served).

### Software Inc. (Coredumping)

**Trigger.** Since Alpha 11.5 (March 2020), companies have real shares and "can decide whether or not they want to be publicly listed", and players "can also precisely choose how much shares they want to be available to the public" ([Software Inc. wiki, Stocks](https://softwareinc.coredumping.com/wiki/index.php/Stocks); the page is marked "WORK IN PROGRESS" and its takeover sections are empty headings).

**Decisions, price and pressure.** The developer's notes say the overhaul replaced five "stock slots" with actual shares, and "other companies can now start a hostile takeover of your company, if they own more than 50% of your shares" ([Alpha 11.5.1 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3893864668621694055)). In the Final Overhaul update (2026-10-04), AI companies gained CEOs who "can be replaced, either through retirement, directly with subsidiaries or through voting by share owners", and "CEOs can also cause controversy, to add some interest in the stock system" ([Final Overhaul update](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/1845383656391730)). The share-price formula was not found (not verified). No board meeting or IPO event was found in the sources read (not verified).

**What you can do back.** Not documented on the wiki beyond controlling how many shares are public (not verified further).

**Length.** Not verified.

**Reception and exploits.** The developer wrote: "I actually sat down and read about how the stock market works and overhauled the stock system … In hindsight, just making stuff up and patching exploits with weird hacks probably wasn't the best approach", and invited players to "report the numerous exploits this new system probably adds". The same notes record "Cross holding has been disallowed to avoid stock trading exploit" (Alpha 11.5.5) and "Fixed being able to keep shares in subsidiary company" (Alpha 11.5.8) ([Alpha 11.5.1 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3893864668621694055)). Adding CEO controversy "to add some interest in the stock system" six years later suggests the stock layer had felt flat ([Final Overhaul update](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/1845383656391730); the inference is mine).

### Startup Company (Hovgaard Games)

**Trigger.** There is no IPO (none found in the wiki or in the 146 Steam news posts searched; not verified beyond that). Funding comes from investors instead. You start with an investor, Johnson Invest, who pays out money as you reach milestones, or you can start without investors, which "will make the game much harder" ([Startup Company wiki, Investors](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Investors)). The milestones are a 25-step task ladder with cash rewards, from renting an office ($45,000) to becoming top #1 ($3,000,000), including "Reach $200,000 valuation" and "Acquire at least 1 competitor" ([Milestones](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Milestones)).

**Decisions.** From Beta 16 (June 2018), "you will now receive offers from other investors as your product grows. While the investors will own a share of your product, they will also instantly transfer you a ton of cash" ([Beta 16 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2499943313644354101)). Beta 22 added a time limit to investment offers ([Beta 22 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3140604542246097950)). Dropping below 51% means sacrificing "some control"; buying shares back costs "usually higher" than investors paid, and gets more expensive as you succeed ([wiki, Investors](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Investors)).

**What the market can do to you.** Investors take an equal cut when you move money to your retirement fund, which is the main objective of Career Mode (your CEO ages from 18 and retires at 67) ([Devlog #53](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2413289226965456109)). A "last chance before bankruptcy" offer appears only if you own more than 15% of your website, haven't bought out the investor, and no loans are available ([Beta 22 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3140604542246097950)). Per the full-release notes, websites "can only be sold if you've already bought out all the investors" ([Full release post](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3112492477890128289), ⚠️ found by keyword search of the post, not read in full).

**What you can do back.** Buy back investor shares. The wiki suggests a workaround: start a second website and buy out its investors before it becomes valuable, so their shares never appreciate ([wiki, Investors](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Investors)). This is a player-facing exploit of the rule that buyback price tracks valuation. You can also buy 100% of a competitor and merge its users into yours, a seven-day process ([Beta 16 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2499943313644354101)).

**Length.** A Career Mode run spans the CEO's working life (ages 18 to 67) ([Devlog #53](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2413289226965456109)); real-time length not verified.

**Reception.** The developer added a "Custom Game" mode partly so players who "don't like investors" can "turn it off" ([Beta 27 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2451574645865797519), ⚠️ found by keyword search, not read in full). The stock market for investing in other companies was "buggy" until Beta 28, which added a 1% trading fee for buying and selling ([Beta 28 notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2592316573877127704)). A trading fee is a simple brake on buy-and-sell loops.

### Railroad Tycoon II (PopTop, 1998), with a note on Railroad Tycoon 3

**Trigger.** You found a company with outside investors from the start: the tutorial reminds you that when you "chose to have outside investment as part of your initial funding … those investors have expectations for your operation, and they'll hold you accountable" ([RT2 manual](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/http://vztimg.exent.com/Prem/products/172350/manual.pdf), ⚠️ read the tutorial's finance section, the options page, Chapter 7's company and finance sections and Chapter 8 "The Stock Market" in full, but not the whole 4,340-line manual; original URL `http://vztimg.exent.com/Prem/products/172350/manual.pdf` no longer resolves, retrieved through the Internet Archive). A difficulty setting gates how fierce the market is: Basic lets you trade only your own stock and nobody can take anyone over; Advanced disables short selling and margin; Expert enables "all aspects", which "makes for some fairly vicious financial plays" (same manual).

**Decisions.** On the Finances tab: issue bonds (only with a credit rating of B or better), issue stock, buy back stock, change dividends ("the board of directors may not let you set this too high"), attempt a merger (once a year, decided by a shareholder vote that a higher offer or your own shares in the target can sway), or declare bankruptcy (half your debt disappears, but you lose all company cash and will "have a hard time issuing stock") (same manual). As a person you trade stock with your own money, on margin, or short (same).

**Price.** "Share price is determined by a number of factors … but most especially Earnings Per Share and Book Value Per Share. Recent sales and purchases of the stock will also temporarily drive it up or down." Selling several blocks in a row drops the price (same manual, ch. 8).

**What the market can do to you.** "Investors are very particular about stock value—they want it to go up. Period. Don't disappoint them, or you'll be out on your ear. Remember that chairmen are appointed, not born." The key metric is the five-year weighted return (dividends plus price change, weighted toward recent years): "If the stock price declines for long enough, investors may fire the chairman" (same manual). The manual names the cheap way to satisfy them: "maintain your profits while steadily buying back shares, driving up the share price." Computer players on Expert "know exactly what they are doing, and can bankrupt you if you get in over your head with borrowed money or borrowed stock", and a margin call forces sales that drive the price down further (same).

**What you can do back.** Buy back enough stock and you "will have sole control of your railroad", though this "can use up all your company's spare cash and leave you in control of a tiny shell while other company's grow" (same). Use a high dividend to "funnel cash from your company to yourself" if you own much of it (same).

**Length.** Scenario-driven; not verified for a typical session.

**Player reports and exploits.** On the FED2k forum (2007), a veteran player explains that prices move with the arrival of profitable trains, so you can buy before a wave of arrivals and sell before a lull, and describes taking 51% of AI railroads, making them float bonds to buy back their own stock, then selling his extra shares at the higher price. Another player suggests taking over a cash-rich rival, having it buy back its stock to burn its cash, and waiting for the inflated price to fall before buying it cheaply ([Workings of the stock market](https://forum.dune2k.com/topic/22720-workings-of-the-stock-market/), player reports). In a 2006–2019 thread, players report that a company left without a chairman is run by a "Committee of Shareholders" that spent over $10M in under six years, that the board's dividend cap annoys owners of 100% of a company ("What board? I am the board!"), and that the only way to extract money is buybacks to prop up the price "all while we have it on 'pause'" ([Committee of shareholders?](https://forum.dune2k.com/topic/22599-committee-of-shareholders/), player reports). Pausing to trade and to re-rig AI companies appears repeatedly; it lets players take market actions with no market reaction.

**Railroad Tycoon 3.** In the same 2007 thread a player asked whether the strategies apply to RT3 and the veteran answered "About the same" ([Workings of the stock market](https://forum.dune2k.com/topic/22720-workings-of-the-stock-market/), player report). RT3's stock rules were not verified from a primary source.

### Sid Meier's Railroads! (Firaxis, 2006)

**Trigger.** Companies are public from the start; you buy and sell shares in 10% blocks priced from "the value of the company in question, their assets and profits", and "a greater than 50% aggregate stake" takes a company over, either merged into your network or sold off ([PC Gamer reinstall, 2009](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/strategy/20-years-later-the-world-deserves-another-sid-meiers-railroads/)).

**Decisions.** The PC Gamer writer calls the interaction of three systems the game's "genius": "Need money? Sell shares. That's a risk. Want to take a controlling interest? Hoard cash and hope the value of your rivals doesn't outpace your savings." The AI exploits this by "triggering auctions you can't afford to drive you to sell stock" (same). A player explains that on Robber Baron difficulty each company starts with three shares (30%) that you can sell for capital "or keep them to protect yourself from a hostile takeover" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/7600/discussions/0/4904953131475940784/), player report).

**What the market can do to you.** Being bought out ends your game ([Gamewatcher review](https://www.gamewatcher.com/sid-meiers-railroads/review)). Players report that owning 100% of your own stock does not protect you: once no shares are on sale, a rival can pay double for all of them; one player says the AI "usually waits until it has about 1.5x the value required" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/7600/discussions/0/1637542668223689238/), player reports).

**Length.** Scenarios can be "played to completion in about a couple of hours" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/strategy/20-years-later-the-world-deserves-another-sid-meiers-railroads/)).

**Reception.** Mixed. Players in the takeover thread call the forced buyout "a very flawed mechanic" because company value ignores cash, so "the losing player can win by having more cash due to saving it by not expanding … That feels wrong to me and is why i stopped playing the game"; another replies that without forced buyouts, "as soon as everyone buys over 50% of their own stock, the game's over, a draw" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/7600/discussions/0/1637542668223689238/), player reports). The Gamewatcher reviewer called it "a rather crude representation of the stock market" and found that buying raises the price enough that "you actually make money by selling it", which he called "the most thoroughly obvious money cheat in the game" ([Gamewatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/sid-meiers-railroads/review)).

### OpenTTD and Transport Tycoon

**Mechanic.** In single player you could buy a company's shares 25% at a time for a quarter of its value, sell later, or buy 100% to take it over, gaining its vehicles, infrastructure and cash and also its debt; shares unlock once a company is six years old; in multiplayer you could buy only 75%, so human companies could not be taken over ([OpenTTD wiki, Economy](https://wiki.openttd.org/en/Manual/Economy)).

**Exploit.** Because company value included cash, a player with two companies could buy 75% of a dummy company for nothing, send it all their cash to inflate its value, sell the shares, and send the cash back, gaining about 75% each loop. Maintainers discussed valuing companies on fixed assets or income, or limiting how fast valuation can change ("e.g. 1% per month"), and closed the issue as not to be fixed in vanilla, noting servers can disable shares ([issue #7691](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/issues/7691)).

**Outcome.** The changelog for the 14.x series records: "Replace buying/selling company shares with hostile takeovers of AI companies" ([changelog](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/blob/master/changelog.md), ⚠️ found by searching the changelog for "share", not read in full). A 2024 community discussion proposing a new system says shares were "removed due to its bugs and possible cheats", and that under the old one "you just geht bought by another company, no real way to predict it"; a proposal there values companies on income history rather than cash, and treats shares as deals the owner chooses to make ([discussion #12751](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/discussions/12751), community proposals; ⚠️ some comments on the page did not load: "There was an error while loading").

**Length.** Not verified.

### Airline Tycoon 2

Patch 1.20 added share trading: sell shares of your airline "at a high price, invest in profitable competitors, or try to drive them out of business through a hostile takeover"; a "Bad Bank" buys tycoons' shares and makes them available to everyone; shareholders get dividends based on daily profit; owning at least 75% of a rival lets you dissolve it and either sell or absorb its planes; trading is only in free play ([Airline Tycoon 2 patch 1.20](https://store.steampowered.com/news/7102/)). Reception and session length not verified.

### Game Dev Tycoon, Mad Games Tycoon 2, Startup Panic, Rise of Industry, Big Pharma

- **Game Dev Tycoon:** the Steam description mentions no investors, stock or IPO ([store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/239820/)). ### AI-lab games

- **Scaling Laws (HCK Labs, free alpha 0.6.2, October 2026).** The richest funding model found. "Investors, loans and grants. Each has a price: a share of your company, a monthly fee, or conditions you have to keep." A state loan of up to $10B costs "almost three times as much" to repay, and "while you owe it, nobody can buy your company", which is a poison pill with a price. You can buy rivals' shares and "once you hold the majority, you can buy the whole company, newest model included", and rivals can do the same to you ([Scaling Laws site](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/)). The design notes give the valuation formula: `valuation = (frontier 4 × 2B + ARR × 20) × sentiment` (ARR is annual recurring revenue), so "raising in a hot market can therefore preserve far more founder ownership than raising the same amount during a cold one" ([How the simulation works](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/development), ⚠️ read only the section around funding, intelligence and testing, not the whole page). The roadmap lists as shipped: an investor sentiment cycle, a cap table with permanent founder dilution, a down-round penalty, debt facilities (bridge, venture debt, bond, sovereign compute debt) that "trade dilution for repayment pressure", and, as playable, "missed payments accumulate before a public default damages standing" ([roadmap](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/roadmap), ⚠️ read only the business and funding section of a long page). No IPO was found (not verified). Reception not verified.
- **AI Lab Tycoon (BT Games, Steam, listed for release on 2026-11-18).** The store page promises "funding offers, runway, equity, and reputation" and asks "Take investment now or hold out for better terms?"; you start "with nothing but a check from a skeptical investor" ([store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5015590/)). No IPO or stock price is mentioned. Unreleased, so mechanics are not verified.
- **AI Lab Tycoon: Misaligned (Gravenpall, Android).** The site describes training runs, paradigm shifts, a hidden-ish "P(doom) / Misalign" counter, and four endings; it mentions no funding rounds, IPO or stock price ([ailabtycoon.com](https://ailabtycoon.com)). It is a useful comparison for the hidden-pressure idea: the counter "is hidden-ish. It is patient. It is keeping score."
- **Singularity: AI Tycoon (Velore Games, per a third-party listing).** A turn-based quarterly game where you "raise capital, corner markets, and outmaneuver rival labs", rivals "poach your people, steal your breakthroughs, smear you in the press", and "alignment is never a number you can see" ([APKPure listing](https://apkpure.net/singularity-ai-tycoon/com.baz.singularity), third-party page, not an official store). No IPO or stock price is mentioned; funding mechanics not verified.
- **Universal Paperclips.** A stage-one "Investments" engine, unlocked by the Algorithmic Trading project: you deposit funds, pick low, medium or high risk, and an automated engine buys and sells random stocks; you have "no control over the table of stocks — it's just a visual"; you upgrade it with "yomi" from Strategic Modeling. A "Hostile Takeover" project ("Global Fasteners acquired, public demand increased x5") is a one-off purchase that the wiki says practically requires the investment engine ([Investment](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Investment); [Hostile Takeover](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Hostile_Takeover)). This is the cash-button model in its purest form: a money multiplier and a one-shot purchase, with no market acting back on you. That fits Paperclips' theme of a number that only grows; it would not fit a game that wants the IPO to bite.

### Board games

- **Acquire (Sid Sackson, 1964).** Each turn you place a tile and may buy a limited number of shares. Tiles found, grow and merge hotel chains. When a chain is absorbed, "the two largest shareholders in the acquired chain receive cash bonuses", and holders can sell, trade into the acquirer, or keep their shares. A chain becomes "safe" from acquisition at a set size. Players can agree before the game to keep holdings secret, which "can greatly extend the game since players will be less certain of their status, and therefore less willing to end the game" ([Wikipedia, Acquire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquire)). Lesson: majority and minority bonuses make the second-largest stake worth fighting for, and a size threshold gives a visible "safe from takeover" goal.
- **1830 and the 18xx family.** The largest holder of a company is its president and makes all its decisions; play alternates stock rounds (buy and sell) with operating rounds (build track, run trains, then pay dividends or withhold). The president picks the par value when buying the 20% president's certificate. Selling shares drops the price; if your sales let another player's holding exceed yours, they take the president's certificate ([1830 rules](https://nastol.io/storage/games/files/1830_railways_robber_barons/699096/en_1830_Rules_1830-RE_EN.pdf), ⚠️ read the introduction, stock market (4.0–4.3), stock round (5.1–5.2), dividends, forced train purchase, bankruptcy and game-end rules, not all 28 pages; section 4.5 on exact price moves was not read). A company's share value rises when it pays dividends and falls when it withholds. If a company must buy a train and cannot afford one, its president must pay from personal cash and sell personal shares, and a president who still cannot pay goes bankrupt and ends the game (same rules). The rulebook mentions "a financial swindle that is too devastating even for 1830", disarmed by requiring a full sequence of operations after each chance to trade. A game takes "3 to 5 hours" (same). The general 18xx pattern includes a certificate limit "to keep the game competitive by preventing snowball effects resulting from early leads" and, in some titles, letting a player put a dying company into "receivership" and "walk away from debts" ([Wikipedia, 18XX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX), ⚠️ skipped the long list of titles). The Wikipedia page on 1830 says players can win by "nurturing a railroad company to increase its stock value, gutting it and running with the money, successful stock trading or arranging for another player to go bankrupt" ([Wikipedia, 1830](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1830_(board_game))). Lesson: dividends versus reinvestment is a visible, recurring choice that moves the price; control can change hands mid-game; a company's failure can land on whoever holds the presidency.
- **Chicago Express (Harry Wu; originally Wabash Cannonball).** The winning bid for a share is paid into the company's treasury, and companies can only expand with money raised by auctioning shares; each company has a fixed number of shares, so "the possibilities to gain additional money are rather limited" ([Kulkmann's review](https://boardgame.de/reviews/chicexp.htm)). Dividends split income by shares held, and the winner is simply whoever has most cash at the end (same). Board Game Arena's tips say games usually end in rounds 3 to 5, often "because three companies run out of shares"; a minority holder in a 2-to-1 split should stop the company reaching Chicago because it "would benefit the majority shareholder more"; and a "null auction" stretches the game ([BGA tips](https://en.doc.boardgamearena.com/Tips_chicagoexpress)). Lesson: when raising capital and selling control are the same act, every funding decision becomes a political decision, and shareholders can sabotage the company they own part of.

### Narrative pressure bars

- **Reigns (Nerial, 2016).** Card choices push four meters (church, people, army, wealth); "if any of the four meters becomes completely filled or empty, the player's reign ends" ([Wikipedia, Reigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reigns_(video_game))). Lesson for a public-market bar: danger at both ends (too low means a revolt; too high means a different failure) keeps a single number from being a "maximise it" target. No game with an earnings call or shareholder meeting as a staged set piece was found in the sources read; Railroad Tycoon II's merger vote and Capitalism Lab's chairman warning are the closest.

Production Line, Prosperous Universe, X4, EVE Online corporations, Suzerain, Frostpunk, This Is the Police, and "Shares" (or a similar stock board game) were not researched; a quick search of the EVE University wiki returned nothing usable. The browser game also named "AI Lab Tycoon", mentioned on the Misaligned site's FAQ, was not found.

## Synthesis table

"Fit" is my judgement for an AI-lab game whose core tension is safety versus speed. It is inference, not a sourced claim.

| Mechanic | Games that use it | Why it works or fails | Fit for an AI-lab game |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your own shares as visible hit points; rivals buy them to eliminate you | Offworld Trading Company | Works because the threat is readable share by share; failed in early access when buyouts arrived in seconds with no warning ([DN17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/271240/discussions/0/618453594754245349/)) | Strong. A rival lab or big-tech suitor buying in is on-theme; must be visible and slow enough to answer |
| Buying back your own shares to defend control | Offworld, Capitalism II, RT2, Startup Company, Railroads! | Real choice when cash spent on defence is cash not spent on growth (Offworld's "turtle"); fails when buybacks also pump the price for profit (RT2 manual, RT2 player reports) | Good if buybacks compete with compute and safety spending; make sure they cannot be looped for profit |
| Accountability metric that can get the CEO fired | RT2 (five-year weighted return), Capitalism Lab (chairman review with a warning), Software Inc. (CEO voted out), Rise of Industry 2 (board) | Works when the clock is long and the warning is explicit (Capitalism Lab guarantees one review cycle); players dislike boards that block obvious owner choices (RT2 dividend cap reports) | Very strong. You already have a seven-member board that can remove the player; an IPO can change who sits on it and what they measure |
| Dilution: each raise costs permanent ownership | Capitalism II, Startup Company, Scaling Laws (cap table, down-round penalty), Chicago Express, Railroads! | Works when ownership has later uses (control, payouts, takeover defence); fails when ownership never matters again | Strong if ownership ties to control over safety decisions (for example, who can veto a slow, careful release) |
| Valuation driven by sentiment and timing | Scaling Laws, Offworld (founding timing changes starting debt and price) | Turns "when to raise" into a decision ([Scaling Laws notes](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/development)) | Strong. Hype cycles and capability releases are natural sentiment drivers; pushes toward speed, which is the right tension |
| Staged funding tied to milestones | Startup Company (25 milestones), Rise of Industry 2 ("goals to ensure further investment") | Gives preparation real tasks and costs | Good for a pre-IPO "readiness" stretch (audits, revenue targets, governance) |
| Price fed by debt with a multiplier | Offworld (debt counts five times) | Makes leverage visibly costly; also required a community-written guide to compute ([OTC wiki](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock)) | Useful idea: a visible liability can weigh on price. Hidden alignment debt should probably not feed the price directly, because markets price what they can see |
| Dividends versus reinvestment moves the price | 1830 and 18xx, RT2, Capitalism II | A recurring, visible choice with a clear price effect ([1830 rules](https://nastol.io/storage/games/files/1830_railways_robber_barons/699096/en_1830_Rules_1830-RE_EN.pdf)) | Moderate. Could map to "return cash or spend on safety and compute" |
| Selling shares is the only way to raise capital, and it sells control | Chicago Express, Railroads!, 18xx | Makes every raise a political choice ([Kulkmann](https://boardgame.de/reviews/chicexp.htm); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/strategy/20-years-later-the-world-deserves-another-sid-meiers-railroads/)) | Strong. The IPO buys compute and costs the founder's ability to choose slow and safe |
| Majority (50%) and supermajority (75%) thresholds | Capitalism II (50% control, 75% merge), Airline Tycoon 2 (75% dissolve), Offworld (6 of 10), Software Inc. (above 50%) | Clear, legible thresholds players can plan around | Good; gives the takeover threat readable stages |
| Forced buyout at a premium even when you own everything | Railroads!, Offworld (last five shares as a block) | Prevents a defensive stalemate but feels unfair when cash-hoarders win ([Railroads! thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/7600/discussions/0/1637542668223689238/)) | Risky. If used, telegraph it and price it so a growing lab is hard to take |

Thematic options exist (government contract, nonprofit control, dual-class shares), each with a price |
| Shareholder or merger vote | RT2 (merger vote once a year), Software Inc. (CEO voted by shareholders) | A staged moment with an uncertain outcome | Good set-piece candidate (annual meeting, activist vote) |

## What turns an IPO into an arc, and what makes it a cash button

**Arc patterns found in the sources.**

1. **Preparation with real costs and gates.** Capitalism II caps how many shares you can issue by profitability and forbids issuing too often ([manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)); RT2 requires a B credit rating for bonds ([manual](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/http://vztimg.exent.com/Prem/products/172350/manual.pdf)); Startup Company ties funding to a 25-step milestone ladder ([Milestones](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Milestones)); Scaling Laws makes valuation depend on frontier position, revenue and the market mood ([notes](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/development)). Each makes "are we ready to raise?" a question with a wrong answer.
2. **A pricing moment with an uncertain outcome.** The 1830 founder picks a par value that other players can punish by dumping shares (the rulebook's example has a player sell to drop a new company from $82 to $71) ([1830 rules](https://nastol.io/storage/games/files/1830_railways_robber_barons/699096/en_1830_Rules_1830-RE_EN.pdf)); Scaling Laws' sentiment cycle means the same company raises on very different terms depending on timing. No video game in this survey was found to stage the listing day itself as an event with an uncertain "pop" or flop; that gap is open.
3. **A long tail of obligations.** RT2 investors judge a five-year weighted return and can fire the chairman; the board limits dividends ([manual](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/http://vztimg.exent.com/Prem/products/172350/manual.pdf)). Capitalism Lab's chairman reviews results, warns once, then fires ([Playing Without a Company](https://www.capitalismlab.com/playing-without-company/)). Startup Company investors take an equal cut whenever you pay yourself ([Devlog #53](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/2413289226965456109)). Scaling Laws lets missed debt payments build toward a public default ([roadmap](https://pcworkman.dev/hck-labs/scaling-laws/roadmap)).
4. **An opponent who can act on your stock.** Offworld rivals buy you out share by share; Railroads! and Capitalism AIs take over undefended companies; AI CEOs in Capitalism II have a takeover-appetite rating ([manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)); Railroads! AIs trigger auctions to force you to sell stock ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/strategy/20-years-later-the-world-deserves-another-sid-meiers-railroads/)).
5. **Control as a currency.** In Chicago Express, 18xx and Startup Company, money and control are traded against each other in every raise; dropping below 51% has consequences ([wiki, Investors](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Investors)).
6. **A way back.** Capitalism Lab lets an ousted founder regain control if the acquirer sells below 50%, or resign, keep their shares and start over ([Playing Without a Company](https://www.capitalismlab.com/playing-without-company/)); Offworld turns eliminated players into subsidiaries that keep paying their owners rather than leaving them to sabotage ([DN17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)). Losing control becomes a chapter, not only a game over.

**Cash-button patterns.**

1. The IPO is one click that adds money and changes nothing else (Capitalism Lab's IPO page; Universal Paperclips; the current Game Night card).
2. The share price never feeds back into anything the player cares about. The Software Inc. developer added CEO controversy in 2026 "to add some interest in the stock system" ([Final Overhaul](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/1845383656391730)).
3. Nobody can act on your stock, so dilution has no cost (Paperclips has no market acting back at all).
4. The player's own trades or cash move the price enough to profit (Railroads!, RT2 buyback pumping, OpenTTD).

## Pitfalls

- **Share-price formulas players can game.** Counting cash in company value enabled OpenTTD's infinite-money loop; maintainers proposed valuing on fixed assets or income, or rate-limiting valuation changes, and the project later replaced share trading with AI-only takeovers ([issue #7691](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/issues/7691); [changelog](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/blob/master/changelog.md)). In Railroads!, buying raised the price enough to sell at a profit ([Gamewatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/sid-meiers-railroads/review)). In Offworld, the community found that buying a rival's unowned stock raises your own price "10x more" than holding the same cash ([OTC wiki](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock)). Software Inc. had to ban cross-holding ([notes](https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/3893864668621694055)); Capitalism II wrote trading regulations "to prevent you from manipulating the stock market" and Capitalism Lab added an option to make speculation harder ([manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf); [Stock Market Enhancement](https://www.capitalismlab.com/new-content/stock-market-enhancement/)). 1830's designers closed a "financial swindle" by forcing a full operating sequence between trading windows ([1830 rules](https://nastol.io/storage/games/files/1830_railways_robber_barons/699096/en_1830_Rules_1830-RE_EN.pdf)).
- **Pause as an exploit.** RT2 players do their buybacks and AI re-rigging while paused, so the market never reacts ([Committee of shareholders?](https://forum.dune2k.com/topic/22599-committee-of-shareholders/), player reports). A real-time-with-pause game should check whether any market action can be chained in a single paused instant.
- **Too much spreadsheet.** Offworld's price formula is understood mainly through a community reverse-engineering post that still calls part of it "unclear" ([OTC wiki](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Stock)); the Capitalism II stock chapter introduces P/E, equity per share, EPS, yield and total return before you can trade ([manual](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/steam/apps/638200/manuals/Cap2_manual.pdf)). In a 30-minute game, players need one or two numbers they can read, plus clear thresholds.
- **A stock price that never matters.** If no board, rival or obligation reads the price, it is decoration (the Software Inc. "add some interest" note; Paperclips' "just a visual" stock table).
- **A takeover that feels random.** Offworld early access ("whammo you lose in 15 seconds"), Railroads! ("i owned all my own stock … where and how did they find stocks to buy?") ([OTC thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/271240/discussions/0/618453594754245349/); [Railroads! thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/7600/discussions/0/1637542668223689238/)), and OpenTTD ("no real way to predict it") ([discussion #12751](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/discussions/12751)). Offworld's fixes were transparency (hit-point shares), a slower AI purchase rate, and a final block that must be bought at once ([DN17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/)).
- **Snowball or standoff.** Offworld's original buyout let the winner snowball and pushed others to hoard for the last buyout; 18xx uses certificate limits to stop early leads from compounding ([DN17](https://www.designer-notes.com/otc-designer-notes-17-stock-market/); [Wikipedia, 18XX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX)).
- **A board that blocks obvious owner choices.** RT2 players who own 100% resent the board's dividend cap ([forum](https://forum.dune2k.com/topic/22599-committee-of-shareholders/), player report). If the IPO changes the board, make the reason it can say no visible (outside holders exist).
