Going public, as a long road instead of one card. Pick a direction.

Today the IPO is one choice on one event card that adds $50M. The research says a real IPO takes months, is mostly hidden until the end, prices in one tense night, and then puts the lab on trial every quarter. Below are four ways to put that in the game. Each picture is the running game with the new screen drawn over it.

Design only: no game code changed. Numbers in the pictures are examples, not tuned. Click any picture to see it full size.

My recommendation

  1. A, the road to the bell: every real stage becomes a beat, from hiring banks in era 2 to quarterly calls in era 5. It reuses screens players already know.
  2. Add C's listing window as a layer inside A, so rival labs race you for the same investors.
  3. Going public stays optional: a run can stay private, and the advisors argue about it.
  4. The market's worst move uses existing endings: an activist wins a board vote, or a buyer takes an unlocked lab (the acquihire ending). No new endings here.

The real road it copies

Draft Jun 1 Jul 13 Aug 10 Sep 4 Oct 1, 9 Public S-1 Oct 26

Anthropic, PBC on SEC EDGAR, 2026: a confidential draft, five amended drafts, then the public filing. The roadshow can start 15 days later. No game surveyed stages this part; it is open ground.

How it plays

  1. Era 2, lateGet ready (a CFO move). Pick banks: two big banks price higher but want a clean story; a cheaper bank also lends to Lodestar. Pick the lock now or never: none, a founders' share, a benefit trust, or a nonprofit parent.
  2. Era 3File in confidence. SEC letters arrive about once a month as event cards, drawn from what the lab did: its biggest customer, a hype claim the press repeated, its compute commitments, an incident it buried, a sovereign investor (which can stall review for months). Each is a candor choice.
  3. Era 3 to 4Go public and go quiet. The filing turns public; the long jump between eras stands in for the last months of review. For about three weeks Comms cannot hype, and a launch risks a "gun-jumping" card.
  4. Era 4, startRoadshow and pricing night. Three investor meetings, answered with the President meeting's answer styles, build the order book. Pick a price sheet. Day one plays like a model reveal: the first trade prints around noon, then the close.
  5. Eras 4 and 5Public life. A ticker under the HUD. An earnings call each quarter of story time; guidance works like a board deal. The lockup ends after the second call, staff can sell, rivals poach. An incident starts a four-day disclosure clock.

Ties into

Board
At listing the growth investor's seat becomes "public shareholders", judged on the share price. An activist can take a money seat. The trust lock shields the four oversight seats.
Cash
The biggest raise of the run, replacing that era's funding round. The bank fee shrinks as the deal grows.
Compute
Proceeds pay for multi-year deals. The SEC asks about commitments; a big new contract in the quiet weeks forces an amended filing.
Safety
What you disclose about hidden debt becomes a risk factor. A buried problem that surfaces later becomes a shareholder lawsuit. Guidance makes a higher safety share cost stock.
Rivals
Rival labs file and list on their own schedules; a rival's flop or incident drags every AI stock; rivals poach your staff when the lockup ends.

What can go wrong

  • Review drags: sovereign money or one dominant customer stalls it for months.
  • A leak or a hype post in the quiet weeks.
  • A weak order book forces a lower price; day one breaks below the offer.
  • The second earnings call misses just as the lockup ends.
  • A short seller's report exposes hidden alignment debt; a lawsuit follows the drop.

Feeds the endings

No new endings. Activist seats can win a removal vote (the existing board ending). A lab listed without a lock can be bought (routes to acquihire, per question 2). A finale tag such as "listed, still independent". The end-of-run reveal gains one row: what you told the market next to what was true.

Sub-choices for A

How it plays

  1. Era 2, endThe listing set piece, offered beside the funding round: pick the lock, pick a price sheet, watch day one. About a minute. No SEC review or roadshow on screen.
  2. Eras 3 to 5An earnings call every quarter of story time: about once per era in eras 3 and 4, once more in era 5. Set guidance, results land, analysts ask about safety spend and rivals, the stock moves after hours.
  3. Between callsThe market acts. Activists, short sellers, takeover bids, the lockup ending and the four-day disclosure clock, the same events as A's public life.

Ties into

Board
The public-shareholders seat reads every call. Missed guidance counts like a broken board deal.
Cash
One big raise early, then follow-on offerings that dilute and drop the price.
Compute
Analysts punish a bigger compute plan the same day, as markets did to Meta and Microsoft.
Safety
The core lesson: every call asks you to defend the safety share. A promise to cut it comes due at the next call.
Rivals
Rival labs' results move your stock. A rival's incident sinks the whole sector for a quarter.

What can go wrong

  • Guide too high and miss; guide too low and the board calls you timid.
  • A promise to "return safety compute to growth" comes due.
  • Activists, short reports and bids, as in A.

Feeds the endings

Same as A: board removal through activist seats, acquihire through a bid on an unlocked lab, a finale tag, a reveal row. The trade-off: the stretch before listing, where most of the realism lives, stays off screen.

How it plays

  1. Era 3The window opens. A screen shows each rival lab's IPO track and an investor-mood read from Comms and the feed. Mood rises after a rival's big first day and shuts after a flop or an industry incident.
  2. Eras 3 and 4File, wait, withdraw and refile, or stay private with a sovereign or cloud mega-round and its strings. You can also delay in public for safety: public trust and the safety chair up, the financier down.
  3. ListingFirst out gets the index funds. A later lab splits the room and gets a smaller book.
  4. AfterLight public life: a ticker and one results beat per era in the feed and the board, no call set piece.

Ties into

Rivals
The core of it: four rival IPO tracks, each moving the mood for all. Your own incident can shut the window on every lab.
Board
The financier pushes to go now; the safety chair backs a delay; the sovereign fund wants to stay private.
Cash
Timing changes the raise a lot: the same lab raises far more in a hot market.
Safety
Delaying for safety is a real, public choice with a price, as it was for OpenAI in 2026.
Compute
Staying private means equity-for-compute deals and their strings (already in the game).

What can go wrong

  • A rival lists first and takes the index funds.
  • You wait, and a rival's flop shuts the window for months.
  • Your own incident shuts it for everyone, and the feed blames you.

Feeds the endings

Mostly through the finale tag ("first lab to list", "never went public"). Thinner after listing than A or B, by design.

How it plays

  1. Eras 1 and 2Every raise hands out a block: funding rounds and equity-for-compute deals each cost one. The holders are the board's money seats.
  2. Era 3The IPO floats three or four blocks. Locks: a founders' share (two blocks nobody can buy) or the trust (oversight seats nobody can replace).
  3. Eras 4 and 5Buyers collect blocks, one at a time and in plain view: a rival lab, a tech giant, an activist. Defend with buybacks, a friendly buyer, or a high share price.

Ties into

Board
The board becomes the register: holders are seats. A deep rework of today's seven directors.
Cash
Buybacks spend money that compute and safety need.
Rivals
Rivals become buyers. A cloud partner sells its block to the highest bidder.
Safety
A poor safety record lowers the price, which makes the lab cheaper to buy.

What can go wrong

  • Raise too often early and own too little to defend.
  • Buybacks starve the safety team.
  • Share-price exploits: Railroads!, OpenTTD and Software Inc. all had to patch money loops.

Feeds the endings

A takeover routes to the acquihire ending, or to a new ending that the endings lane would have to design.
