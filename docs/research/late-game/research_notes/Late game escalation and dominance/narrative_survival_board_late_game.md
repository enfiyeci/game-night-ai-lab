## 1. Plague Inc.: the world's late response, and how the end race becomes visible

### Takeaway
Plague Inc. builds its climax from a hidden world-side clock (cure research) that is switched on by the player's own success (a pathogen that is noticed). The clock is made visible through a progress bar with pop-up warnings at 25, 50, 75, 95 and 100 percent, and the world's other defences (closing airports, ports and borders) fire off numeric "priority" thresholds that the player can read about and plan around. The player's counter-tools are timed to those same warnings ("use Drug Resistance at 75%, take extra actions at 95%").

### Cited Findings

**The cure clock: how it starts, how fast it runs, what slows it**
- Cure development starts "shortly after" the plague is spotted. Plagues are more likely to be spotted when severity rises through symptoms. On Mega Brutal, discovery can also happen at random later in the game, "forcing the player on an aggressive approach". — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure) (page source, read in full)
- Money for the cure comes from research facilities: each invests $1,146.56 per day; the normal world has about 340 (the page calls the table values estimates); each country or country group has 2 to 10. China, USA, Japan, Germany, France, UK and others have 10 (about $11.4 thousand per day maximum); Bolivia has 2. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.wiki.gg/wiki/Cure) ⚠️ WebFetch digest of the wiki.gg copy; the fandom copy of the Cure page, read in full, only embeds this table from another page (Research Facilities), so the numbers are not independently confirmed; the next bullet is confirmed on the fandom copy
- How much a country spends depends on plague severity, lethality and how important the infected country is to the rest of the world. More deaths means less money governments can spend. Countries whose government is destroyed stop contributing. Lethality can destroy facilities and so slow research. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Counters the player can buy: Genetic Hardening 1 and 2 raise the money needed; Genetic ReShuffle 1 to 3 lowers the money already spent; stifling symptoms (Coma, Seizures, Paralysis, Insanity) also slow research. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Progress can go backwards, even after 100%: "doing anything that sets back progress (such as evolving Genetic Reshuffle/Coma or destroying Gen-Sys / WHO labs) will still bring down the cure progress by adding requirements, preventing anymore people from being cured and allowing them to be reinfected." — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Speed-ups and shocks: Patient Zero identified in the origin country gives a 10% boost to cure progress; an adverse reaction in the experimental cure sets it back 10%. In the Science Denial scenario, the first time progress reaches 100% it resets to zero (protesters destroy the main facility), so the cure effectively needs twice the research. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Win/lose end: once the cure is done, teams "rush all over the world" and the game ends when all infected are cured (defeat by cure in the main game). A plague can also die out by itself (extinction), which is common with Bio-Weapon. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)

**How the end race is made visible**
- "Warnings will be issued in popups when cure development has reached 25%, 50%, 75%, 95%, and lastly, 100%." The game also gives a tip to use Drug Resistance abilities when the cure reaches 75% and "to take some extra actions if it reaches 95%". Drug Resistance does not touch the cure directly; it unlocks abilities that slow it. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- The UI has a "Cure Counter" (Plague Inc: Evolved), flask icons for empty, full and broken flasks, and "cure bonus bubbles" that appear on the map (gallery captions on the page). — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- The in-game manual text for the three core stats tells the player what the world will notice: severity "will slow down cure research ... People will notice and care about a severe disease"; lethality "can slow/stop cure research ... People will really notice and care about a disease that kills." — [Plague Inc. wiki, Severity page (quotes the manual)](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Severity) (read to within about 300 characters of the end)

**The world closes its doors: government actions with numeric thresholds**
- Government actions can only begin once the disease has been spotted, has enough attention, and the country's government has not fallen. The wiki table was built by extracting the game files (version 1.18.0, Android), and its author notes some variables were converted to percentages. — [Plague Inc. wiki, List of government actions](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_government_actions) (read in full)
- Transport actions (normal standard game). Each lowers local infectivity by 3. Priorities are listed as local and global: Airports Closed local 13 / global 20; Land Borders Closed 23 / 30; Ports Closed 28 / 35. The page notes that scenarios such as Who Cares and Created Equal change these priorities to make closures earlier or later. — [Plague Inc. wiki, List of government actions](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_government_actions)
- Cure-related actions: "Infectious Disease Teams mobilised" (local 30 / global 30) gives +0.5 cure allocation and -5 local infectivity. "Pandemic Alert issued" (local 40 / global 15) gives -5 local infectivity. "Drug Research safeguards removed" (local 75, needs at least 10% of the country's population dead) gives +0.1 cure allocation. "Human Experimentation approved" (local 95, needs half the population dead) gives +0.5 cure allocation. — [Plague Inc. wiki, List of government actions](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_government_actions)
- Society actions escalate in a chain: Curfews (local 60), then National Emergency (55) or Martial Law (85, needs Curfews), then Internet shutdown and Mobile networks shutdown (80 / 80, need 1% dead and National Emergency). Corpse handling chains from Central Corpse Disposal (at least 0.1% dead) to Mass Graves (0.5%) to Burning Corpses (10%). — [Plague Inc. wiki, List of government actions](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_government_actions)
- When a government collapses, public order drops, "all progress for the cure" in that territory halts, infectivity, severity and lethality increase there, and land borders reopen. — [Plague Inc. wiki, List of government actions](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_government_actions)
- Lethality is the stat that makes countries close airports, seaports and land borders: "Once lethality starts to take effect in gameplay, countries seem more likely to close their airports, seaports and land borders. Overall, lethality is best to be prioritized last in gameplay once all countries are infected." (The wording "seem more likely" is the wiki editors' observation, not a developer statement.) — [Plague Inc. wiki, Severity](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Severity)
- The page does not define "local priority" and "global priority". ⚠️ The only separate hint is a Cure Mode random event whose condition is "global priority is greater than or equal to 15 and less than 50, and ... at least 8 countries in lockdown". — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure Mode](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure_Mode) ⚠️ only text matching "priority", "win" and "Authority" was inspected on this 42,939-character page, not the whole page

**Difficulty is mostly a dial on the cure clock**
- Casual: "Research doctors don't work" (reduced cure speed). Normal: "Doctors work 3 days a week". Brutal: "Doctors never go home" (increased speed). Mega Brutal: "Doctors invest in research" (greatly increased speed) and random "Anomalous blood test" detection, plus automatic detection once any country is fully infected. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Difficulty](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Difficulty) (read to within about 800 characters of the end)

**Special plagues re-skin the cure race instead of removing it**
- Nano-Virus: the cure broadcast starts at the beginning of the game, advances "as fast as a regular cure does at maximum speed", has a much larger requirement than any other plague, and the plague has more counter-options than any other. Necroa Virus: the cure is "of no relevance" in a carefully planned game. Simian Flu: the cure is developed by the company Gen-Sys, and raiding its labs sets progress back. Shadow Plague: WHO labs "speed up research dramatically", Templar bases hold flasks, and at 100% each vampire feeding poisons the vampire. Bio-Weapon: "skyrocketing infectivity/lethality" make it "basically unstoppable", except by fully quarantining the home continent. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)

**The Cure mode: the same race from the other side**
- Announced 24 March 2020, mobile release 11 November 2020, PC DLC 28 January 2021, free while COVID-19 was under control; later $4.99. — [Wikipedia, Plague Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_Inc.) ⚠️ WebFetch digest of the Wikipedia page (this is not the raw page text)
- Cure Mode gives three separate quarantine switches (close air access, sea access, land borders), a lockdown, Non-Compliance (rises when people think a measure is unnecessary or lack economic support), and an Authority meter. "The cure must be properly developed before Authority is drained." Difficulty changes starting Authority (Casual 110, Normal 100, Brutal 98). — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure); [Plague Inc. wiki, Difficulty](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Difficulty); ⚠️ the quarantine and Non-Compliance details come from a [WebFetch digest of the wiki.gg Curemode page](https://plagueinc.wiki.gg/wiki/Curemode)
- Cure Mode's cure has two phases, Research then Manufacturing; the wiki lists initiatives that speed each. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Wikipedia: Outbreak Mode (daily challenges with a pre-selected disease type and modifiers) was released on mobile 24 November 2025 and on PC 15 December 2025. — [Wikipedia, Plague Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_Inc.) ⚠️ WebFetch digest

**What James Vaughan (Ndemic Creations) said**
- On Cure mode's late-game trap: "Players are typically caught out by how long it takes to make a vaccine," and players learn they must prepare manufacturing facilities in advance. A frequent beta-tester complaint was: "Disease ran out of control because I didn't start making the vaccine until after I've researched it." — [Kotaku, Plague Inc. now has a new mode](https://kotaku.com/plague-inc-now-has-a-new-mode-in-which-you-try-to-stop-1845647409) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- On why the victory condition is manufacturing: "We need to draw it to a conclusion," and it was "one of the areas where we had to gamify it." — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/plague-inc-now-has-a-new-mode-in-which-you-try-to-stop-1845647409) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- On public trust: the reporter paraphrases Vaughan as saying that when public trust in officials drops, people refuse measures like masks and the disease spreads faster. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/plague-inc-now-has-a-new-mode-in-which-you-try-to-stop-1845647409) ⚠️ WebFetch digest (paraphrase by the reporter, not a quote)
- On the original design: the game grew from a five-year-old web game, Pandemic 2, and he wanted "a deeper, more strategic game with a strong narrative and lots more features." — [Dread Central interview](https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/36863/exclusive-interview-ndemic-creations-ceo-james-vaughan-talks-plague-inc/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- On what players get from it (2020): "an element of control during a very scary and worrying time", and it teaches "the terrifying power of exponential growth" and "the mechanisms that global governments have to stop a pandemic." — [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/plague-inc-developer-never-expected-reality-to-look-like-its-video-game/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- On unlock-gated progression: a new plague type unlocks when the player beats the previous one on Normal; cheats unlock after beating all types on Brutal. — [Dread Central interview](https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/36863/exclusive-interview-ndemic-creations-ceo-james-vaughan-talks-plague-inc/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest; the wiki Difficulty page confirms the same unlock rule for standard and special plagues

### Inferences
- The "priority" columns are thresholds: a closure action appears to fire when a country's own priority (local) or the world's overall alarm level (global) crosses the listed number, with lower numbers firing earlier. Airports (13 / 20) close before land borders (23 / 30) before ports (28 / 35). That ordering is the same escalation ladder a player can learn and plan around. This is an inference; the source does not define the columns.
- The pattern worth borrowing is a two-sided loop: the player's own growth (severity, lethality) feeds the opponent's response (money, closures), and the response is shown in coarse, loud milestones (25, 50, 75, 95, 100) with a matching toolkit, rather than a hidden number.
- The cure bar can move backwards, so the finale is not a plain countdown. Players keep having something to do at 95%, and a "completed" cure can still be undone. That keeps the last stretch active.
- Vaughan's own comment that the Cure mode "had to" be gamified into a manufacturing victory condition shows that the designer chose a clear end event ("draw it to a conclusion") over realism.

### Gaps
- No Vaughan statement found about the design of the main game's cure and late game (the Pocket Gamer.biz "Live and Kicking" interview, opened as a digest, says nothing on the cure; the Dread Central interview likewise has none; the Kotaku podcast page was not opened). No GDC talk or postmortem by Ndemic found.
- The in-game meaning of "local priority" and "global priority" is not documented on any page opened. The numbers come from fans extracting game files (version 1.18.0, Android), not from the developer.
- Plague Inc: Evolved scenario details (Cure Counter behavior, multiplayer) were not researched beyond the Wikipedia digest.

## 2. Frostpunk (A New Home) and Frostpunk 2: scheduled storm, Hope/Discontent, laws, factions

### Takeaway
Frostpunk 1 ends its campaign on one scheduled, telegraphed, week-long storm. The developers killed the "keep building after the storm" version because it felt anticlimactic and chose "the way a novel ends". The storm is preceded by a checklist-style quest chain, a visible grey fog approaching on the map, three waves of refugees, and a panic event arc. Meanwhile, the Book of Laws is an ordered ladder that escalates cruelty slowly ("creeping normality"), and its final rung replaces the Hope meter with a fixed 100% so the player can skip the pressure by crossing a moral line. Frostpunk 2 swaps the weather climax for a faction civil war that critics found chaotic and its ending rushed.

### Cited Findings

**The scheduled climax in A New Home**
- The scenario lasts 48 days. It has three acts: Act I (arrival to finding Winterhome), Act II (the Londoners faction), Act III (the Storm). — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home) (page source, read in full)
- The storm timer starts when the player learns of Winterhome (a dying man arrives by day 15, or scouts find it earlier). "The latest the Storm can hit without exploits is day forty-three." Rushing Winterhome or clearing the Londoners early makes the storm come earlier. — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home)
- Act II is triggered when scouts find Winterhome: Hope "drops sharply", some of the population join the Londoners, and Purpose laws (Order or Faith) are unlocked at that point to manage Hope and Discontent. — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home)
- Act III is announced by refugees. After the Londoners are resolved, three waves of refugees arrive, all carrying the same message ("doom is coming", a huge storm from the south). Each wave forces a choice between "Send them on their way" (Hope falls), "Only the healthy ones" (40 healthy people enter in wave one; Discontent rises slightly, Hope falls slightly), and "Everyone's welcome" (70 people, 30 ill; Discontent rises, Hope rises in wave one). — [Frostpunk wiki, Refugees from Frostland](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Refugees_from_Frostland_(Arc))
- The endgame is a visible checklist. After the refugees, the quest chain is: "What looms ahead" (research Beacon: Stereoscopic Lenses), then four parallel quests (Food: a week's worth of food for every citizen; Survivors: rescue three named locations; Outposts: recall all scouts and outpost teams; Cold: research Overdrive Couplings and Generator Power Upgrade III), then "Final days: Wait for the storm to hit", then "Survive". — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home)
- The storm is shown on the world map as a thick grey fog slowly approaching ("travel phase"). It disables scouting locations it touches (they show a snowflake), and scouts and outposts it hits "die instantly". — [Frostpunk wiki, The Great Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Great_Storm) (read in full)
- When it arrives (A New Home): temperatures hit an all-time low (typically a drop of 2 to 3 heat levels), hothouses and hunters' huts stop working (no raw food), scouting and the beacon are disabled, panic reduces Hope, the sky darkens, and the music changes to "The City Must Survive". It "lasts a week (seven days)" and is stronger and longer than other storms. — [Frostpunk wiki, The Great Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Great_Storm)
- It also adds a story arc: the Coal Mine Crisis forces choices about sending volunteers (10 per mine, some die) or abandoning the lower levels (all coal mine output falls by 80%), followed by follow-up crises such as a second round of volunteers (5 per mine), coal theft, and a coal shortage panic. — [Frostpunk wiki, Coal Mine Crisis](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Coal_Mine_Crisis_(Arc))
- Panic before and during the storm is an event chain on a daily rhythm: Hungry mother, then Food theft causes riots a few hours later, then Doom-mongering drunks the next day, then Prayers at work a day later, then Fight over supplies, then A mob storms the stockpiles, then People refuse to work, Panic spreads, and Work ceases near the end. Many options require an active Guard Station or Faith Keepers, so the earlier law choices decide which options exist. — [Frostpunk wiki, Fear of the Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Fear_of_the_Storm_(Arc))
- A false relief is scripted: near the end, an engineer reports the storm will pass "in a couple of days. But ... the temperature just before the end will drop drastically!" Hope falls again. The scenario's final temperature is -150 degrees Celsius (the wiki trivia). — [Frostpunk wiki, The Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Storm_(Arc)); [A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home)
- Endless mode keeps storms but reduces their meaning: Endurance's first blizzard comes at 14 days and each following one comes 2 days sooner until the gap reaches 8 days (first storm 2 days of -70 degrees, second 2 days of -80). The Great Storm page says that in Endless "as the city's capability grows this starts as a potential end to your city and devolves into a minor annoyance." — [Frostpunk wiki, Endless mode](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Endless_mode) ⚠️ first 3,500 of 11,793 characters read; [Frostpunk wiki, The Great Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Great_Storm)

**What 11 bit studios said about the ending**
- Kuba Stokalski (lead designer): whether the game should end or be open-ended "wasn't really set in stone" for a long time and "came out really naturally from the format that we arrived at through iteration". The three episodes were basic survival, social fractures, then "the ultimate test of the storm". After the storm the build-on-in-peace version was "anti-climatic ... This kind of climax didn't work, so we knew we wanted to end this the way a novel ends." — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/) (HTML saved and the interview text extracted and read to its end)
- Wikipedia's account agrees: the three-act structure was survival basics, internal conflict in a divided city, then surviving the huge storm; the team "wanted the game to end after reaching a big climax, similar to a novel." — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk) (Development and Reception sections read; the Gameplay section was read only as a digest)

**Hope and Discontent**
- Hope is "the confidence people have in terms of their chances of survival"; the game is lost if it falls too low for too long. Discontent is the likeliness of people overthrowing the leader; if it rises too long, the leader is exiled or, in extreme cases, executed. — [Frostpunk wiki, Hope](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Hope); [Discontent](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Discontent)
- Hope falls from "Storm is coming", "People are frightened", "Fall of Winterhome", "Promise Broken", "Controversial law", and "Forceful Persuasion"; Discontent rises from cold, hunger, "Lack of shelter", "Overtime work", "Emergency Shift", and ignored requests. Most Discontent modifiers "have a chance to trigger an event if they remain that way for too long". — [Frostpunk wiki, Hope](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Hope); [Discontent](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Discontent)
- Origin of Hope: Marta Fijak and the team say the hope meter came from research on survival: "the most important thing was hope", citing stories of mountaineers crawling down from high altitudes. Discontent "came quite naturally" from citizens' needs. — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/)

**Laws: the escalating authoritarian ladder**
- The Book of Laws has two trees. Adaptation laws are always available. Purpose laws arrive later and are Faith or Order, mutually exclusive. Each law shifts Hope or Discontent, often both; new-building laws act as a promise to build, and the population is displeased if it is not built soon. — [Frostpunk wiki, Laws](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Laws) ⚠️ first 9,000 of 17,570 characters read (the law trees and the cooldown table were in the read part; the rest was not)
- Laws have cooldowns between signings (examples: 18 hours for Extended Shift, Emergency Shift, Cemetery; 36 hours for Child Labour - All Jobs and House of Pleasure; 45 hours for Alternative Food Source, Duelling Law, Triage). — [Frostpunk wiki, Laws](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Laws)
- The final Purpose law ("New Order" or "New Faith") removes Hope as a going concern: "Roughly 10-40 people will die when you sign it, and Hope will be replaced by Obedience [or Devotion]," which "remains at a 100 % at all times", the only way to be overthrown is to raise Discontent too much, and an Execution Platform is built automatically to cut Discontent by executing a "Public Enemy". — [Frostpunk wiki, Order](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Order); [Faith](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Faith)
- The scenario tracks "the line". It is crossed by signing Pledge of Loyalty or Forceful Persuasion (Order), Protector of the Truth or Righteous Denunciation (Faith), or Alternative Food Source (an Adaptation law). If you sign any of the last three laws of either path, the ending says "We have crossed the line" and "But was it worth it?" Crossing before the storm appears changes the background music. — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home); [Laws](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Laws)
- Order and Faith are built with different cost curves: on Order, the Foreman ability needs 3 laws and Agitator 3 more; the wiki says all essential laws take 6 on Faith and 8 on Order. Faith has the Sermon (free), Evening Prayers (20 rations) and Ceremonial Service to restore Hope, which "provide incentives to not cross the line". — [Frostpunk wiki, Order](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Order); [Faith](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Faith)
- Design intent: Marta Fijak said events driven by citizens had no player agency, so laws were put "in a sequence" to show "creeping normality or the boiling frog syndrome": "enacting small laws that [don't] change much ... over a longer period of time, you see that the sum of those parts makes something bigger and something more terrifying." "Every law should be a little bit more than the previous one." Stokalski: "that's basically how Totalitarian states are born." — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/)
- Stokalski also states a design tenet: a choice is made interesting by "asking you whether you're willing to sacrifice some of your morals, your ideals, or making this one step [over] the line, in exchange for a better outcome". — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/)
- A third-party summary quotes Stokalski on the slow-decline concept: "enough small, tenuous steps take us, as a society, to a very bad place," and says the Book of Laws offers harsher options as conditions worsen. — [Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/editorials/fifth-dont-kill-on-moral-dilemmas-in-video-games/a-shard-of-ice-instead-of-a-heart/zf4b9) ⚠️ WebFetch digest

**Player and critic reception of the Frostpunk 1 finale**
- PCGamesN's Julian Benson found the game "unexpectedly easy as a management game", finishing the campaign in a single try, though balancing citizen demands and Hope/Discontent was the most challenging part. Several critics said replay value was limited since it is not a sandbox: with different choices, "the overall story beats in the main campaign were the same." Eurogamer's Robert Purchese was disappointed the game never judged the player's decisions, making some feel hollow. Rock Paper Shotgun's Xavier Nelson Jr. praised the juggling of priorities but lamented there is no way to avoid the gameplay-favoring choices simply by playing better. — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk) (Reception section read)
- Sales: over 5 million within six years. — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk)

**Frostpunk 2: faction council and a political climax instead of a storm**
- The Council Hall has 100 members, each representing a faction. Most laws need a simple 51-vote majority; anything that grants the Steward more power needs two-thirds. Negotiation requires promises about the future, and failing to keep them upsets factions. Timescale is weeks, not hours and days. Radicals can grow within each faction and the player must counter them "to avoid the city from descending into chaos." — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2) (read in full)
- The campaign climax: after the first Whiteout and the Winterhome choice (use Steam Cores and make Winterhome uninhabitable, or seal it with an avalanche), tensions rise "ultimately resulting in the murder of a council member and igniting a civil war between the two factions." The Steward chooses to negotiate a peace treaty, banish one faction, or gather enough support to become Captain and segregate the factions in camps. "The game ends once the player either resolves the conflict and stabilizes the city, or fails and the city collapses into anarchy." An epilogue follows a child born when the Generator was re-ignited. — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2)
- The sandbox mode (Utopia Builder) allows optional objectives such as "preparing for a massive storm" or a disease outbreak; failing those leads to "Game Over". — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2)
- Director Jakub Stokalski: "social survival is even more important" than the cold, and "the biggest enemy is always human nature". — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2)
- Breach of Trust DLC (1927): the final stage is a triggered disaster. Once the city has enough food, a dormant volcano erupts and the player must evacuate the whole city to the colony of Aurora; after a second eruption the city is destroyed. — [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2)
- Reception of the late game (reviewers): Shacknews (score 9) says the game "just goes from bad to worse, in the best way possible", and its climax was "begging the vote to go through" or ordering militia on unrest; its only con is "Bit of an information overload at times". — [Shacknews review](https://shacknews.com/article/141448/frostpunk-2-review-score) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- A 4/5 review criticised faction juggling ("pinball aimlessly bouncing around and reacting to the whims of a bunch of idiots"), the loss of intimacy ("it's another pop-up amongst many" past 50,000 population), the story "wraps up in an unsatisfying flurry", and "you won't know you've made bad choices sometimes until the resulting snowball of destruction." — [Wolf's Gaming Blog](https://wolfsgamingblog.com/2024/09/21/frostpunk-2-is-a-bold-sequel-that-doesnt-always-work-review/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- ⚠️ Search-result snippets only (pages not opened): one reviewer found factions "don't feel useful" and revolts "mostly just hurt productivity"; a Steam user argued civil war is "entirely scripted"; a GOG reviewer described slow framerate with many districts and a "very slow and very frustrating last 1-2 hours". These are anecdotal and should not be treated as established. — [Search result list](https://shacknews.com/article/141448/frostpunk-2-review-score) (via web search of Frostpunk 2 reviews; individual review pages not read)

### Inferences
- Frostpunk 1's climax works because three things line up: a fixed timer the player can see (fog on the map, a checklist of named preparations, three refugee waves as warning), the previous act having planted problems the storm then amplifies (Londoners, Hope, law-gated event options), and a hard stop after the climax. The developers' own rationale is that continued building afterward felt anticlimactic.
- The law ladder is the escalation engine of the middle game: each rung is a small, plausible step, rungs are ordered, and the final rung removes the pressure meter (Hope) at a moral cost. This lets the same game offer a gentle finish (pay the pressure) or a cruel finish (cancel the pressure), and the ending text judges the choice.
- The "unexpectedly easy" and "never judged" criticisms suggest a failure mode: if tools to cancel pressure are available, the finale can be passed without feeling threatened. The Endless mode comment ("devolves into a minor annoyance") shows the same failure when capacity grows faster than the threat.
- Frostpunk 2 shows the cost of replacing a single external clock with an open-ended political simulation: critics report information overload, intimacy loss at scale, and a rushed conclusion. This is the closest cautionary example to a 2027 "world dominance" finale built on many systems at once.

- No source found for how many days the Frostpunk 1 storm is telegraphed in advance in real minutes of play; only the in-fiction chain (refugees, quests, fog) and the "latest day 43" are documented.

## 3. Twilight Struggle: DEFCON, deck eras, scoring cards, the VP track and why the Late War is tense

### Takeaway
Twilight Struggle escalates through three stacked devices on a fixed ten-turn clock: card eras that are physically shuffled in at turns 4 and 8, a single shared VP track with instant-win thresholds (20 VP, or Europe control when Europe is scored), and DEFCON, a single ratchet that restricts where you may act and makes losing the game a possible result of a player's own aggression. Scoring cards must be played the turn they are drawn, so every hand holds a clock the opponent can only guess at.

### Cited Findings

**Structure and deck eras (GMT Deluxe rulebook, read for sections 1 to 12, the Late War scenario, designer optional rules, credits and Designer's Notes)**
- Ten turns; each turn "represents between three and five years" and has "six or seven normal card plays by each player". Turns 1 to 3 have six action rounds and turns 4 to 10 have seven. Hand size is 8 on turns 1 to 3 and 9 on turns 4 to 10. — [GMT Games, Twilight Struggle Deluxe rule book (4.1, 4.5)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- "At the beginning of turn 4, the Mid War deck is shuffled into the draw pile ... At the beginning of turn 8, the Late War deck is shuffled into the draw pile." The new cards are added to the existing draw deck only: "The ignored discards remain in the discard pile for now, but will be reshuffled into the deck in the next reshuffle." Cards played as events marked with an asterisk are removed from the game and never come back. — [GMT rule book (4.1, 4.3, 4.4)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Each card shows an Early, Mid or Late War indicator; the Deluxe edition has 110 cards. The first edition had 103 (Wikipedia). — [GMT rule book (2.2, 2.0)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf); [Wikipedia, Twilight Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)
- Turn sequence: improve DEFCON by 1 (if below 5), deal cards, Headline Phase (each player secretly picks a card, reveal together, higher Ops value resolves first, ties go to the US, scoring cards count as 0), Action Rounds (USSR first), check Military Operations, optional reveal of held cards (tournament), flip the China Card, advance the turn marker, and Final Scoring after turn 10. — [GMT rule book (4.5)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Space Race milestones are one-off bonuses for the first to arrive: two Space Race cards per turn at space 2; opponent shows Headline first at space 4; discard the held card at space 6; eight action rounds per turn at space 8. The designers call the Space Race "your 'safety valve'" for dumping a card whose event helps your opponent. — [GMT rule book (6.4 and the Design Note)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)

**DEFCON as a ratchet**
- DEFCON starts at 5. Any coup attempt in a Battleground country lowers it by one level. Reaching 1 ends the game immediately and "the phasing player ... loses the game". It improves by 1 at the start of each turn if below 5. — [GMT rule book (8.1)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Level effects: DEFCON 4, no coups or realignment rolls in Europe; 3, none in Europe or Asia; 2, none in Europe, Asia or the Middle East. — [GMT rule book (8.1.5)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Required Military Operations: by the end of each turn each player must have spent Ops on coups or war events equal to the current DEFCON number, otherwise the opponent gains 1 VP per missing point. So low DEFCON means a lower quota but fewer legal coup targets; high DEFCON means a bigger quota but more room to act. — [GMT rule book (8.2)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- The example in the rulebook shows the trap: a phasing player plays the Olympic Games event at DEFCON 2, the opponent boycotts it, DEFCON drops to 1, and the phasing player loses. — [GMT rule book (8.1.3 example)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Designers' notes: "we are proud of the interaction of the DEFCON chart with military operations. It really compels each turn to have a diversity of actions that makes for a more tense and exciting game." They also say they assume nuclear war would be a bad thing and, when it happens, "We do not reward failure" (no mushroom cloud animation in the game they cite as inspiration). — [GMT rule book, Designer's Notes](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)

**Scoring, the VP track and instant wins**
- Six regions (Europe, Asia, Central America, South America, Africa, Middle East), with Europe and Asia holding sub-regions. Each region has its own scoring card. A region has Presence (control at least one country), Domination (more controlled countries and more battlegrounds, plus at least one battleground and one non-battleground), or Control (more countries and all battlegrounds). Scoring adds +1 VP per Battleground controlled and +1 per controlled country adjacent to the enemy superpower. Central America domination is worth 3 VP in the base score (Wikipedia) and the rulebook example scores 5 VP in total for the Soviet player. — [GMT rule book (10.1)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf); [Wikipedia, Twilight Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)
- Scoring cards "must be played sometime during the turn they are drawn", may never be held, and an Event cannot force you to hold one. The designers' play note: "Trying to play scoring cards to coincide with your superpower's peak influence in a region is often a crucial factor in winning the game." — [GMT rule book (2.2.1, 4.5, 10.1.5, play note)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- One shared VP track runs from US-20 to USSR-20. "The instant one player reaches a score of 20 VP, the game is over and that player is the winner." All VP from the event or scoring card (for both players) are applied before checking. "If either side Controls Europe, that side wins when the Europe Scoring card is played." Nuclear war is also a win for the opponent. — [GMT rule book (10.2, 10.3.1)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- If nobody has won by the end of turn 10, every region is scored as if its scoring card had just been played; "Reaching 20 VPs does not result in Automatic Victory during scoring at the end of turn 10; however, Control of Europe does grant automatic victory." Otherwise most VP wins; zero is a draw. — [GMT rule book (10.3.2)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)

**Late War content and the late-war scenario**
- Volko Ruhnke's Late War scenario (in the Deluxe rulebook) starts at turn 8 with DEFCON 4, VP at -4, Space Race at "Soviet Space Walk; US Eagle Landed", and the setup removes all asterisked Early and Mid War events except Bear Trap, Camp David, John Paul and Panama Canal. Victory: "After Turn 10 final scoring or if US plays WARGAMES event, US must have 20+ VP or Soviet player wins." Ruhnke's note maps turns to years (turn 8 is 1975 to 1979, turn 10 is 1985 to 1989) and observes that the US begins the post-Vietnam era strongly, "a US Automatic Victory is well within reach". — [GMT rule book, Volko Ruhnke Late War Scenario](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- ⚠️ The base Wargames card text itself was not read (the rulebook's card-history section was only searched for card names, and the scenario rule above is the only place that text appears in the part that was read). The rulebook's card history for WARGAMES discusses brinkmanship and launch-on-warning in flavor text.
- ⚠️ Early War deck size of 35 cards (38 with three optional cards) comes only from a web-search result summary; the 42 and 21 for Mid and Late War are not confirmed by any source. Do not quote them.

**Designers on why it plays this way**
- "The number one constraint on the design was time" (Matthews): "You can play Twilight Struggle from beginning to end in the same time it takes to play the 'short' scenario of many other games." Compromises "sided with playability". Areas become important "just because your opponent thinks they are important—he must be going there for some reason!" — [GMT rule book, Designer's Notes](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Early versions were more complex and simulation-like; playtesting abstracted nearly every mechanic and shrank the event deck. "Many clever and interesting ideas were discarded as being contrary to the fundamental guiding principles of the game – fast play, simple rules, and getting a lot of gameplay value out of the cards." — [Wikipedia, Twilight Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)
- Matthews on cards: each card works as a small rule, giving "enormous complexity and depth to a game without increasing the rule book"; editing one card affected others, and playtesting was "arduous". — [Opinionated Gamers interview with Jason Matthews](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/06/13/the-art-of-design-interviews-to-game-designers-26-jason-matthews/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest; the first quote about complexity and depth also appears in a search-result summary of a different interview, not opened
- Tournament practice shows a tuning problem: the original rule had players reveal held cards at end of turn to stop players from holding a scoring card; modern events show the bottom of the card instead. In most competitions the US gets +2 influence and optional cards are used "obtaining a balance very close to 50%". — [Wikipedia, Twilight Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)

**What reviewers say makes the late war tense, and what they criticise**
- Asymmetry over time: "conventional wisdom" is that the USSR does better early and the USA late, which "creates paranoia" (the USSR must end the game quickly). Scoring spread across many regions stops a player concentrating on one. The reviewer says it takes "about ten three-hour games to totally understand it." — [Brandon the Game Dev, Twilight Struggle: Maintaining Tension](https://brandonthegamedev.com/twilight-struggle-maintaining-tension/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest; that article does not discuss deck phases or the headline phase as tension sources
- Scoring cards: players read each other for clues about which region is about to be scored and feint; DEFCON often hovers near 2 "because the risk favors the current player"; the reviewer lists the ways to win (20 VP, opponent triggers DEFCON 1, Wargames in the late war, Europe control when scored). Criticisms: "Knowledge of deck and event composition is massively important", dice swings are "a structural problem", three hours is long, and a good player "will crush a new player". — [Meeple Like Us](https://www.meeplelikeus.co.uk/twilight-struggle-2005/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- ⚠️ Search-result snippets only: Shelfside says many mid and late cards can upset early plans and that the theme weakens for some players as unfamiliar events dominate the late game; RPGnet and Misutmeeple note players are on edge about which scoring card the other holds. Those pages were not opened. — [Search results for Twilight Struggle late-war reviews](https://www.shelfside.co/reviews-2025/twilightstruggle-review)

### Inferences
- The late war is tense because several pressures coincide at turn 8: the Late War cards enter the same draw pile as the leftover Early and Mid cards, the VP track is probably already skewed (so one scoring card can win outright), the Europe-control instant win is always live, hand size is 9 and each turn has seven action rounds, and DEFCON restricts which regions are still playable. None of this requires more rules text; it is the same four rules interacting at higher stakes.
- Instant-win thresholds (20 VP, Europe control, DEFCON 1) turn the end into many possible finales, but all of them are visible on the board at all times, and the player has a deadline in the hand (the scoring card). The "scheduled climax" is therefore the turn-10 Final Scoring, while the "unscheduled climaxes" are the three instant-win checks.
- The rulebook's decision to exclude 20 VP from the final scoring (but keep Europe) is a deliberate guard so that the last scoring does not feel like a sudden end by arithmetic.

### Gaps
- No source found that quotes Ananda Gupta or Jason Matthews directly on why the Late War is tense, how Wargames was balanced, or why 20 VP was chosen. The Steam Q&A with Ananda Gupta was fetched but the page returned only navigation text; the 55-minute SDHistCon interview and the BoardGameGeek design threads were not reached.
- The extended example of play (rulebook pages 14 to 20) was read only for turns 1 and 2, and the card histories (pages 21 to 30) were searched for specific card names but not read. No exact card counts per era were verified.
- The text and the exact cost of the Wargames event were not read in a source.

## 4. Suzerain (and Kingdom of Rizia): how a political narrative builds to its end

### Takeaway
Suzerain is a long, text-heavy game whose end is a single fixed event (the 1957 election in Sordland, reached on Turn 11 per a ⚠️ search-summary of a walkthrough; the Turn 10 reform or coup point in Rizia) preceded by a crisis around the neighbouring power Rumburg. It escalates by tracking hundreds of small decisions that decide which of nine main endings and about 20 sub-endings are available, and many failures are set up by early choices (for example, the assassin at the final speech). No source I could open mentions a "referendum"; the constitutional reform is a parliamentary and court procedure, not a vote of the people.

### Cited Findings

**Structure and tracked values**
- Suzerain is a text-based government simulation visual novel by Torpor Games (Berlin), published by Fellow Traveller; the player is Anton Rayne, president of Sordland; "The plot is largely limited to a single term of office". Developers say the game has 450,000 words; the base game "can be completed in under twelve hours"; supplementary text is optional; there are secondary decisions that "do not require a narrative-driven deliberation"; the end shows a political compass and statistics. — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game)) (read in full)
- Sordland's status is tracked across five areas (Law, Economy, Military, Welfare, Order). The game has "350+ choices", tracks and gives feedback on most decisions, and "No single action topples the country. Major consequences depend on 'a string of choices'" (lead developer Ata). — [Articy showcase, Suzerain](https://www.articy.com/en/showcase/suzerain/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- Ata describes the design process as "lots and lots of deleting old designs and planning anew"; dialogue is split into categories placed in story "turns" and linked through variable sets. — [Articy showcase, Suzerain](https://www.articy.com/en/showcase/suzerain/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest
- Inspirations cited by the creators: Weber's Politics as a Vocation, Machiavelli's Il Principe, House of Cards, The West Wing, Borgen, Tyranny, Orwell. — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game))

**The base-game ending as a menu of consequences (Suzerain wiki, Endings page)**
- "Suzerain has nine main endings and twenty sub-endings", plus a political compass (four quadrants: Malenyevist, Sordish Reformism, Arcasian Capitalism, Sollism). — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings) ⚠️ first 9,000 of 14,290 characters of the page source read, then the Rizia portion from its heading (roughly 5,000 characters); the end of the Rizia civil war entry and later text were not read. A store listing summary states "9 unique major endings and more than 25 sub-endings"; the two counts disagree, so quote neither without checking.
- Reelection: "Winning the war against Rumburg guarantees re-election unless you get assassinated." Keeping approval high, pulling the economy out of recession and balancing tensions matter too. — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)
- Assassination "will always happen at the final speech prior to the elections" if you angered the Oligarchs or Bluds enough; it can be avoided by getting "Serge's lucky charm before the reforms are passed in the assembly or by establishing Secret State Police". Winning the war without firing Livia Suno during the sex scandal leads to a Rumburg assassination. — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)
- Other endings: Lost Election (the 1957 elections), Impeached, Early Retirement (avoid corruption and the war), Path to Exile (give territory to Rumburg and flee), Coup d'etat (the military), "Hail the Queen" (lose the war to Rumburg), and "World At War" (ally with a superpower and declare war on Rumburg: "Due to Rumburg's nuclear capabilities, a thermonuclear apocalypse will ensue"). — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)
- The constitution reform path: lose the USP primary to Lileas Graf, and with the "A New Hope" sub-ending "your new political party successfully block Lileas Graf's attempt to reform the constitution"; the easiest route is to "deliberately destroy the economy towards the end-game after successfully passing an ultra-reformist constitution." — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)
- In-universe constitution: an amendment needs a proposal by Assembly members with at least 150 signatures, a two-thirds majority in the Grand National Assembly, and a simple majority in the Supreme Court. The page states there has been no amendment since 1929 (as of 1954). — [Suzerain Wiki, Constitution](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Constitution) (read in full)
- ⚠️ Search-result summary of Neoseeker walkthrough pages (pages returned 403, not read): the base game's final turn is Turn 11 in "Chapter IV - Checkmate"; a normal war victory "needs success in both phases of the war plan, no Sordish Depression, and no more than 3 Late-Game Crisis points"; the other six routes go straight into their epilogues. — [Neoseeker, Chapter IV Turn 11](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/walkthrough/Chapter_IV_-_Checkmate_-_Turn_11) ⚠️ search summary only. A Steam guide cited in the same search warns that version 2.0 changes may have altered the war mechanics.

**Kingdom of Rizia**
- The player is Romus Toras, newly crowned monarch; the kingdom has fragile economy, stability and foreign relations after losing the Zille and Pales regions; the player must balance "Rizia's three royal houses". It includes "an optional interactive war chapter with corresponding game mechanics, allowing the player to organize their armed forces and lead them through an armed conflict." Update 3.1.0 "Sovereign" (7 May 2025) "featured a significant overhaul of the Rizia storyline in response to player feedback." — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game))
- Rizia's endings (page marked WIP): at Turn 9 the player can abdicate; "If the conditions for a coup or revolution are met, Estela Toras will warn you during the start of Turn 10 and ask you to leave Rizia"; the Reform ending has the player "disband the royal council, give up your decree powers, and publicly announce your new constitution" during Turn 10; coups by House Azaro, House Toras and House Sazon are triggered by earlier choices, and a "Reform Coup" happens "on your way to Congress to pass the new Constitution" if radical reforms were forced through in secret without first weakening the houses. — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings) ⚠️ page partly read, and the Rizia section is labelled WIP, with several entries "[TBA]"

**Critical reception**
- Metacritic: 81/100 "generally favorable" for PC (Wikipedia digest figure); Der Spiegel called it "perhaps the most realistic video game about politics"; PC Gamer called it "fascinating" and "truly unique". — [Wikipedia, Suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game)) (the Metacritic number appeared in a [WebFetch digest of the same page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game)); the raw plain text I read says only "generally favorable")
- GamingTrend scored it 85: "when things get a bit slow it tends to drag" and "Some sections unnecessarily go on too long"; it took about 6 to 7 hours and replays "could triple your playtime". — [GamingTrend review](https://gamingtrend.com/youve-got-my-vote-suzerain-review/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest; the digest says the review does not discuss the war, election or ending

### Inferences
- Suzerain's finale is built from a few big "gates" (the war with Rumburg, the USP primary, the final speech), each of which reads many flags set much earlier. The player can see the gate coming in the story (a neighbouring power, an election date), but cannot see which early choices will lock an ending.
- Because reelection hinges on the war, the endgame is a single pass/fail event with several mutually exclusive failure endings (military coup, assassination, loss in war, nuclear escalation), each tied to a different set of earlier choices. This yields a high number of distinct endings from one final branch, not from many parallel branches.
- Rizia's rework in 2025 "in response to player feedback" is evidence that the DLC's storyline (including its finale) did not land at launch. The nature of the complaints was not found.

### Gaps
- No source found describing a "referendum" in Suzerain or Kingdom of Rizia; the reform procedures found are legislative and court votes (Sordland) and a decree-and-congress path (Rizia). The user's brief may be using the term loosely.
- No developer statement found that explains hidden values or how many hidden variables exist. A fan guide (Neoseeker, via search summary only) lists "Alignment, Unrest, Opinion" as hidden values; I did not read it and it is not developer-confirmed.
- Torpor Games' own interviews (Paste, via a redirect that returned 404; the Adventure Games Podcast) were not read. The Rizia war chapter's actual rules and the reception of the Sovereign update were not found.
- Rock Paper Shotgun's and PC Gamer's Suzerain reviews were not opened; only Wikipedia's one-line summaries.

## 5. Cross-cutting: making the end visible, keeping choices rising without bloat, one climax versus many

### Takeaway
All five games make the finish visible early through a named, countable thing: a cure bar with milestone pop-ups (Plague Inc.), a storm that has a map object and a quest checklist (Frostpunk 1), a turn track plus deck-era shuffles plus a VP track with fixed ends (Twilight Struggle), an election date and a war with a named neighbour (Suzerain), and a council vote that eventually breaks (Frostpunk 2). They keep the number of decisions modest by using a few strong constraints (laws in an ordered ladder, DEFCON limiting legal targets, scoring cards that must be played, five stat areas) and by letting earlier choices decide which late options exist. Each uses one scheduled main climax plus a small number of triggered side-climaxes.

### Cited Findings (patterns already cited above, collected by question)

**Making the end visible from early on**
- Plague Inc.: 25/50/75/95/100 pop-ups plus a Cure Counter and flasks. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Frostpunk 1: storm timer starts at Winterhome discovery; latest hit day 43 of 48; map fog; quest chain "Final days: Wait for the storm to hit". — [Frostpunk wiki, A New Home](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/A_New_Home); [The Great Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Great_Storm)
- Twilight Struggle: Turn Record Track, deck-era indicator on every card, VP track ends at 20, DEFCON track with printed consequences, and in the late war the scenario states "the Late War period would begin in 1975". — [GMT rule book](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Suzerain: an election in 1957 and a war with a neighbour; in Rizia the warning "at the start of Turn 10" that a coup is coming. — [Suzerain Wiki, Endings](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Endings)

**Rising pressure without a growing decision count**
- Frostpunk: laws are ordered and each is a small step ("creeping normality"), with cooldowns (18 to 45 hours) pacing signings. — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/); [Frostpunk wiki, Laws](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Laws)
- Frostpunk: the storm panic arc is a fixed chain of events whose options depend on prior law choices (Guard Station or Faith Keepers). — [Frostpunk wiki, Fear of the Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Fear_of_the_Storm_(Arc))
- Twilight Struggle: turns are one card play at a time, with 6 or 7 action rounds, so the number of decisions per turn is fixed while stakes rise; the designers said they cut ideas that did not serve "fast play, simple rules, and getting a lot of gameplay value out of the cards." — [GMT rule book (4.5)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf); [Wikipedia, Twilight Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)
- Plague Inc.: late-game decisions are the player's counters at each cure milestone (Drug Resistance at 75%, extra actions at 95%). — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)
- Suzerain: 350+ choices, but major consequences come from "a string of choices", not single choices. — [Articy showcase](https://www.articy.com/en/showcase/suzerain/) ⚠️ WebFetch digest

**One scheduled climax vs many small ones**
- Frostpunk 1 chose a single scheduled climax and ended the game after it. — [PC Gamer interview](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/)
- Frostpunk Endless and Frostpunk 2 Utopia Builder use repeated or optional hazards (cyclical storms; optional "massive storm" objective). Endless storms lose bite as capability grows (the wiki's words). — [Frostpunk wiki, The Great Storm](https://frostpunk.fandom.com/wiki/The_Great_Storm); [Wikipedia, Frostpunk 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk_2)
- Twilight Struggle has one scheduled climax (turn 10 Final Scoring) and three unscheduled instant-win triggers (20 VP, Europe control at the Europe scoring card, DEFCON 1). — [GMT rule book (10.3)](https://www.gmtgames.com/living_rules/TS_Rules_Deluxe.pdf)
- Plague Inc. has two sequential moments: the cure reaching 100% and the game ending only once all infected are cured, with reversals possible in between. — [Plague Inc. wiki, Cure](https://plagueinc.fandom.com/wiki/Cure)

### Inferences (designer-oriented, drawn from the findings, not from any source)
1. **Pattern: a visible, named end object.** Give the finale its own thing on screen from early (a fog, a bar, a track), that moves in coarse milestones, and attach a matching action to each milestone (Plague Inc.'s 75% and 95% tips; Frostpunk's checklist).
2. **Pattern: the player's own success wakes the opposition.** Plague Inc. only starts a cure when the plague is noticed; Frostpunk's storm timer only starts at the Winterhome discovery; Twilight Struggle's coups lower DEFCON and restrict the board. For a lab game, rival labs, regulators or public alarm should be triggered by the player's visible growth, with each response having a published threshold.
3. **Pattern: an ordered ladder of concessions, with a final rung that cancels the pressure at a cost.** Frostpunk's laws and the Execution Platform; Plague Inc.'s governments escalating from Pandemic Alert to Martial Law. The "cancel pressure" rung needs a visible price (ending text, a "crossed the line" flag), or the climax is skipped (the PCGamesN "unexpectedly easy" comment).
4. **Pattern: a hard stop after the climax.** Frostpunk dropped post-storm building as anticlimactic; Twilight Struggle ends at turn 10; Suzerain ends at the election. A 30-minute browser game should end at the climax, not after a tail.
5. **Failure to avoid: capacity outgrowing the threat.** Frostpunk Endless storms turn into "a minor annoyance" as the city grows; a late game that scales the player's economy faster than the threat will do the same.
6. **Failure to avoid: a many-system finale without a clear priority.** Frostpunk 2 reviews report information overload, loss of intimacy at scale, and a rushed ending; Suzerain's slow stretches "tend to drag". The finale needs a small number of live numbers on screen, not all of the simulation.
7. **Failure to avoid: hidden locks.** Suzerain's assassination at the final speech is avoidable only through earlier choices (a charm item or the Secret State Police), which is a surprising punishment unless the game telegraphs it (Rizia's Turn 10 warning is the better pattern).
8. **Pattern: multiple instant-win conditions that are always visible.** Twilight Struggle's three triggers keep the whole map relevant. A world-dominance finale can use the same idea if each condition is shown as a number the player and rivals can race.
