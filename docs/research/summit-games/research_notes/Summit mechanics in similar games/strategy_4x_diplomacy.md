# Multi-Party Diplomatic Summits, Congress Votes, Resolutions and Treaties in 4X / Grand Strategy Games

## Civilization VI World Congress (Gathering Storm): proposal flow, A/B options, Diplomatic Favor as voting currency, visibility of others' preferences, emergencies. Plus Civ V World Congress / UN for comparison.

### Takeaway
Civ VI's World Congress runs on a fixed cadence: each session surfaces a slate of pre-defined resolutions (not player-authored), each with two mutually exclusive outcomes and, where relevant, a target (a civ, district type, resource, etc.); civs vote with a Diplomatic Favor currency whose spend also breaks ties, and separate "Emergency" special sessions let the group vote to sanction a civ that did something provocative. Civ V/BNW's older World Congress instead lets the two highest-delegate civs actually *propose* which resolution comes up, which several reviewers considered a more legible, less "randomly generated" system than Civ VI's.

### Cited Findings
- In Civ VI, for each regular-session resolution "every civilization will first choose one of the two outcomes, and if there are multiple possible targets within that outcome, the civilization will then choose a single target, which may be a civilization or it could be a district, a type of terrain feature, or a kind of luxury resource." Voting is plurality (most votes wins) — [World Congress (Civ6) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/World_Congress_(Civ6))
- Confirmed via a full page fetch of the Civilopedia: resolutions are "automatically generated options relevant to the current Era"; players "select a target and choose between two options affecting that target"; the "winning option is determined by plurality vote and becomes binding until the next session." — [World Congress — Civilopedia (Gathering Storm)](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)
- Tie-break rule: "the Civilization that spent the greatest percentage of their Diplomatic Favor when voting on the Resolution will be the winner." — [World Congress — Civilopedia](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)
- Diplomatic Favor is the core currency: "the voting power of each leader depends on their accumulated Diplomatic Favor," which is spent "to purchase additional votes during every session" — more Favor spent buys more votes on a given resolution/treaty. — [Diplomatic Favor (Civ6) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Diplomatic_Favor_(Civ6))
- Favor is refundable in specific cases: full refund if the measure fails or a diplomatic promise is rejected; half refund if the measure passes but targeted a different civ than the one the player voted for. — [World Congress — Civilopedia](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)
- After the June 2019 patch, every civ that voted for the winning outcome/target combination gets 1 Diplomatic Victory point (rewarding correctly predicting the majority, not just voting your preference). — [World Congress (Civ6) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/World_Congress_(Civ6))
- "Scored Competitions" (e.g., World's Fair, Climate Accords) are separate World Congress events where civs compete to contribute the most to a cause; top contributors win rewards including Diplomatic Favor and victory points — a resource-race side-channel rather than a vote. — [World Congress — Civilopedia](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)
- Emergencies: "triggered in response to a serious action by a civilization, such as conquering a city-state, converting a Holy City, or launching a nuclear attack," but there is "still a dice roll" after the triggering act before the Emergency actually fires — not every qualifying act causes one. Once triggered, it "has to be put to a vote through a Special Session," and other civs can then choose to join the coalition against the triggering civ. — [Emergency (Civ6) — Fandom via search snippet, corroborated by Civilopedia fetch](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Emergency_(Civ6))
- Confirmed via Civilopedia fetch: Special Sessions come in (at least) two named types — "Aid Request" (triggered by major natural-disaster damage) and "Military Aid Request" (triggered when a civ is attacked by someone against whom they'd built up many Grievances). The affected civ requests the session; everyone else votes to approve or reject it. — [World Congress — Civilopedia](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)
- Emergency outcome stakes: if the coalition's goal is met in time, participating members get a special reward plus a lump sum of gold; if time runs out, the target instead receives gold and the triggering civ suffers a penalty. — [Emergency (Civ6) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Emergency_(Civ6))
- By contrast, Civ V/Brave New World's World Congress is convened once a civ has researched Printing Press *and* met every other civ — the discovering civ becomes the first host. During each session, only two civs (the host, and whichever member has the next-highest delegate count) can actually **propose** which resolution is voted on — proposal rights are earned/ranked, not procedurally generated for everyone. — [World Congress (Civ5) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/World_Congress_(Civ5))
- Civ V resolution examples: hosting the International Games, trade embargoes, banning specific luxury resources, taxing armies (military unit upkeep tax), and banning nuclear weapon construction. Delegate counts (a civ's voting weight) come from city-state alliances, host status, and current era. — [World Congress (Civ5) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/World_Congress_(Civ5))
- The World Congress is renamed the United Nations once any civ reaches the Information Era, or once at least half the civs in the game reach the Atomic Era; from that point the UN can be prompted to vote on a "World Leader," and winning that vote is the Diplomatic Victory condition. — [World Congress (Civ5) — Fandom via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/World_Congress_(Civ5))
- Civ VI's Diplomatic Victory instead requires accumulating a target number of Diplomatic Victory Points across many sessions/eras rather than one single "world leader" vote. — [PC Gamer — Civilization 6: Gathering Storm review](https://www.pcgamer.com/civilization-6-gathering-storm-review/)

### Inferences
- Civ VI's model (fixed, systemically-generated resolution slate + a spendable favor currency that both buys votes and breaks ties) is the more directly transferable pattern for a small browser game: it removes the need to simulate "who gets to propose" as a separate negotiation, and the favor-spend tie-break gives weight to how much a party cares, not just whether it agrees.
- Civ V/BNW's "only the top two delegate-holders can propose" design creates a built-in power hierarchy (like our lab/government asymmetry) and could map onto "only the player and the strongest rival lab can put forward commitments," but it adds complexity our 20-minute run probably doesn't need since our game already fixes the player as sole proposer.
- The dice-roll-after-trigger step in Civ VI Emergencies (a real event happens, but it only sometimes escalates into a Congress vote) is a useful template for our "someone secretly breaks the deal" step: detection could be resolved as a single check gated by monitoring level, rather than deterministic reveal.

### Gaps
- Exact Diplomatic Favor income rates (how much Favor a civ earns per turn from what sources) were not retrieved — the Fandom "Diplomatic Favor" page returned an HTTP 402 (paywalled/blocked) on direct fetch, so this note relies on a WebSearch synthesis of that page rather than the full source; flagged with ⚠️ below in the coverage statement.
- Did not find a description of what happens mechanically if a civ reneges on a *promise* made during Congress diplomacy (e.g., a "please repeal this" request) beyond the Favor-refund rule; Civ VI's promise-breaking penalties (if any exist distinct from ordinary agenda/opinion effects) were not covered in the sources found.

## Stellaris Galactic Community and Galactic Council: proposing resolutions, diplomatic weight, voting, the Custodian, and how breaking resolutions is handled.

### Takeaway
Stellaris resolves multi-party votes with a continuous numeric "diplomatic weight" score (built from economy, fleet power, tech, and population) rather than one-vote-per-member; a resolution passes if the combined weight supporting it exceeds the weight opposing it, and members can spend Influence to convert a slice of the opposing side's weight to their own. Breaking a passed resolution ("Galactic Law") triggers an automatic empire-wide opinion penalty from every other member plus law-specific sanctions, and leaving the body carries a long cooldown and opinion hit — giving the game two distinct "cost of defection" levers (instant social penalty vs. structural sanctions).

### Cited Findings
- "Any member empire can propose one resolution at a time" and resolutions are tiered, costing 100–300 Influence depending on tier; before formal voting, "members build diplomatic support by voting to support, oppose, or abstain" — abstaining adds no weight to either side. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki (paradoxwikis.com), full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- Voting resolution: "the side with higher combined [diplomatic] weight prevails." Members can also spend "10 Influence per favor (max 100 favors total per resolution) to add 10% of opposing voters' diplomatic weight to their side" — i.e., Influence-bought "favors" let you peel weight away from the other side rather than just adding your own. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- Diplomatic weight composition: 15% of an empire's economic power, 2.5% of fleet power, 40% of technology level, and population-derived weight (2×pop happiness + 1 per happy pop; unhappy pops contribute 1; subversive/enslaved-style pops contribute 0). An assigned Council Official adds +10% weight per leader level. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- Structural cadence: the Senate/Community alternates between a 720-day "Recess" (no voting) and a 1440-day "Session" (resolutions can be voted on); a Galactic Council member can force an emergency measure that instantly ends Recess, usable roughly every 20 years. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- The Custodian is elected from the Council (via a "Nominate Custodian" resolution) and gets concrete powers: +5 monthly Influence, +30% diplomatic weight (replaces the ordinary Council bonus, doesn't stack), −50% Emergency-measure cooldown, −50% veto cost/cooldown, open borders with every member, and the ability to end a Session early (after half its duration) for 200 Influence. The Custodian can also freeze a proposed resolution so it can't be voted on (though the proposer may withdraw and re-propose something else instead), and can change the "Galactic Focus" unilaterally without a resolution. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch; Fanbyte/CBR summaries via WebSearch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- Compliance/enforcement: "If a member of the Galactic Community breaches the Galactic Law, all other members will lose 100 opinion with the breaching empire," and while in breach the empire is also subject to any passed Sanction resolutions targeting law-breakers. — [Galactic community — Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://stellaris.fandom.com/wiki/Galactic_community)
- When resolutions ban a policy the member currently holds, that member is periodically (every 10 years) offered the chance to change the policy to come into compliance, "within ethical constraints" — so breach isn't necessarily permanent, but isn't instantly forced either. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- AI-specific compliance behavior: AI empires "never vote for resolutions causing breaches" and will "always vote for repeal if forced into violation"; separately, AI empires will always leave the Community if they're in breach of 3+ resolutions and can afford the exit cost. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch; corroborated by WebSearch synthesis](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)
- Leaving costs 100–500 Influence scaled to empire size; a departing empire cannot rejoin for 20 years and carries a permanent −30 opinion penalty with current members until it rejoins. — [Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, full fetch](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)

### Inferences
- Stellaris's split between an *immediate, universal* social penalty (−100 opinion from everyone, applied the moment you breach) and a *structural, resolution-specific* punishment (Sanctions) maps well onto our design: "breaking a pacing-summit commitment" could similarly carry both an instant reputation hit with all parties and a specific mechanical consequence tied to which commitment was broken (e.g., breaking the compute cap specifically triggers something different from breaking the pause-AI-research commitment).
- The Custodian's reduced-cost veto and ability to freeze proposals is a "structural power imbalance" pattern — in a lab/government summit, an analogous role (e.g., "whichever government has the most inspection leverage") could get a cheaper veto or the ability to stall a specific commitment before it's even voted on.
- The 10-year "chance to come into compliance" grace mechanic (rather than perpetual breach) suggests our own broken-commitment state need not be permanent — a party could get an opportunity to walk it back at a cost, rather than being flagged as a defector for the rest of the run.

### Gaps
- Did not find confirmation of exactly *how* a breach is detected by the game (i.e., is it deterministic/automatic the instant the empire's own policy state contradicts the law, or is there a hidden/probabilistic detection roll analogous to Civ VI's Emergency dice roll?). The sources describe consequences of breach but not a detection mechanic with uncertainty — Stellaris's version appears to be state-based and automatic (the game already knows your policies), not investigation-based, which is a meaningful contrast with what our design wants (secret-breaking + monitoring-dependent detection).
- Steam Community discussion further indicates strong player dissatisfaction that AI voting can feel arbitrary/"meaningless" (see criticism section below), but that's about resolution *outcomes* being perceived as bad, not about the vote-resolution math itself, which is well documented above.

## Endless Space 2 Senate/Laws, Terra Invicta council/nations, Victoria 3 diplomatic plays, EU4/CK3 truces and alliance-breaking, HOI4 factions, Alpha Centauri planetary council.

### Takeaway
Across these games, "multi-party group decision" is handled by very different structures: Endless Space 2 uses an internal parliamentary system (parties/laws) rather than an inter-empire congress; Terra Invicta and Alpha Centauri use faction-vs-faction councils with population- or mission-based voting weight and a governor/leader role with veto power; Victoria 3 replaces "vote on a resolution" entirely with a real-time escalating multi-party negotiation ("diplomatic play") where third parties are actively recruited with concrete offers; and EU4/CK3/HOI4 model treaty-breaking as direct, quantified opinion/trust/reputation penalties rather than a formal congress mechanism at all.

### Cited Findings
**Endless Space 2 (internal, not inter-empire, but instructive for "party has to be won over"):**
- The Senate is a single empire's internal government body; "two to six laws may be active at a given time, depending on the current Government Type," and most laws require a specific political party to hold power plus a minimum political-experience level. — [Senate — Endless Space 2 Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://endless-space-2.fandom.com/wiki/Senate)
- Voting a law "requires paying its cost immediately"; each party has laws that auto-activate once that party leads; elections occur every 20 turns, and parties gain experience while in power, unlocking higher-tier laws as they level up. — [gamepressure.com Endless Space 2 government guide; Endless Space 2 Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.gamepressure.com/endless-space-2/governments-elections-introducing-laws/z79c74)

**Terra Invicta (faction vs. faction over control of nations, with an explicit hidden-relationship score):**
- Diplomacy is councilor-to-councilor contact for trading or bribing to improve relations. Faction relationships run on a "FactionHate" float score, tracked *separately for every pair of factions* — i.e., a distinct bilateral hidden relationship value per pair, not a single shared reputation. — [Diplomacy — Terra Invicta Wiki (Hooded Horse), via WebSearch synthesis](https://wiki.hoodedhorse.com/Terra_Invicta/Diplomacy)
- Trade is explicitly called out as the only action that reduces hatred directly: an even trade lowers it by −9, a favorable trade by −18. — [Diplomacy — Terra Invicta Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://wiki.hoodedhorse.com/Terra_Invicta/Diplomacy)
- Nations themselves are won or lost via councilor missions (Control Nation, Coup d'État), not via a voted resolution — control is contested directly rather than negotiated communally. — [Nations — Terra Invicta Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://terrainvictagame.fandom.com/wiki/Nations)

**Alpha Centauri Planetary Council:**
- Each faction can convene the council roughly once every 20 years (the elected Planetary Governor only waits 10); apart from Governor/Supreme Leader elections, each faction gets exactly one vote, and the Governor holds near-absolute veto power over motions. — [Sid Meier's Alpha Centauri — Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Sid_Meier's_Alpha_Centauri)
- For the Governor/Supreme Leader election specifically, voting weight switches from one-vote-per-faction to a population-and-bonus-weighted count (total population plus modifiers from faction abilities and secret projects). The Governor also gets automatic infiltration of every other faction and a commerce bonus. — [Sid Meier's Alpha Centauri — Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Sid_Meier's_Alpha_Centauri)

**Victoria 3 diplomatic plays (full mechanics fetched from the wiki):**
- A diplomatic play runs through three phases on an escalation meter: Opening Moves (20 days, only overlords/subjects auto-join, target sets one free demand, no backing down yet), Diplomatic Maneuvering (60 days, the main phase — countries can join without being swayed, declare permanent neutrality, or be swayed to a side), and Countdown to War (20 days, locked — no new joiners or demands, backing down is the main option left before war triggers at 100% escalation). — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)
- Adding a demand or swaying a country pauses the escalation timer for 5 days per action (cap of 20 days from up to four simultaneous actions), which is how a play can run past its nominal 100-day length. — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)
- Sway resources ("maneuvers") are rank-gated: Great Powers get 100, Major Powers 75, Minor Powers 60; specific tech (Philosophy line) adds +25% per tech, +50% from the final one. Concrete sway costs: an "obligation call" costs 10 maneuvers, a subject transfer 5–10 maneuvers. — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)
- Swayed parties are won over by a real offer (e.g., promising war reparations from the loser, or a slice of territory), and a party can be re-swayed away by a better counter-offer from the other side; abandoning a side costs a −50 relations penalty with the remaining members of that side. — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch; PCGamesN Victoria 3 diplomatic plays guide, via WebSearch synthesis](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)
- Diplomatic demands generate "Infamy" (a quantified aggression score) via a formula factoring in base value, population, state modifiers, and the initiator's rank; a Great Power initiator gets +20% infamy, an unrecognized major power +50%. Escalating a "secondary" demand (one that can only be won through actual war) to "primary" status (winnable just by the other side backing down) costs an extra 50% maneuvers and duplicates the original demand's infamy. If the defender backs down, the attacker gets 75% of their spent infamy refunded, though relationship damage remains. — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)

**EU4 / CK3 — breaking bilateral agreements (no congress, but strong opinion/trust/reputation modeling):**
- EU4: breaking an alliance costs the ally −50 opinion of you; a trust value (default 50) that's low imposes negative modifiers on diplomatic actions with that nation, and trust ≥80 blocks that nation from rivaling you (though not from wanting your land). Breaking a truce or royal marriage can tank a nation's "Diplomatic Reputation" by up to −5, making even friendly nations refuse future proposals — i.e., there's a generalized "known truce/agreement-breaker" reputation stat, not just a bilateral one. Force-breaking an alliance creates a one-way truce (the target can attack you, you cannot attack them). — [Steam Community / Codexery "Diplomatic Reputation" summaries, via WebSearch synthesis](https://codexery.com/europa-universalis/e/gameplay/diplomatic-reputation/)
- CK3: declaring war on an ally costs 250 extra Prestige, a full level of Fame, and a −25 opinion penalty *with every character in the game* for 3 years — i.e., breaking faith with one ally damages your reputation universally, not just with the betrayed party. Refusing a war-leader's call to arms lets the war leader demand Gold/Prestige/warscore contribution within a year; failing that yields a further −50 opinion with the war leader and a lost Fame level. — [Crusader Kings III forum/Steam summaries, via WebSearch synthesis](https://gamerant.com/crusader-kings-3-how-form-alliances/)

**Hearts of Iron IV factions:**
- Leaving a faction requires not currently being at war alongside other faction members; after leaving, the ex-member carries a −75 "Faction Traitor" opinion penalty specifically with the former faction leader (a targeted, not universal, penalty). The faction leader can expel a member even mid-war, or dissolve the faction if only their own subjects remain; if the leader ceases to exist, leadership passes to the largest remaining member (or, if the faction's Rules permit any nation to take over, whoever pays 200 Political Power). — [Hearts of Iron IV forum/Steam/wiki summaries, via WebSearch synthesis](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/leaving-a-faction-is-it-a-big-deal.1513167/)

### Inferences
- Victoria 3's "sway with a concrete, revocable offer, and parties can flip to a better counter-offer" pattern is the clearest analog for making rival labs/governments feel like they have real, biddable interests rather than a fixed alignment — it suggests our pacing summit could let the player (or rival AI actors) offer something concrete to bring a wavering party on-side, and have that commitment be defectable if a better offer appears later.
- The contrast between EU4's *universal* reputation stat (a general "does this actor break promises" score visible to everyone) and CK3's *mixed* model (a universal opinion hit plus a targeted worse hit with the direct victim) suggests our detection/reputation system should probably do both: a general "this lab breaks deals" flag that colors future summits, plus a sharper penalty specifically with whichever party's commitment was violated (e.g., breaking the West–East inspection line should hit relations with the inspecting government hardest).
- HOI4's faction-leader veto/expulsion power and Alpha Centauri's Governor veto both show a common pattern: whoever "hosts" or leads the coalition gets outsized structural power (expulsion, veto, tie-break) beyond their raw vote share — worth considering whether the player (as summit convener) should get an analogous asymmetric power, e.g. proposing which three commitments are even on the table (which the brief already gives them) plus a tie-break vote.

### Gaps
- All of the Endless Space 2, Terra Invicta, Alpha Centauri, EU4, CK3, and HOI4 findings above are sourced from WebSearch-synthesized snippets of wiki/forum/guide pages rather than full page fetches (the assistant did not independently open and read each full source page) — flagged with ⚠️ in the coverage statement. The Victoria 3 diplomatic-play findings and the Stellaris Galactic Community findings were confirmed via full-page WebFetch and are more reliable.
- Could not confirm whether Endless Space 2 has any *inter-empire* congress/senate at all (distinct from its per-empire internal Senate) — the searches only surfaced the internal-government version, so if the game does have a multilateral diplomacy body it wasn't found in this pass.

## Hidden or uncertain intentions of other parties: Civ's "agendas," Victoria 3's "sway," and comparable mechanics.

### Takeaway
The clearest, best-documented "hidden intent" system among the games surveyed is Civilization VI's leader Agendas: every AI leader has one *visible* historical agenda (revealed on first contact) and one or more *hidden* agendas that must be uncovered via espionage, and the AI's friendliness/hostility toward the player tracks whether the player's behavior lines up with those agendas. Victoria 3's sway mechanic is a related but different kind of uncertainty: it's not about hidden long-term motives but about a live, quantified "will this offer be enough" calculation (preference score vs. neutrality value) that the player can influence in real time with concrete incentives.

### Cited Findings
- Every Civ VI AI leader starts with two agendas: "one that is unique to the leader and is revealed when the player meets him or her for the first time, and one that is 'hidden' and can only be discovered through espionage." — [Agenda (Civ6) — Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Agenda_(Civ6))
- The visible ("historical") agenda is a fixed personality tied to the leader's real-world characterization (example given: Victoria of England wants the "Sun to Never Set," driving her to expand across continents, befriend nearby empires, and clash with those who block her expansion). — [Fandom "Why Civ 6 Leader Agendas Mean You'll Never Play the Same Game Twice", via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.fandom.com/articles/1281-why-civ-6-leader-agendas-mean-youll-never-play-the)
- The hidden agenda is drawn from "a pool of motivations... randomly assigned to leaders" (e.g., wanting to befriend all nearby city-states, or maximizing culture/tourism); Gathering Storm expanded this so each leader has two hidden agendas — one weighted to early game, one to late game — in addition to the fixed historical one. — [Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.fandom.com/articles/1281-why-civ-6-leader-agendas-mean-youll-never-play-the)
- General rule: AI leaders become friendlier toward players who respect their agendas (visible or hidden) and more hostile toward those who defy or ignore them — so the hidden agenda still silently shapes relationship swings even before it's uncovered; only the *label/reason* is hidden, not its effect. — [Fandom, via WebSearch synthesis](https://www.fandom.com/articles/1281-why-civ-6-leader-agendas-mean-youll-never-play-the)
- Victoria 3's sway "acceptance depends on preference scores versus neutrality values, influenced by relations, attitudes, and ideological alignment" — a hidden or semi-hidden calculation the player influences with concrete offers (war goals, subject transfers, obligation calls) rather than reads directly. — [Diplomatic play — Victoria 3 Wiki, full fetch](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Diplomatic_play)

### Inferences
- Civ VI's split between a *visible* fixed trait and a *hidden, discoverable* trait maps directly onto a design where, e.g., each rival lab has one publicly-known priority (e.g., "Lab X is racing for capability") and one hidden leaning (e.g., secretly willing to accept a compute cap, or secretly planning to defect on the pause) that the player can only learn through some in-game "espionage" equivalent — which would also give a concrete reason to build or reward monitoring/inspection investment before the summit, not just after it.
- Because the Civ VI hidden agenda still *affects* opinion even while unrevealed, our game could similarly let hidden party preferences silently bias whether a rival lab accepts/rejects a proposed commitment, while only letting the player see the *reason* if they've invested in monitoring — preserving mystery without making the outcome pure noise.

### Gaps
- Did not find a description of exactly *how* espionage uncovers a hidden agenda in Civ VI (e.g., which spy mission, what success chance) — the sources describe the existence of the hidden/visible split but not the specific unlock mechanic.
- No source surveyed described a mechanic where a party's *stated* position in a vote/summit could differ from its *true* preference in a way the player must deduce from indirect signals (bluffing) — Civ's agendas and Victoria 3's sway are both "uncertain preference," not "the party is actively bluffing about their vote."

## Treaty compliance monitoring or verification: espionage revealing violations, observers, audits, and how detection works.

### Takeaway
None of the surveyed games appear to implement a distinct "monitoring/inspection effort you invest in, which then increases your odds of detecting a specific rival's specific treaty violation" — the closest real analogs are Stellaris's general-purpose spy-network infiltration system (which reveals broad intelligence, not violations specifically) and Civ VI's Emergency dice-roll (a probabilistic gate on whether a provocative act becomes visible/actionable at all). Actual treaty-breach detection in these games tends to be automatic and state-based (the game simply knows and applies opinion penalties) rather than modeled as an investigation with a success/failure chance.

### Cited Findings
- Stellaris espionage: envoys build "spy networks" in a target empire; network strength depends on empire size and requires an assigned spymaster (a network without one "will deteriorate and lose power" over time). Networks feed "intelligence reports" and enable proactive operations grouped into four categories: subterfuge, sabotage, manipulation, and provocations. — [Stellaris Intelligence — Wiki/PCGamesN/TheGamer, via WebSearch synthesis](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Intelligence)
- Infiltration ceiling is a function of both sides' stats: "the maximum infiltration a spy network can achieve depends heavily on the respective Encryption and Codebreaking scores of the two empires" — i.e., detection/counter-detection is modeled as a duel between one side's concealment stat and the other's investigative stat, a directly reusable pattern. — [Stellaris Intelligence — Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Intelligence)
- Separately, some intel (fleet movements, colony info) comes for free from "sensors from fleets, starbases, or colonies" rather than requiring espionage at all — a passive-detection channel distinct from the active spy-network channel. — [Stellaris Intelligence — Wiki, via WebSearch synthesis](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Intelligence)
- EU4 spy networks: building one up carries a real cost/risk — "there are risks with building up a spy network to fabricate claims when the threshold of discovery is above 25 in the spy network," implying EU4 models a discovery-threshold check on the *spying* activity itself, separate from what the spying reveals. — [EU4 Cheats spynetwork command reference, via WebSearch synthesis](https://eu4cheats.com/command/spynetwork)
- Civ VI's Emergency system (see first section) is the clearest example of a probabilistic gate on whether a violation becomes an actionable, votable event: "there's still a dice roll" after a qualifying provocative act, and "not every city capture or natural disaster sets off an emergency resolution." — [Emergency (Civ6) — Fandom / CivFanatics forum threads, via WebSearch synthesis](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/how-do-emergencies-work-now.642203/)
- No source found describes a "monitoring investment directly raises your chance of catching a specific rival's specific violation" mechanic of the kind the brief is asking about (i.e., something that ties a summit-time commitment like "outside testers" or "inspection line" to a post-summit detection roll). Stellaris's law-breach detection is automatic/state-based (the game already tracks each empire's actual policies, so "breach" is simply checked, not investigated), not a hidden fact that must be uncovered.

### Inferences
- Given the absence of a close precedent, the game's planned mechanic — detection odds that scale with how much monitoring was agreed in the summit — appears to be a genuine design contribution rather than a borrowed pattern; the two closest transferable primitives are (1) Stellaris's Encryption-vs-Codebreaking "duel of stats" framing for modeling detection as a contest between the cheater's concealment and the monitor's inspection strength, and (2) Civ VI's "dice roll after the triggering act" framing for keeping detection probabilistic and only sometimes actionable, rather than either fully hidden or fully automatic.
- Because Stellaris treats law-breach as automatically known (no uncertainty) while Civ VI treats provocations as sometimes-invisible, the design decision of "should breaking a commitment always eventually surface, or can it go undetected entirely" is a real fork the two closest genre precedents fall on opposite sides of — worth deciding deliberately rather than assuming one is "the standard."

### Gaps
- Did not find any grand-strategy or 4X game with an explicit "inspectors" or "auditors" unit/mechanic analogous to real-world arms-control verification (e.g., IAEA-style on-site inspection) — this appears to be an underexplored space in the genre, or the search terms used did not surface it. A further pass with terms like "on-site inspection game mechanic" or naming specific games (e.g., Twilight Struggle, other Cold War-themed titles) might find closer precedent but was outside this pass's scope/budget.

## Player and critic reception: praise and criticism of these systems.

### Takeaway
Civ VI's World Congress draws a specific, recurring complaint: it starts voting before every player has necessarily met every other civ, and it presents randomly-generated topics rather than member-proposed ones, which players contrast unfavorably with Civ V's system (start only after everyone's met; top delegates actually propose topics). Stellaris's Galactic Community draws harsher, more frequent player criticism — being called "bland," "meaningless," an "annoying and inconsequential pop-up," and prone to AI voting patterns that "make no sense" — though there is a countervailing minority view that its bonuses (and the endgame Custodian/Emperor path) make it worth engaging with regardless of the vote content.
Reviewer sentiment on Civ VI: Gathering Storm's diplomacy overhaul (World Congress + Diplomatic Favor together) is more mixed-positive than the player-forum sentiment on Stellaris — reviewers called the added diplomatic options a genuine deepening of the game, while also flagging that Diplomatic Victory itself feels "anticlimactic" below Emperor difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- Player complaint (Steam forums): the World Congress in Civ VI "starts without every player having met each other," so civs "vote in the same 'room' while not knowing each other," and players explicitly contrast this with Civ V, which "handled this better by only starting congress when one player had met all others." — [Steam Community discussions, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/289070/discussions/0/3378284761891517701)
- Player-proposed fix cited in the same discussion: use two voting rounds — one to decide which topics come up, one to actually vote — "noting that topics don't just appear randomly at the UN but are brought up by members" in reality, unlike Civ VI's auto-generated slate. — [Steam Community discussions, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/289070/discussions/0/3378284761891517701)
- Domination-focused players specifically question the World Congress's relevance to their playstyle — "why should [they] care about rules imposed by nations they plan to conquer" — a reception point specific to games with mutually exclusive win conditions, likely less relevant to our single-scenario summit design. — [Steam Community discussions, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/289070/discussions/0/3378284761891517701)
- PC Gamer's Gathering Storm review: "The Congress is great when it works," and the expansion's "embellished diplomatic options extend the range of interactions with other leaders, allowing you to work cooperatively towards common goals or pull the strings to your advantage behind the scenes" — net-positive framing, but the "when it works" qualifier signals inconsistency. — [PC Gamer — Civilization 6: Gathering Storm review](https://www.pcgamer.com/civilization-6-gathering-storm-review/)
- A separate review-synthesis line: "the Diplomatic Victory is anticlimactic on difficulties lower than Emperor," suggesting the payoff of accumulating Diplomatic Favor/votes is muted unless the AI is playing at a competent skill level. — [Review synthesis via WebSearch (source review not individually identified in results)](https://www.pcgamer.com/civilization-6-gathering-storm-review/)
- Stellaris player criticism (Steam Community, multiple threads): the Galactic Community is described as "just a bunch of bland, boring decisions that nations can vote on," "an annoying and inconsequential pop-up in the upper right corner of the screen," and "trash" that "will constantly vote in the worst resolutions but vote against the ones most beneficial to everyone," with AI voting patterns called out as making "no sense whatsoever." — [Steam Community discussions, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/281990/discussions/0/3887226763719189927/)
- Counterpoint from the same discussion pool: some players argue there's good reason to join anyway, since "there are a lot of useful bonuses in there that you're getting for basically free," and separately that "becoming a Galactic Emperor is a worthwhile goal" (the endgame unification path that runs through Council mechanics). — [Steam Community discussions, via WebSearch synthesis](https://steamcommunity.com/app/281990/discussions/0/597389609964406762/)
- Note on the Federations expansion (which introduced the modern Galactic Community): pre-Federations, the game's original single federation type had "massive upkeep, little functionality, and a lot of headache, where AI 'partners' would often ruin everything," and post-Federations reviewers noted improvement — "Federations are full of laws, experience bars, and tooltips" — suggesting the current, better-regarded version is itself a response to earlier, harsher criticism of a shallower system. — [Federations expansion coverage via WebSearch synthesis (specific outlet not resolved — see gap below)](https://www.cbr.com/stellaris-federations-galactic-community/)

### Inferences
- The single most consistent, cross-source criticism of Civ VI's Congress is *legibility*: players want to know a topic is coming and who's driving it, mirroring real-world diplomacy, whereas an auto-generated slate feels arbitrary. For our one-shot pacing summit this is less of a risk (the player already knows the topics — the three commitments are player-chosen), but it argues for making the *other parties'* positions on those specific commitments legible before the vote/reveal, not just the resolution outcome.
- The Stellaris "AI votes make no sense" complaint is the sharpest warning sign in this research: if rival labs'/governments' summit responses look arbitrary rather than traceable to a visible-or-hidden preference (agenda-style), players will likely react the same way Stellaris's playerbase did. This strengthens the case (from the Agendas section above) for giving each rival party a legible-enough stance, even if partly hidden, so post-hoc it can be explained.

- Did not locate a dedicated Rock Paper Shotgun review of Stellaris: Federations or the Galactic Community specifically; RPS's own site search only surfaced a Steam news-feed re-post, not an original RPS review, so this key question's RPS-specific ask is only partially answered (Steam forums and other outlets substitute for it above).
- Did not find a comparably detailed critical reception discussion for Endless Space 2's Senate, Terra Invicta's council, Victoria 3's diplomatic plays, or Alpha Centauri's Planetary Council — this pass focused review/criticism search effort on the two most-discussed systems (Civ VI, Stellaris) given the 15-tool-call budget, so sentiment on the others is a genuine gap rather than a confirmed absence of criticism.
