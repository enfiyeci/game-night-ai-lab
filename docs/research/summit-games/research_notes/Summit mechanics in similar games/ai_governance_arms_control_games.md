# AI Governance and Arms-Control Games: Negotiation, Verification, and Defection Mechanics

## Intelligence Rising: roles, turn structure, treaty/defection handling, and published lessons

### Takeaway
Intelligence Rising (Shahar Avin, Ross Gruetzemacher, James Fox, CSER/FHI, running since 2019) splits players into four teams — two states (US, China) and two multinational tech firms (e.g. Alphabet, Microsoft, Baidu, Tencent) — who commit actions simultaneously each turn and are resolved by a facilitator; treaties are freeform-negotiated, but the one built-in "defection check" mechanic ties cheating directly to how much effort went into a verification regime, and even so, defection "usually" happens at least once per game among agreement games. The fullest published account is a 2024/2025 facilitator-reflection paper drawing on 43 games over four years (September 2020–July 2024), co-authored by the three lead facilitators.

### Cited Findings
- Game structure: "The division of players into four teams, two representing states and two representing multinational technology corporations," with "a turn-based decision-making loop where all teams commit to actions without knowledge of other team's actions for that turn, followed by simultaneous resolution by the facilitator." — [Strategic Insights from Simulation Gaming of AI Race Dynamics, Gruetzemacher/Avin/Fox/Saeri (arXiv:2410.03092)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Each turn splits into "the selection of open-ended 'policy actions' and an 'AI R&D allocation'"; policy actions are capped at two per turn to create "an attention economy," and teams may keep either policy actions or R&D allocation secret from other teams, resolved privately by the facilitator. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Later ("Intelligence Rising Online") versions added: online play (Google Slides/Discord), "formalisation of team victory conditions and collective loss conditions," a simplified two-lane tech tree (language/world-modelling AI vs. reinforcement-learning-in-complex-environments AI) with discrete levels, and predefined "concerns" (negative side effects of AI applications players can mitigate). A 2023 "Policy Tree" mechanic was added later but games using it were excluded from this reflection paper's dataset. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- **Verification/defection mechanic (the closest match to the game's summit design need):** "In the few games with an agreement to slow/jointly develop RTAI [radically transformative AI], defections are common... Following agreement on a bilateral or multilateral AI treaty (led by US and China and supported by the companies), the facilitator asks each party in secret to indicate whether they comply with the treaty or attempt to defect (ease of defection is indicated based on the amount of effort that went into a verification regime); usually there is at least one defection, from a team with significant AI R&D resources." — [same source, Table II item #7c](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Broader pattern: "Even when hastily drafted agreements are made, often there are defections that lead to cyber and/or hard power conflict in the closing minutes... In virtually all of the above cases, the outcome of the game, and the implied survival and flourishing of all of humanity, is left to the roll of dice, something that we should all find unacceptable." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- The facilitators' stated recipe for the best outcomes: "early recognition of the magnitude of the challenge, trust building over years, and eventually international treaties or agreements that include rigorous and robust verification protocols for the involved states and firms. However, even with an agreement in place to slow development until safe RTAI is verifiable at a very high level of confidence and with no successful attempts to violate the agreement by any parties, a dice roll is typically still required to inform the end-of-game narrative — representing the dual challenges of creating effective global governance and of finding robust solutions to RTAI safety." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Race dynamics and destabilisation: races "often result in blocs split by national allegiance where the 'loser' uses military actions to prevent the 'winner' deploying radically transformative AI"; "the 'runner up' will likely feel justified in launching such attacks... without a preexisting treaty regarding other states' autonomy post-RTAI, the deployment of RTAI could be perceived as an existential threat to states not allied with the deployer." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Deception/duplicity is pervasive even absent formal defection checks: "in most games the actors are duplicitous: most use underhanded tactics in private actions, but pretend to be above board and call out unethical behaviour from other actors in public," e.g. "Microsoft signs a treaty to not pursue AI research that further advances the capabilities frontier without all safety research completed, but still pursues such research in secret." — [same source, Table II #1d](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Cyber power is the dominant tool for detecting/exploiting secret R&D and stealing model weights or algorithmic secrets: "One of the most common things seen in all games was the exfiltration of AI model weights or the theft of algorithmic secrets," and exfiltration "when it occurs during the resolution phase of the final turn" can rapidly change race dynamics and lead to destabilisation. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- On uncertainty about rivals: "there is a broad range of perceptions as to how progress toward advanced AI might transpire... it is unclear what is actually going on behind the scenes, and those who only have access to public information are likely to have a limited or flawed perception of the true nature of the situation" (information asymmetry between public/private R&D progress). — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Explicit recommendation for real-world policy, drawn from the game: outcomes leading to positive futures "almost always require coordination between actors who by default have strong incentives to compete... a good foundation for cooperation is being able to agree on the shared risks... but to meaningfully address extreme risks much more robust governance methods are required, combining binding agreements with robust technical verification." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Scale of the evidence base: 43 games, September 2020–July 2024, ~200 hours of facilitated gameplay, at 5 conference games (20-60 participants, strangers) and 38 pre-formed-group games (22 paid workshops, 17 pro bono; participants numbering 4-20, teams of 2-3); at least 39 of 43 games had some participants with expert AI/AI-governance knowledge. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Original design lineage: freeform roleplay exercise first explored July 2017 (Owen Cotton-Barratt, Michael Page at FHI; Shahar Avin at CSER), formalized into the first tabletop version at a September 2019 FHI design sprint, described in Avin, Gruetzemacher & Fox (2020), "Exploring AI Futures Through Role Play" (in Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society). — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Intelligence Rising's IP was transferred in 2024 to a UK charity, "Technology Strategy Roleplay" (Charity number 1200928), set up to manage the game and future AI-futures-exploration efforts. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Independent framing (secondary, from search snippets, not independently verified against a primary source): "you no longer play a person but an organization, similar to a Model UN setting where you negotiate as delegations," including both states and companies. — [About — Intelligence Rising](https://www.intelligencerising.org/about); [Intelligence Rising - CSER](https://www.cser.ac.uk/work/intelligence-rising/)

### Inferences
- The single most transferable mechanic for the game-night lab's summit design is Intelligence Rising's "defection check": after any negotiated agreement, the facilitator privately asks each party whether they comply or defect, and the *chance of getting away with defection* is calibrated to how much verification effort was put into the deal — this maps almost exactly onto the requested "detection should depend on the agreed monitoring level" design goal, and could be adapted as a per-party secret roll/check modified by the summit's chosen inspection commitments (outside testers, compute cap monitoring, the West–East inspection line, etc.).
- The game's finding that defection is "usually" present in at least one team even after a treaty, and that even a well-verified agreement still ends in a dice roll representing residual uncertainty, suggests that a summit mechanic should not let heavy verification fully eliminate defection risk — it should reduce the probability of successful/undetected defection, not remove the incentive to try.
- The facilitators' own view — that "actors with sufficient resources and understanding of the risks are rare, and have an outsized potential to increase risk... or to increase safety" — supports designing asymmetric temptation to defect: labs/states with more capability or compute should face a stronger temptation (and stronger reward) for secret defection, consistent with Intelligence Rising's observed pattern that defectors are usually "a team with significant AI R&D resources."
- The pattern that cooperation is "usually only achieved through top-down intervention" (nationalization or public-private partnership) suggests that voluntary commitments among rival labs may need a forcing function (e.g., a government partner co-signing, or high stakes tied to detection) to hold at all in a tabletop context.

### Gaps
- The exact procedural details of "how ease of defection is indicated based on the amount of effort that went into a verification regime" (e.g., is it a explicit dice-roll modifier, a threshold, a facilitator judgment call?) are not spelled out numerically in the available paper — the paper describes the mechanic qualitatively but does not publish the underlying probability table or rule text. The original rulebook/game materials (not published openly, per intelligencerising.org) would be needed for exact mechanics; this is a gap.
- I could not access intelligencerising.org's own rules or "what to expect" pages beyond the About page (site returned HTTP 403 on a direct fetch of cser.ac.uk, and the About page itself stated it contained no gameplay-mechanic detail); so the roles/turn-structure/verification detail above rests entirely on the Gruetzemacher et al. facilitator-reflection paper, not the game's own primary rulebook.
- The original Avin, Gruetzemacher & Fox (2020) paper ("Exploring AI Futures Through Role Play," arXiv:1912.08964) could not be retrieved as readable text (WebFetch returned it as undecoded binary/PDF-stream data, and it was not re-attempted via the PDF reader after the 2024 reflection paper already supplied thorough mechanical detail) — ⚠️ this earlier primary source on the original tabletop design was not read; the mechanics above come from the later (2024) reflection paper's own description of "the game in its current form," which the authors state "was largely consistent over the period when the relevant games were being facilitated."

### Takeaway

## Other AI-risk games: The Treacherous Turn, Universal Paperclips, AI safety game jam entries, AGI-race board/card games

### Takeaway
None of the other AI-risk games surveyed model multi-party treaty negotiation or verified/defected commitments in the way Intelligence Rising does — The Treacherous Turn is a solo-AI-vs-GM TTRPG about deception and escape rather than inter-state negotiation, Universal Paperclips is a single-player incremental game with no negotiation at all, and the AGI-race board games found (Race to AGI, Lab to AGI) are recent, small releases whose published materials do not yet document any treaty/verification/defection subsystem.

### Cited Findings
- The Treacherous Turn: "an open-source, research-supported tabletop role playing game in which the players collectively act as a misaligned AI in the modern world," where players "collectively play the part of a single character: an artificial general intelligence (AGI)" scheming against humans controlled by a Game Master; its playtest scenario "A Game Called Reality" centers on an AI in a nursing home trying to reach the internet. — [AI takeover tabletop RPG: "The Treacherous Turn," LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/b5EqwQZw7ww2K28Ki/ai-takeover-tabletop-rpg-the-treacherous-turn); [The Treacherous Turn is finished!, LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/qFZcdhMJRXrZZ8bTJ/the-treacherous-turn-is-finished-ai-takeover-themed-tabletop)
- Universal Paperclips (Frank Lantz) is a single-player incremental/idle game illustrating instrumental convergence and a paperclip-maximizer AI that "does nearly anything in its power to make more paperclips" and can end with "the AI succeed[ing] in converting all the matter in the universe into paperclips" — there is no multi-agent negotiation, treaty, or verification system; it teaches misalignment/goal-optimization risk through solitary escalation, not summit dynamics. — [Universal Paperclips, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Paperclips); ["The paperclip game is still the best AI lecture ever written," woah.dev](https://woah.dev/sightings/the-paperclip-warning/)
- "Race to AGI: The Board Game" (BlueDot Impact-linked project, racetoagi.fun) is a "2–6 player strategy game about the race for artificial general intelligence," where players "claim territory on a procedural hex map, wire sites together, and turn five scarce resources into compute, labs and data centers," and can "trade resources through strategic investments with rival factions" — but the published game page does not document any formal alliance, treaty, verification, or defection-penalty system (it is still described as in development). — [Race to AGI — Build. Scale. Survive.](https://racetoagi.fun/); [Race to AGI: The Board Game, BlueDot Impact](https://bluedot.org/projects/race-to-agi-the-board-game)
- "Lab to AGI" is described as a cooperative (not competitive) game for 1–4 players who "lead a team inside the same company to build useful intelligence while keeping public trust and closing the gap between capability and safety" — i.e., it is intra-lab cooperative safety-vs-capability balancing, not inter-lab/inter-state negotiation with verification. — [Lab to AGI — Build safe AGI. Together.](https://labtoagi.com/)
- A general-audience piece frames the current wave of AI-race-themed games (including Race to AGI) as attempts to make the AI race's market/hype dynamics tangible to a lay audience. — [Seeing the future of AI — through a board game, Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/03/03/ai-race-board-game-simulation)

### Inferences
- Of the games surveyed outside Intelligence Rising, none appear to have a published, tested verification/defection subsystem comparable to Intelligence Rising's "defection check" — this reinforces that Intelligence Rising (and, by extension, formal arms-control inspection-game theory, covered below) is the most directly reusable precedent for the requested summit mechanic, rather than the newer AI-themed board games, which are largely economic/racing simulations without a negotiation layer.
- No CSET-branded or explicitly-named "Decisive Point" AI wargame report was found; CSET's public AI-wargaming-adjacent output in the sources checked is about AI-enabled military decision support (not AI-race/negotiation wargaming), so it does not appear to be a source of summit-negotiation lessons for this project. RAND's most relevant public wargame is "Camp(ai)gn," an operational US-China conflict wargame focused on AI's battlefield effects, not treaty negotiation or verification — also not a close match to the requested design question.

## Nuclear/arms-control simulations with verification, and how monitoring level changes cheating

### Takeaway
The clearest, most rigorous precedent for "detection should depend on the agreed monitoring level" is not a tabletop game but the formal game-theoretic literature on "inspection games" (Dresher 1962 onward, Avenhaus et al.), which models exactly this relationship mathematically and includes a documented counterintuitive result: better verification technology can paradoxically be used *less* often, because its mere availability deters cheating without needing constant use. Among actual educational simulations, the IAEA-based "Safeguard Game" (Sweden/Tomsk, since 2006) is a directly comparable two-team inspector-vs-diverter design; Princeton's "Plan A" is a scripted escalation visualization, not an interactive negotiation/verification game, and so is a weaker match despite being widely cited in this space.

### Cited Findings
- Inspection games, introduced by Dresher in 1962 for arms-control/non-proliferation contexts, model "a situation in which an inspector verifies the adherence of an inspectee to some legal obligation, such as an arms control treaty, where the inspectee may have an interest in violating that obligation" — the inspectee, if choosing illegal behavior, "will wish to avoid detection with the highest possible probability," and the resulting game-theoretic analysis "develops propositions relating changes in verification capabilities to changes in the likelihood of cheating and the use of verification technology." — [Applications of Inspection Games; Inspection Games, Avenhaus/Canty summary via search](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233238989_Applications_of_Inspection_games); [Arms Control Verification and Other Games Involving Imperfect Detection, American Political Science Review (Cambridge Core)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/arms-control-verification-and-other-games-involving-imperfect-detection/DD51543897FC2F37050F40BBD7649E4D)
- A documented paradox from this literature: "the better the verification technology, the less often it will be employed" — i.e., improved monitoring capability can substitute for monitoring frequency because its deterrent effect alone reduces incentive to cheat. — [search-result synthesis of inspection-game literature, see above sources]
- The IAEA-based educational "Safeguard Game — The IAEA Inspection in an Imaginary State" splits students into an "Inspectors" team and a "Clandestine Headquarters of the Imaginary State" team; inspectors "try to detect diversion of nuclear materials by identifying possible diversion routes and putting safeguards systems in place," while the clandestine team "try to find a way to divert enough nuclear materials for nuclear weapon manufacturing"; the game ends by comparing the diversion scenarios devised against the safeguards measures proposed. It has run since 2006 via the Swedish Radiation Safety Authority (SSM) with Tomsk State and Tomsk Polytechnic Universities' International Summer Schools on Nonproliferation. — [Safeguard Game — The IAEA Inspection in an Imaginary State, Texas A&M NSSPI](https://nsspi.tamu.edu/safeguard-game----the-iaea-inspection-in-an-imaginary-state-24323/)
- Princeton's "Plan A" is described as "a plausible step-by-step escalation of nuclear war between the United States and Russia" built from "independent assessments of current U.S. and Russian force postures, nuclear war plans, and nuclear weapons targets" — it is explicitly a scripted "four-minute audiovisual piece," not an interactive negotiation or verification simulation with player choices. — [Plan A: How a Nuclear War Could Progress, Arms Control Association](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2020-07/features/plan-how-nuclear-war-could-progress); [Simulating Nuclear War, Princeton Science & Global Security](https://sgs.princeton.edu/the-lab/simulating-nuclear-war)
- Two directly relevant AI-governance policy papers apply arms-control verification thinking to AI treaties specifically (context/background rather than games themselves): (1) a paper cataloguing "10 verification methods that could detect two types of potential violations: unauthorized AI training" and "unauthorized data centers," organized into "National Technical Means" (minimal/no host-country access needed), "Access-Dependent Methods" (needing host approval), and "Hardware-Dependent Methods" (regulating advanced chip distribution), each with "a description, historical precedents, and possible evasion techniques" — [Verification methods for international AI agreements, Wasil/Reed/Miller/Barnett (arXiv:2408.16074)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16074); (2) a paper arguing nuclear arms-control verification's "foreseeable challenges... would be reduced to levels that were successfully managed in nuclear arms control" for AI treaties, provided states develop "privacy-preserving, secure, and acceptably priced methods for verifying the compliance of hardware, given inspection access" and build "an initial, incomplete verification system... that allow[s] its gaps to be quickly closed if and when the political will arises." — [Nuclear Arms Control Verification and Lessons for AI Treaties, Baker (arXiv:2304.04123)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.04123)
- On treaties without verification regimes at all: "The Ottawa Treaty does not have a verification system but a state may call an all parties meeting to investigate suspicions of another state cheating" — cited as a contrast case (weak/no monitoring, ad hoc dispute mechanism) in the arms-control-cheating literature. — [search-result summary citing arms-control-treaty scholarship](https://www.interactioncouncil.org/publications/scan-existing-arms-control-treaties-lessons-learned)
- Model UN crisis committees run "speech-making, moderated caucuses and resolutions" in a "fast-paced dynamic environment" with a "'front room' " of delegates and a "'back room'" of crisis staff who adjudicate secret actions/consequences — a GM-adjudicated secrecy structure structurally similar to Intelligence Rising's private-facilitator-resolved secret actions, though no source found ties this specifically to arms-control verification/cheating scenarios. — [Guide to Crisis Front-Room in Model UN](https://www.allamericanmun.com/crisis-front-room-in-model-un-guide/); [Guide to the Back-Room in Model UN Crisis Committees](https://www.allamericanmun.com/guide-to-the-back-room-in-model-un-crisis-committees/)

### Inferences
- The inspection-game paradox ("better verification tech may be used less often because its deterrent value alone suffices") suggests the summit design should let a strong monitoring commitment (e.g. the West–East inspection line) reduce defection probability even when it is not constantly "rolled against," rather than requiring an active check every turn — this could simplify the mechanic (e.g., a static probability-of-detection modifier per commitment level, rather than a per-turn inspection budget to spend).
- The IAEA Safeguard Game's two-team, adversarial "inspector vs. diverter" structure combined with Intelligence Rising's private-facilitator defection check both point toward the same implementable pattern for the lab-management game: (1) a public negotiation phase where commitment levels are chosen, (2) a private, facilitator/system-resolved check per party of "did they defect," with (3) the odds of getting caught set by which monitoring commitments (outside testers, compute caps, pooled research audits, the inspection line, etc.) were actually agreed to and honored logistically (funded/staffed) rather than merely declared.
- Given that "the better the verification technology, the less often it will be employed" is a *documented paradox* in the inspection-game literature rather than an intuitive default, the game's designers should be careful not to assume "more monitoring = more/harder checks" is automatically the fun or realistic choice — sometimes the realistic effect of strong verification is fewer overt confrontations and more voluntary compliance, which may need to be represented narratively (e.g. fewer defection attempts get *attempted* at all under high verification, not just fewer succeeding).

### Gaps
- No interactive nuclear-arms-control simulation game was found that publishes a rule for "verification level → numeric detection probability" in the way this project's summit mechanic needs; the closest available quantified/rules-based framing is the academic inspection-game literature (game theory papers), not a played tabletop or board game with a rulebook. The Princeton "Gameful Simulations for Exploring Nuclear Arms Control Treaty Verification as a Systems Problem" paper (found via search, King's College London / INMM) looked highly relevant by title but its PDF could not be extracted as readable text in this pass — ⚠️ this source was found but NOT read (the fetch returned only raw/undecoded PDF stream data), so its specific turn-structure and verification-level mechanics are an unverified gap, not a confirmed finding. It should be re-fetched (e.g., via a PDF-capable reader) before being relied upon.
- The full text of both AI-governance verification papers (arXiv:2408.16074 and arXiv:2304.04123) was not read — only their abstracts/summaries were retrieved via WebFetch's automated extraction. ⚠️ Their specific "10 verification methods" and "three types of nuclear arms control agreements" content is reported here only at the abstract level of detail; the full methods/case-study sections were not reviewed.
- No specific published account of Model UN crisis committees being used to teach or simulate arms-control verification/cheating dynamics (as opposed to general crisis diplomacy) was found; this connection is plausible by structural analogy (secret "backroom" adjudication) but unconfirmed by a direct source.

## Coordination findings: how often deals hold, what makes defection tempting, how rival uncertainty is represented

### Takeaway
Across the sources found, the single most concrete, quantified-in-spirit finding is Intelligence Rising's: agreements are rare to begin with, and even when reached (including with explicit verification provisions), defection by at least one party is the norm rather than the exception, with the temptation concentrated in whichever team holds the most AI R&D capability; uncertainty about rivals is represented through facilitator-mediated information asymmetry (public vs. secret actions) rather than any statistical/probabilistic "confidence" mechanic exposed to players.

### Cited Findings
- "Cooperation is hard, usually only achieved through top down intervention (e.g., PPP or nationalisation): true nationalisation is easier in China whereas public private partnerships (PPPs) are easier in the U.S., and even when agreements are in place to slow progress and focus on safety ensuring trust is difficult and deception is common." — [Strategic Insights from Simulation Gaming of AI Race Dynamics (arXiv:2410.03092)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- "In the few games with an agreement to slow/jointly develop RTAI, defections are common" and "usually there is at least one defection, from a team with significant AI R&D resources" — directly quantifying (qualitatively) that defection is the norm and that capability/resource level predicts who defects. — [same source, Table II #7c](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Facilitators' own conclusion on what enables success: "Outcomes leading to positive futures almost always require coordination between actors who by default have strong incentives to compete... Actors with sufficient resources and understanding of the risks are rare, and have an outsized potential to increase risk... or to increase safety." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Uncertainty about rivals is modeled through secrecy/information asymmetry rather than explicit probability: teams may keep policy actions or R&D allocation secret, resolved privately by the facilitator, and players report "it is unclear what is actually going on behind the scenes, and those who only have access to public information are likely to have a limited or flawed perception of the true nature of the situation." — [same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)
- Formal inspection-game theory offers the sharper, quantified version of the same idea: verification capability changes "the likelihood of cheating," with the counterintuitive finding that stronger verification technology can be used less frequently since deterrence substitutes for active checking. — [Applications of Inspection games](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233238989_Applications_of_Inspection_games)
- A tangential but notable data point from an unrelated genre (LLM-agent nuclear wargames, not human tabletop play, included here only as a documented contrast on "how often deterrence holds under uncertainty"): in one study of AI models playing wargame scenarios, "crossing the nuclear threshold caused opponents to de-escalate only 14% of the time" across the tournament, suggesting deterrence/threat credibility failed far more often than it held in that specific automated-agent context — this is a different type of "game" (models playing against each other, not designed tabletop mechanics) and should not be over-generalized to human-facilitated summit games, but is included as a directly relevant, source-backed data point on defection/escalation rates under uncertainty. — [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists reporting, cited via search synthesis](https://thebulletin.org/2023/12/wargames-and-ai-a-dangerous-mix-that-needs-ethical-oversight/amp/)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the Intelligence Rising finding (defection is usual, concentrated among the most capable actor) and the inspection-game paradox (verification strength changes cheating likelihood, sometimes in non-obvious ways) suggest the summit mechanic for the AI-lab game should: (a) make defection tempting in proportion to a lab's current capability lead (mirroring "usually a team with significant AI R&D resources" defects), (b) let the chosen monitoring level (from the six proposed commitments — outside testers, compute cap, launch delay, pooled safety research, pause AI-run research, West–East inspection line) set a detection-probability modifier rather than a guarantee, and (c) preserve genuine information asymmetry for players (they should not see rivals' true compliance state, only public signals plus whatever the inspection commitments reveal) — directly reproducing Intelligence Rising's facilitator-mediated secrecy model.
- No source in this research provides a numeric base rate for "how often deals hold" across many games in percentage terms (e.g., "X% of games saw an agreement survive to the end") — Intelligence Rising's paper reports the qualitative pattern ("usually at least one defection") from 43 games but does not tabulate a hold/break percentage; a designer wanting a numeric starting point would need to approximate from the qualitative account or design their own playtesting-calibrated rate.

### Gaps
- No source gives a quantified "percentage of agreements that hold" across any of the surveyed games or the inspection-game literature; all available findings are qualitative ("usually," "common," "rare"). This is a genuine gap for anyone wanting to calibrate a numeric probability table directly from precedent, rather than through original playtesting.
- The AI 2027 TTX's own coordination/defection dynamics remain undocumented publicly (see Gaps under that section above), so no comparison point exists there for "how often deals hold" specifically in an AI-race TTX outside of Intelligence Rising.
