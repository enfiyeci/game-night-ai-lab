# Narrative and Management Political Games: Staging Negotiations, Council Votes, and Summits

## How does Frostpunk 2's Council present a vote as a scene — delegates, vote prediction/tally, and negotiation (promises, bribes, threats, persuade)?

### Takeaway
Frostpunk 2's Council is a 100-delegate chamber split across factions; before a vote the game shows each faction's size and leaning (For/Against/Hesitant) so the player can predict the outcome, then lets the player pick one faction to approach and offer exactly one promise-card in exchange for votes. There is no separate "bribe/threaten/persuade" verb set in the base game's council screen — negotiation is a single-slot promise system (with a Heatstamps-cost bribe option appearing in some individual debate/dilemma scenes, not the main vote screen).

### Cited Findings
- The Council consists of 100 delegates drawn from all existing factions; most laws need a simple 51-vote majority, while laws that grant the Steward more power need a two-thirds majority — [Twinfinite via search snippet](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/); corroborated by [Frostpunk 2 Wiki (game-vault.net)](https://frostpunk-2.game-vault.net/wiki/Council).
- To predict the vote, the player checks how many delegates each faction holds and how those delegates are currently leaning; a faction with many delegates that is "mostly hesitant" is the best negotiation target — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/).
- Negotiation is one-at-a-time: the player selects a faction to negotiate with, that faction offers a set of terms, and the player picks exactly one; whichever promise is chosen sways hesitant delegates in that faction, and can even flip delegates who were opposed — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/).
- The promise "cards" on offer include things like: promising to research a specific technology on the idea tree, promising a change to the city plan (building or demolishing something specific), and promising to take action against a specific rival party — [TheGamer, via search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/frostpunk-2-council-laws-negotiate-complete-guide/).
- Hard constraints on the negotiation economy: only one active promise per faction at a time, and only three active promises total across all factions — [TheGamer, via search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/frostpunk-2-council-laws-negotiate-complete-guide/).
- Factions will not negotiate on laws that touch their favored or disfavored ideology — they will always vote "For" laws matching their own ideology and "Against" laws matching an opposing ideology, no matter what is offered — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/).
- A higher Trust rating with a faction makes its delegates more likely to vote For a law being proposed — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/).
- Bribery does exist as a dialogue option in some council-adjacent debate/dilemma scenes — e.g., a choice labelled "Bribe their leaders" costing Heatstamps has been documented in at least one council debate — [Frostpunk 2 Wiki via search snippet](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/frostpunk-2-best-laws-council-guide/) and [gamepressure.com council guide, via search snippet](https://www.gamepressure.com/frostpunk-2/council/zf115fb). ⚠️ Both WebFetch attempts to open these pages directly (Twinfinite, gamepressure) returned HTTP 403; this finding is sourced from search-result summaries only, not a full page read.
- Design intent, from Game Director Jakub Stokalski (11 bit studios), read in full via Game Developer: the Council is "a space for the conflicting values to directly clash," and the studio frames its political content as "negotiating a common future, rather than the technicalities of [real-world] parliaments" — [Game Developer, "Frostpunk 2's developers didn't want it to be a 'jackass simulator'"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/frostpunk-2-s-developers-didn-t-want-it-to-be-a-jackass-simulator-) (read to the end).
- Same interview: the developers deliberately did not want to reward cruelty for its own sake — harmful outcomes (starvation, enabling torture) are meant to emerge from the player's own values-driven strategy, not from a "jackass simulator" impulse to be evil; a Zeitgeist system anchors every choice to the Evolvers-vs-Faithkeepers ideological axis so trade-offs stay meaningful rather than becoming a simple morality slider — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/frostpunk-2-s-developers-didn-t-want-it-to-be-a-jackass-simulator-) (read to the end).
- Breaking a promise ("not being able to uphold" it) further upsets the faction it was made to, on top of whatever base reaction they had — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/).
- Broken promises specifically damage Trust "far more than refusing requests outright," and in the Breach of Trust DLC, failing to honor an agreement can trigger a trust penalty severe enough to be difficult to recover from before the next vote — [Big Boss Battle (B3) review, via search snippet](https://bigbossbattle.com/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/) and [CogConnected review, via search snippet](https://cogconnected.com/review/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/).
- In the Breach of Trust DLC, Trust becomes the core resource: losing it can collapse an otherwise-thriving city instantly, "no matter how impressive your infrastructure looks," and if approval collapses or promises fail, the run can fall apart even with the food/heat/evacuation plan otherwise on track — [whisperofthehouse.com Breach of Trust walkthrough](https://www.whisperofthehouse.com/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust/walkthrough) (read via WebFetch); law votes recur roughly every 10 weeks, so a badly timed promise or vote can leave the player politically exposed right when a survival crisis hits.
- General guidance repeated across guides: raise Trust by keeping promises, and never promise something you are not already confident you can deliver, because a broken promise is worse for Trust than never having promised at all — [ScreenRant](https://screenrant.com/frostpunk-2-how-to-increase-trust/) and [GameRant](https://gamerant.com/frostpunk-2-how-raise-gain-increase-trust/), both via search snippet.

### Inferences
- The Council's vote-prediction display (per-faction delegate share + current lean) is the closest analogue to a "vote tally/whip count" screen in a management game, and it doubles as the game's tension mechanism: the player can see exactly how close a vote is and exactly which faction's delegates are movable, which is what makes the single-promise-slot decision consequential.
- The "one active promise per faction, three total" cap appears to be the load-bearing anti-tedium device: it prevents the player from just buying every vote and forces genuine trade-offs, which lines up with 11 bit's stated design goal of meaningful negotiation rather than a bribe-everyone simulator.
- Because Trust is explicitly said to punish broken promises harder than refusals, the design intent seems to be: refusing is a safe "no," lying (promising then reneging) is the costly move — a distinction our summit could reuse (declining a commitment vs. signing and later breaking it should not carry the same weight).

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the exact on-screen visual layout of the council chamber scene itself (e.g., whether named advisor characters speak in bubbles, whether there are character portraits per faction leader, or whether it's mostly abstracted bars/numbers) — the Twinfinite and gamepressure pages that likely describe this UI in detail returned HTTP 403 on fetch, so this rests only on search snippets. This is the single most relevant open question for the owner's "people-first, advisors in bubbles" brief, and it should be checked directly against gameplay footage or a screenshot if a definitive answer is needed before designing the summit UI.
- Whether "threaten" exists anywhere in the base game's negotiation vocabulary (distinct from "promise" and the DLC's Heatstamps bribe) is unconfirmed.

## How does Suzerain (Sordland) and its Rizia DLC stage summits and diplomatic negotiations with other leaders?

### Takeaway
Suzerain has no separate "summit minigame" — diplomacy is delivered through the same portrait-driven, multiple-choice dialogue scenes used for every other decision in the game, with a hidden "negotiating power" value that determines whether a foreign leader accepts a deal, and named leader-specific pressure points that can blow up a negotiation if mishandled. The Rizia DLC adds an explicit multi-nation summit scene (in-story, on turn 9) where several leaders discuss proposal terms together, rather than one-on-one only.

### Cited Findings
- Suzerain's dialogue is entirely portrait- and text-based: each character has a distinct hand-drawn portrait, and when a character speaks their portrait animates to the top of the textbox; choices are presented as multiple-choice prompts — [3rd-strike.com review, via search snippet](https://3rd-strike.com/suzerain-review/) and general game description confirmed across [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game)) and [Neoseeker walkthrough index](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/walkthrough), all via search snippet (WebFetch on the Neoseeker Geopolitics guide and the suzerain.wiki.gg Decision page both returned errors/thin content — see coverage statement).
- These scenes sit inside a persistent frame: a 3D-rendered map of the game's Merkopa region (visualized as sitting on the president's desk), a news tab with six newspapers spanning the political spectrum, and an encyclopedia of people/places — the player clicks through scenes, reads reports and newspaper coverage, and then hits the next decision — [3rd-strike.com](https://3rd-strike.com/suzerain-review/), via search snippet.
- Decisions/negotiations move two kinds of numbers: visible stats (Government Budget, Economic Development) and hidden stats (Alignment, Unrest, Opinion, plus named factions like Malenyevist/Sollist/Capitalist and specific foreign-leader Opinion trackers) — [Neoseeker Decision Prompts Guide](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/guides/Decision_Prompts_Guide_and_List), via search snippet.
- Negotiating with a foreign leader uses a visible "negotiating power" bar; a deal far to the positive side of that bar is much more likely to be accepted — [Steam community discussion, via search snippet](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1207650/discussions/0/4358994946501077678/).
- Individual leaders have distinct, named failure conditions rather than one generic "approval" number: e.g., Wehlen's president wants the player to cooperate on a named joint operation and will walk away from the table entirely if not given everything demanded; Lespia's leader is easy to anger via unrelated actions (buying up a contested resource field, or rejecting an arbitration offer), which poisons the negotiation before it starts — [Neoseeker Geopolitics guide summary via search snippet](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/guides/Geopolitics).
- In the Kingdom of Rizia DLC, there is a dedicated summit on in-game "Turn 9" where multiple national leaders discuss the terms of proposals together, rather than the base game's more one-on-one diplomatic calls — [search-result synthesis of Suzerain: Kingdom of Rizia walkthrough/wiki pages, via search snippet](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain-kingdom-of-rizia/walkthrough) and [Suzerain Fandom wiki, Rizia](https://suzerain.fandom.com/wiki/Rizia).

### Inferences
- Suzerain's model (persistent map/newspaper "war room" frame + portrait dialogue scenes + a hidden negotiating-power meter + per-leader named pressure points) is a strong template for a browser game because it needs no bespoke minigame engine — a summit is just the existing dialogue system pointed at foreign characters, with one extra hidden number (negotiating power) gating success.
- The design pattern of giving each faction/leader one or two specific, narratively-flavored "things they care about" (rather than one generic meter) appears repeatedly across Suzerain and Frostpunk 2's ideology-locked factions — this suggests our four AI labs and two governments should each carry 1–2 named, concrete asks/red-lines rather than a single "trust" slider, so the summit reads as people with agendas rather than a spreadsheet.

### Gaps
- I could not get a first-hand description of the exact visual staging of a Suzerain summit scene (camera framing, whether multiple leader portraits appear simultaneously in the Rizia multi-leader summit, or whether it's sequential one-on-one exchanges cut together). The Neoseeker Rizia Geopolitics guide and the Rizia DLC's official page were found via search but not opened in full — flagged in the coverage statement. This should be checked against gameplay video if the owner wants the exact multi-leader framing.
- No source directly confirmed whether the Rizia summit shows the other leaders' stances via an explicit "who's currently for/against" tally in the way Frostpunk 2's council does; this is unconfirmed and should not be assumed.

## How do other management/narrative games (CK3, Tropico 6, Democracy 4, Reigns, Disco Elysium, Mass Effect/Dragon Age) stage persuasion, votes, or councils with relevant mechanics?

### Takeaway
Across the surveyed games, three recurring patterns show up wherever a "vote" or "persuade" moment happens: (1) a visible meter or bar that turns a soft social relationship into a hard pass/fail number the player can see moving in real time (CK3's scheme power vs. resistance, Suzerain's negotiating-power bar, Reigns' four faction meters); (2) a currency the player spends to buy the outcome (CK3's hooks, Tropico's embassy favors, Frostpunk 2's Heatstamps bribes); and (3) a persuasion check gated by an investment the player made earlier, not by a dice roll at the table itself (Disco Elysium's skill points shape the odds before the check fires). Mass Effect/Dragon Age's "council/war table" scenes are the weakest fit for a live negotiation model — they resolve as a menu of missions or a single dialogue-morality check, not an ongoing multi-party vote.

### Cited Findings
- Crusader Kings 3: "Schemes" are the primary non-military way to pressure a character (including for political/council goals); every scheme has a Power value, every target has a Resistance value (each built from the schemer's and target's own skills plus their Spymaster), and the scheme's chance to advance each month is based on Power vs. Resistance — [PC Invasion, via search snippet](https://www.pcinvasion.com/crusader-kings-3-intrigue-schemes-hooks-secrets/); a character can normally run one hostile scheme and one personal scheme at once — [same source, via search snippet].
- CK3 "Hooks" are reusable (Strong) or one-time (Weak) obligations/blackmail the player holds over a character, usable to coerce actions from a lopsided marriage acceptance to joining a murder plot; a vassal can spend a hook on their own liege specifically to force a long-term Council seat — [Attack of the Fanboy / gamewatcher, via search snippet](https://attackofthefanboy.com/guides/crusader-kings-3-what-are-schemes-and-hooks/); holding a Strong Hook also blocks the target from taking hostile action (assassination, joining a rebel faction) against the hook-holder — [same cluster, via search snippet].
- A Spymaster's Intrigue skill both raises a scheme's success chance and lowers the chance of the scheme being discovered, and can also be tasked to "Disrupt Schemes" against the player or "Find Secrets" on a target location — [PC Invasion, via search snippet](https://www.pcinvasion.com/crusader-kings-3-intrigue-schemes-hooks-secrets/). ⚠️ I could not confirm the exact on-screen presentation of a scheme/hook interaction (whether it plays as a character-bubble scene or a menu); a WebFetch of PC Gamer's dedicated CK3 intrigue article hit a paywall/signup wall, and a FandomSpot hooks article (fetched in full) explicitly does not describe the UI either.
- Tropico 6: relations with each foreign "Superpower" rise or fall based on completing (or refusing) that power's mission requests; embassies unlock a menu of paid diplomatic actions once relations cross thresholds — Praise ($5,000, +10 relationship, 24-month cooldown), Send Delegation ($2,500, triggers one demand from that Superpower, 12-month cooldown), Ask for Financial Aid (needs 71 relationship, grants $20,000 at a cost of 20 relationship), and Request Alliance (needs 81 relationship, triggers a demand that, if met, prevents Tropico from being conquered by other superpowers) — [Tropico Fandom wiki, via search snippet](https://tropico.fandom.com/wiki/Superpowers).
- Democracy 4: coalition partners periodically make explicit trade offers to the player — "implement/cancel/change THIS policy, and we will give you X political capital" — functioning as a running one-on-one negotiation rather than a single set-piece vote; broader voter groups are shown as colored happiness bars but (per one community report) do not have an equivalent explicit "make me happy or else" demand mechanic — [Steam Community discussion, via search snippet](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1410710/discussions/0/4633736485039795516/) and [Democracy 4 basic gameplay guide, via search snippet](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2242836711).
- Reigns: presents every decision as a swipeable card (an advisor, citizen, or animal with a request), where swipe-right/left is yes/no; every swipe nudges some combination of four faction meters (Church, People, Army, Treasury), and the player loses the run if any single meter hits either extreme (full or empty) — [GamesRadar+, via search snippet](https://www.gamesradar.com/how-reigns-convinced-two-million-people-to-swipe-right-to-rule-their-kingdom-and-captured-the-game-of-thrones-license/); deliberately over-favoring one faction is a viable but risky strategy that can unlock its own high-risk event chains — [same source, via search snippet].
- Disco Elysium: skill checks are resolved as dice rolls against a difficulty rating, gated by two tiers — "White" checks are recoverable (they can reopen later, e.g., after raising the relevant stat or meeting a story condition) and failing one has limited permanent narrative cost; "Red" checks are one-shot, cannot be retried or stat-adjusted in the moment, and are permanent, though a failed Red check can sometimes produce a more interesting/positive outcome than success — [gamers.wiki, via search snippet](https://gamers.wiki/en/games/disco-elysium/guides/how-to-pass-skill-checks-in-disco-elysium-white-red-and-dice-rolls); the number of skill points invested does not gate whether a dialogue/persuasion option even appears, only how likely it is to succeed once attempted — [same source, via search snippet].
- Mass Effect: the series' Paragon/Renegade point totals (accrued from earlier dialogue choices across the whole game, not from a single negotiation scene) determine which of two scripted outcomes plays out in a late-game council-composition decision (an all-human Council vs. a multi-species, human-led Council) — [Mass Effect Fandom wiki, via search snippet](https://masseffect.fandom.com/wiki/Morality). ⚠️ I found no source describing a real-time, multi-party "vote" scene in Mass Effect or Dragon Age comparable to Frostpunk 2's council or a live summit — their "council"/"war table" scenes function as mission-select menus or single binary-outcome checks, not an ongoing negotiation with visible vote counts. This is a genuine gap, not an oversight: searches specifically for Dragon Age's war table returned no negotiation-relevant detail.

### Inferences
- The common through-line for a "persuasion" scene that stays legible to the player is: show a bar/number the audience can watch move (resistance vs. power; negotiating-power bar; faction meters), attach a currency or investment that changes it, and keep the actual accept/reject decision to a single moment rather than a drawn-out haggle. For a ~20-minute run, this argues for a compact per-party "stance" indicator (visible lean, e.g., For/Hesitant/Against) rather than a hidden percentage, so the player can read the room the way they read a Frostpunk 2 delegate breakdown.
- Games that gate persuasion behind investment made earlier (Disco Elysium's skill points, CK3's hooks acquired via prior schemes) make the negotiation feel earned rather than a slot machine; a pacing summit that lets earlier-era choices (e.g., reputation built with a specific lab or government across the run) set the odds going in would fit this pattern and reuses work the player already did.
- None of the surveyed games besides Frostpunk 2 combine a visible multi-faction vote tally with a promise-then-betrayal consequence loop in a single scene — Frostpunk 2 and (per the treaty-detection question below) Civilization's diplomacy AI are the two clearest precedents for the "sign now, get caught later" structure the owner's summit needs.

### Gaps
- This Is the Police and Papers, Please were in the suggested source list but neither surfaced negotiation- or vote-specific mechanics in searches (This Is the Police is a bribery/corruption-management game but not a face-to-face negotiation-with-a-tally game, and Papers, Please is an inspection/interrogation game without a vote or treaty system) — I did not find a defensible reason to include either and have left them out per the assignment's "include only those with relevant negotiation/vote mechanics" instruction, rather than force a weak connection.
- Pentiment was named as a maybe; I did not search it directly because nothing in the initial survey suggested it has a negotiation/vote/summit mechanic (it's primarily an investigation-and-consequence narrative game), and the question flagged it with "Include only those with relevant negotiation/vote mechanics" — flagging this exclusion rather than guessing.
- Dragon Age's war table specifically (as opposed to Mass Effect's council) was not covered by any source I could retrieve — searches returned only generic Mass Effect material. This is a real gap if the report writer wants Dragon Age specifically.

## Where is a treaty or promise signed in one scene later tested, with detection or betrayal reveals?

### Takeaway
Frostpunk 2's Trust/promise system is the clearest, best-documented example of "sign now, get judged later" from this survey: a promise made during Council negotiation is an explicit ticking commitment, and failing to deliver it costs more Trust than never promising, with the Breach of Trust DLC turning this into the central win/lose resource. Classic 4X diplomacy AI (Civilization II/V) implements the same idea at a simpler level — breaking a promise or declaring war on an ally after receiving tribute directly damages a tracked reputation score.

### Cited Findings
- Frostpunk 2: a promise is a live commitment with a deadline; "not following up with that promise in the agreed time span will damage your relationship with that group" — [TheGamer, via search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/frostpunk-2-council-laws-negotiate-complete-guide/); reviews of the Breach of Trust DLC state broken promises damage Trust "far more than refusing requests outright" — [Big Boss Battle](https://bigbossbattle.com/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/) and [CogConnected](https://cogconnected.com/review/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/), both via search snippet.
- Civilization V: the diplomatic AI can promise to go to war alongside the player, ask for time to prepare, and then simply not follow through when the moment comes — a scripted example of an AI "breaking" a cooperative promise — [Civilization Wiki (Fandom), Diplomacy (Civ5), via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Diplomacy_(Civ5)).
- Civilization II: breaking a treaty, or declaring war on a civilization shortly after accepting tribute from it, directly damages the player's/AI's tracked reputation — [Civilization Wiki (Fandom), Diplomacy (Civ2), via search snippet](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Diplomacy_(Civ2)).
- The indie strategy game Airships ties the size of a broken treaty's reputation penalty to how strong the treaty bond was — declaring war on a close ally costs much more reputation than betraying a weak one — [zarkonnen.com, "On Diplomacy," via search snippet](https://zarkonnen.com/airships/on_diplomacy).

### Inferences
- The strongest precedent for the owner's "everyone can secretly break the deal" post-summit beat is Frostpunk 2's asymmetry rule: refusing to commit is cheap, but committing and reneging is expensive. This suggests the AI-lab summit should let labs/governments openly decline specific clauses without a big penalty, while a lab that signs and is later caught cheating should take a bigger reputation/relationship hit than one that never signed at all — that asymmetry is what makes the later "reveal" land as a betrayal rather than a shrug.
- None of the surveyed games stage the "reveal" itself as its own designed scene (a moment where the broken promise is specifically dramatized with characters reacting) — in every case found, the breach shows up as a stat penalty or a changed AI behavior, not a bespoke "confrontation" cutscene. This is a genuine design opening: a pacing-summit sequel scene where an advisor bubble specifically calls out which party broke its word would be a novel beat rather than one borrowed wholesale from a precedent.

### Gaps
- I did not find a game that stages a bespoke "the treaty was broken, here is the reveal scene" moment as a designed set-piece (with characters, dialogue, and a discrete reaction) rather than a background stat change — this may not exist in the surveyed titles, or my searches did not surface it. Total War's diplomacy was mentioned only in passing (a player complaint thread) and not investigated further given the time budget; it is a plausible additional example worth a follow-up search if the report writer needs more than two precedents.

## What makes negotiation/vote scenes tense and fun vs. tedious, per critical reception?

### Takeaway
Reviewers repeatedly single out Frostpunk 2's Council as a genuine source of stress and engagement ("few games have made reviewers feel as stressed"), praising the way political management is made to feel as high-stakes as physical survival — but the same reviews and player discussions flag two concrete tedium risks: promises that silently accumulate into an unmanageable web of obligations, and UI/tutorial gaps that hide the consequences of a choice (e.g., not being able to see what law a new one replaces, or which past promises a repeal would violate) until the player is already several turns deep into a bad decision.

### Cited Findings
- "Frostpunk 2 blends classic city development ideas with an interesting take on political horse-trading. To get a vote passed in the council means assessing the mood of each faction, considering the percentage of the council they hold, and engaging in negotiations; a promise to vote for or against a motion that always comes with conditions attached." — [VideoGamer review, via search snippet](https://www.videogamer.com/reviews/frostpunk-2-review/).
- "Few games have made reviewers feel as stressed [as Frostpunk 2]," and one reviewer, after 30 hours, "wasn't sure it's all that fun, but remained hooked" — managing politics is described as being just as stressful as managing resource scarcity — [search-result synthesis of multiple reviews, via search snippet, exact outlet(s) not individually confirmable from the aggregated summary — see coverage statement].
- Managing faction expectations is called "the most difficult part of Frostpunk 2," and reviewers/players note the tutorial is "too simplistic to effectively teach the deep mechanisms," leaving council mechanics under-explained early on — [search-result synthesis, via search snippet].
- Promises "can easily spiral into a complicated web of agreements"; failing to meet a promise costs favor with the faction, and making too many promises at once limits the player's ability to persuade delegates on future votes (the 1-per-faction/3-total cap noted above is the mechanical reason) — [search-result synthesis, via search snippet].
- Concrete UI complaints from players: not being able to see what law a proposed law would replace, and no clear warning about whether repealing an existing law would break an earlier promise tied to it — [search-result synthesis of player/review commentary, via search snippet].

### Inferences
- The tension reviewers credit to Frostpunk 2's council comes specifically from visible stakes (you can see the vote count and the clock) combined with a scarce resource (limited simultaneous promises) — remove either half and the scene likely becomes either a non-event (no visible stakes) or a spreadsheet chore (unlimited bribing). For a one-shot, ~20-minute pacing summit, this argues for showing the player a clear "who's leaning which way" readout and hard-capping how many separate deals they can strike in the scene, rather than letting them satisfy every party.
- The recurring tedium complaint is not about the negotiation choice itself but about hidden bookkeeping (unclear what a promise conflicts with, unclear what a repeal breaks) — the actionable lesson is to make every commitment's downstream consequence visible at the moment the player makes it, not buried in a systems screen they have to cross-reference later.

### Gaps
- I could not pin these tension/tedium quotes to a specific single outlet with full confidence — several came back as WebSearch's own cross-review synthesis rather than a quote clearly attributed and read in full from one article page. I attempted to open individual review pages (Twinfinite, gamepressure) to verify sourcing and both returned HTTP 403. Treat the "tense vs. tedious" quotes above as directionally reliable (multiple independent review outlets were returned across two separate searches making the same points) but not verbatim-verified against a single fully-read article.
- I did not find dedicated critical commentary on Suzerain's or CK3's negotiation pacing (tense vs. tedious) — the search budget was prioritized toward Frostpunk 2 per the assignment's emphasis, and this is a gap for those two titles specifically.
