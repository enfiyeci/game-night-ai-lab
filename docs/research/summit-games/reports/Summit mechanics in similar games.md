# Monitoring, not trust, catches the cheater

Across strategy games, narrative political games, tabletop games, and the small handful of games built specifically to simulate AI governance, one pattern shows up again and again: a negotiation scene is only tense if the audience can *see* who is leaning which way and what it costs to move them, a pledge only means something once it is backed by something tangible (a token, a resource, a signature), and — most relevant to this project — **no shipped, played game was found that already implements "detection odds scale with how much monitoring was agreed," the exact mechanic the game-night team wants for era 5**. The closest real precedent is the tabletop wargame *Intelligence Rising*, whose facilitators report that after any negotiated AI treaty they privately ask each side whether it complied or cheated, and that "ease of defection is indicated based on the amount of effort that went into a verification regime" — almost exactly the brief's ask, but published only as a qualitative description, not a rulebook with numbers ([Strategic Insights from Simulation Gaming of AI Race Dynamics, arXiv:2410.03092](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)). The actual math for "how much monitoring buys how much detection" comes not from a game but from decades of academic "inspection game" theory built for real arms control, which treats detection as a probability both sides reason about, not a certainty — and which includes the counterintuitive finding that *better* verification can sometimes be used *less* often, because its mere existence deters cheating (search-summarized inspection-game literature, [Applications of Inspection Games](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233238989_Applications_of_Inspection_games); ⚠️ the underlying academic PDF itself could not be opened — see coverage section). Four other design patterns recur widely enough across genres to be treated as load-bearing precedent rather than one-off flourishes: a visible "who's leaning which way" tally before any vote (Frostpunk 2, Civilization); a hard split between binding commitments and cheap talk (Twilight Imperium, Cosmic Encounter, Sheriff of Nottingham); an asymmetry where refusing a deal is cheap but signing and later breaking it is expensive (Frostpunk 2, Stellaris); and a split between "the deal broke" (public, loud) and "who broke it" (hidden, monitoring-gated) (The Resistance, Civilization VI). The sections below walk through each pattern with its sources, then close with six concrete mechanics for the era-5 summit, each tied to the specific game it borrows from.

## A summit reads as tense only when the room's leanings are visible and spending money moves them

The single clearest, best-documented negotiation scene in this survey is Frostpunk 2's Council: a 100-delegate chamber split across factions, where before any vote the game shows each faction's size and current lean — For, Against, or Hesitant — so the player can read exactly how close a law is to passing and which faction is worth working on ([Twinfinite, via search snippet](https://twinfinite.net/guides/frostpunk-2-council-voting-explained-how-to-pass-every-vote-negotiate-more/); corroborated by [Frostpunk 2 Wiki](https://frostpunk-2.game-vault.net/wiki/Council)). The player then picks one faction and offers exactly one "promise card" — research a specific technology, build or demolish something specific, take action against a rival party — and is capped to one active promise per faction and three total across the whole council, a limit reviewers and players credit as the thing that keeps the scene from turning into "just buy every vote" ([TheGamer, via search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/frostpunk-2-council-laws-negotiate-complete-guide/)). The game's own director frames the council as "a space for the conflicting values to directly clash," built around "negotiating a common future" rather than parliamentary technicalities — a framing this project's summit, with only three commitments and six named parties, can match without needing a full simulation of a legislature ([Game Developer interview with Jakub Stokalski, read in full](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/frostpunk-2-s-developers-didn-t-want-it-to-be-a-jackass-simulator-)). Civilization's World Congress does the same "see the room" job with a currency instead of a promise slot: civilizations spend a resource called Diplomatic Favor both to buy extra votes on a resolution and to win tiebreaks, so how much a party cares about an outcome is expressed as a spend, not just a stance ([World Congress — Civilopedia, read in full](https://www.civilopedia.net/en-US/gathering-storm/concepts/world_congress/)). Stellaris goes further and replaces "one member, one vote" with a continuous "diplomatic weight" built from a civilization's economy, fleet, technology, and population, so bigger, richer, more advanced empires simply carry more weight in the room by default — a detail worth noting given that this project's four labs and two governments are explicitly unequal in power ([Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, read in full](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)).

Suzerain adds the piece Frostpunk 2 and Civilization both leave out: named, specific things each party cares about, instead of one generic meter. Its foreign leaders are not "more or less friendly" sliders; each has one or two concrete, story-specific pressure points — one leader will walk away from the table entirely if not given everything he asks for on a named joint operation, another can be poisoned against the player by an unrelated action like buying up a contested resource field ([Neoseeker Geopolitics guide, via search snippet](https://www.neoseeker.com/suzerain/guides/Geopolitics)). Civilization VI's leader "Agendas" formalize this into a system: every leader has one *visible* historical agenda, revealed on first contact, and one or more *hidden* agendas that must be uncovered through espionage, and AI opinion of the player quietly tracks alignment with both even before the hidden one is uncovered ([Civilization Fandom, via search snippet](https://www.fandom.com/articles/1281-why-civ-6-leader-agendas-mean-youll-never-play-the)). Stellaris player forums supply the cautionary counter-case: when a faction's votes look arbitrary rather than traceable to a legible motive, players call the whole council system "bland," "meaningless," and complain the AI "votes make no sense" ([Steam Community discussions, via search snippet](https://steamcommunity.com/app/281990/discussions/0/3887226763719189927/)). Taken together, this argues for giving OpenBrain, Lodestar, DeepThink, Qilin, the West, and the East each one or two named, concrete asks or red lines — "OpenBrain won't accept a training-run cap without a compute-parity guarantee," say — rather than a single hidden trust number, so a lab's reaction to a proposed commitment reads as a *reason*, not a random number. One important gap: the Frostpunk 2 and Suzerain sources that would show exactly how such a scene looks on screen — camera framing, whether multiple leaders' portraits appear together, whether named characters speak in dialogue bubbles — could not be opened; two of the most relevant pages returned "page not found" errors on every attempt, so this stays an open question for the game's own UI design rather than a confirmed answer from precedent (⚠️ flagged in the coverage section below).

## A pledge only counts once it costs something real — everything else is free to break

Three unrelated tabletop games independently converge on the same rule: spoken promises are worthless as game objects, and only a pledge backed by something tangible is enforced by the rules. Twilight Imperium 4 states this explicitly — "if the terms of a deal cannot be resolved immediately, it is a non-binding deal," and "when a deal is non-binding, a player does not have to adhere to any part of the agreement" — and gives players a scarce supply of physical "promissory notes" as the one instrument that actually transfers and locks in an obligation ([Twilight Imperium Wiki, via search snippet](https://twilight-imperium.fandom.com/wiki/Transactions_%26_Deals); ⚠️ this specific page returned an access error and is sourced from a search snippet, not a full read). Cosmic Encounter has almost the identical rule for its high-stakes "Negotiate" card: "players may also offer promises and other non-tangible things, but such actions are never binding and are not considered part of the deal — there must be at least one granted colony or traded card for a deal to be successful" ([Cosmic Encounter Wiki, via search snippet](https://cosmicencounter.fandom.com/wiki/Negotiate_card)). Sheriff of Nottingham, a game built entirely around a border-crossing merchant who may lie about what is in their bag, has the same split baked into its bribery rule: the Sheriff may accept a bribe of gold, goods, or "future favors" in exchange for letting a bag through unopened, but "agreements are binding except for future promises and goods not actually in the bag" — only what physically changes hands at the table is enforceable ([Sheriff of Nottingham rules, read in full](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/sheriff-of-nottingham/game-rules.php)). Frostpunk 2 supplies the matching consequence side of this rule: a promise made during council negotiation is a live commitment with a deadline, and failing to deliver on it damages Trust "far more than refusing requests outright" — refusing is a safe no, but signing and reneging is the expensive move ([TheGamer, via search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/frostpunk-2-council-laws-negotiate-complete-guide/); Breach of Trust DLC reviews at [Big Boss Battle](https://bigbossbattle.com/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/) and [CogConnected](https://cogconnected.com/review/frostpunk-2-breach-of-trust-review/), both via search snippet).

The design lesson generalizes cleanly to the era-5 summit: the three commitments on the table (outside testers, a training-run cap, a launch-delay window, pooled safety research, no AI doing AI research, the West–East inspection line) should each require an actual tangible cost to sign — funding testers, staffing an inspection line, opening a facility to audits — rather than a verbal yes, and a lab or government that declines outright should take little or no reputational hit for it. Twilight Imperium adds one more transferable constraint worth naming: it caps side-deals to "one transaction per pair, per agenda," which forces a player negotiating with several parties at once to prioritize rather than lobby everyone simultaneously ([Galactic Council, UltraBoardGames, read in full](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/twilight-imperium/galactic-council.php)) — a useful pacing lever for a scene with six other parties and a fixed slice of a 20-minute run to spend on it.

## Detection should be a probability the monitoring level sets, not a certainty anyone can rely on

The clearest existing model for "how much monitoring a summit agrees to should change the odds of catching a later cheater" comes from *Intelligence Rising*, a wargame built specifically around AI-race dynamics where two states and two tech firms negotiate and can defect on treaties. Its facilitators, reporting on 43 games run over four years, describe the mechanic directly: after any negotiated agreement, "the facilitator asks each party in secret to indicate whether they comply with the treaty or attempt to defect (ease of defection is indicated based on the amount of effort that went into a verification regime)," and even then, "usually there is at least one defection, from a team with significant AI R&D resources" ([Strategic Insights from Simulation Gaming of AI Race Dynamics, arXiv:2410.03092, read in full as a rendered PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)). This is the single most directly transferable finding in the whole research pass, but it comes with a real limit: the paper describes the mechanic only qualitatively — it does not publish the dice-roll table, threshold, or formula behind "ease of defection," and the game's own rulebook is not public, so the exact numbers behind this design would have to be invented rather than copied (⚠️ noted explicitly as a gap in the source notes). The same paper's broader finding matters for pacing the post-summit weeks: even a well-verified deal, with no successful defection by any party, still ends with "a dice roll... typically still required," meaning the designers deliberately never let strong verification fully remove uncertainty about the outcome.

The formal academic backing for this comes from "inspection games," a branch of game theory built explicitly for arms-control verification since the 1960s, which models an inspector and an inspectee as two rational players where the inspectee's incentive to cheat depends on the expected cost of getting caught, not on certainty of being caught (search-summarized: [Applications of Inspection Games](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233238989_Applications_of_Inspection_games); ⚠️ the primary academic source for this literature could not be opened in this research pass — an SSL error blocked the main PDF and a Wikipedia article on the topic returned a 404 — so this is the weakest-sourced section of the underlying notes and should be read as directionally reliable, not verbatim-confirmed). One specific, useful finding from that literature is a documented paradox: better verification technology can be used *less* often, because its deterrent value alone does the work — this argues against a design where high monitoring means "you must run an active check every single week," and toward a design where high monitoring quietly lowers the odds a rival even *attempts* to cheat, not just the odds they get caught. A related, more game-shaped precedent is Sheriff of Nottingham's inspection rule, which is symmetric by design: if the Sheriff inspects a bag and the merchant was telling the truth, the Sheriff has to pay the merchant a real penalty — wrongly accusing an honest party costs the accuser something, not just the accused ([Sheriff of Nottingham rules, read in full](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/sheriff-of-nottingham/game-rules.php)). Civilization VI's "Emergency" system supplies the closest 4X-genre analogue to a probabilistic detection gate: a provocative act (conquering a city-state, a nuclear strike) does not automatically trigger a vote — "there's still a dice roll" afterward, so not every violation becomes actionable ([Emergency (Civ6) — Fandom, via search snippet, corroborated by a full Civilopedia fetch](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Emergency_(Civ6))). Stellaris supplies a useful framing for the *mechanism* of detection rather than just its odds: its spy-network system models infiltration as a duel between one side's concealment stat and the other's investigative stat, meaning "did the cheater get caught" is a contest between two numbers, not a flat percentage ([Galactic Community and Intelligence — Stellaris Wiki, read in full and via search snippet respectively](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Intelligence)). Notably, the research explicitly did not find any 4X or tabletop game that already ties a specific monitoring *investment* to a specific detection *probability* the way this project wants — Stellaris's own law-breach detection turns out to be automatic and state-based (the game already knows every empire's policies, so "breach" is simply checked, not investigated), which is the opposite of the hidden, monitoring-gated detection this project needs. That absence is worth stating plainly: this part of the era-5 design is a genuine contribution, not a straightforward port from an existing game.

## After the deal breaks, split "something broke" from "who broke it," and let capability drive temptation

Several games separate the *fact* that a cooperative deal failed from the *attribution* of blame, and that split is directly useful for a game that wants weekly advisor warnings to surface before the culprit is confirmed. The clearest example is The Resistance: a sabotaged mission fails visibly and immediately (everyone sees the mission failed), but which specific team member submitted the sabotage card stays hidden among the whole team unless a separate mechanism — the Spy identity being confirmed elsewhere — reveals it ([The Resistance (game), Wikipedia, via search snippet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Resistance_(game))). This maps cleanly onto "a rival lab breaks the deal": the break itself (a training run exceeding the cap) can be public and immediate, while the identity of the breaker is confirmed only once the weekly monitoring check resolves. Stellaris shows the same idea from the punishment side, with two separate levers rather than one: the instant a member breaches a passed law, "all other members will lose 100 opinion with the breaching empire" — a universal, automatic social penalty — while separately, any Sanction resolutions targeting law-breakers apply their own structural consequences on top ([Galactic Community — Stellaris Wiki, read in full](https://stellaris.paradoxwikis.com/Galactic_community)). Frostpunk 2's asymmetry rule from the previous section applies again here directly: because breaking a signed commitment costs more Trust than never signing it, the design already has a built-in reason for a caught cheater to suffer more than a party that was honest about not committing in the first place.

Two further precedents suggest how to make the temptation to cheat feel motivated rather than arbitrary. Intelligence Rising's facilitators report that defection is concentrated among "a team with significant AI R&D resources" — the strongest actor is usually the one who breaks the deal, because it has the most to gain from cheating and the most capability to hide it ([arXiv:2410.03092, read in full](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03092)). Pax Pamir contributes a detection-independent deterrent worth layering on top of a pure detection-probability system: switching loyalty away from an accumulated coalition costs a player everything they had invested in it — gifts, prizes, standing patriots — regardless of whether anyone "catches" the switch ([Pax Pamir loyalty mechanic, via search snippet](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/pax-pamir/game-elements.php)). Applied to era 5, this suggests a lab currently leading on capability should feel the strongest pull to break the training-run cap first, and that breaking the deal should cost a lab some of whatever benefit it had been getting from being seen as compliant (pooled research access, goodwill with a government partner), independent of whether the break is ever detected. Finally, Twilight Struggle's DEFCON track is worth naming as a pacing device rather than a detection mechanic: a single shared, visible number that both sides push and that ends the game outright if it bottoms out gives players a reason to care about the aggregate state of the world even before any single violation is confirmed ([Twilight Struggle, Wikipedia, via search snippet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight_Struggle)) — a useful shape for a running "how close is the deal to collapsing" indicator across the three post-summit weeks, separate from any individual party's guilt.

## Six summit-mechanic ideas for era 5

1. **Split "a break happened" from "who did it," and reveal them on different clocks.** The training-run cap gets breached, and an advisor warning about a suspicious run surfaces immediately and publicly the week it happens — but which lab caused it (including possibly the player) stays unconfirmed until the weekly monitoring check resolves. Borrows from *The Resistance*'s public mission-failure/hidden-culprit split, *Civilization VI*'s post-violation "dice roll before it becomes an actionable event," and *Intelligence Rising*'s private, per-party defection check.

2. **Let the summit's monitoring commitments set a detection-probability tier, not a guarantee, and let strong monitoring mean fewer active checks, not more.** Outside testers, the compute cap, and the inspection line each raise the odds a break is caught on the weekly check, but even the fully-monitored deal should leave real uncertainty, and a strongly monitored world can plausibly need *fewer* dramatic "we caught you" beats because the threat alone deters some attempts. Borrows from *Intelligence Rising*'s "ease of defection tied to verification effort" mechanic and the inspection-game-theory finding that better verification tech can be used less often because deterrence substitutes for active checking.

3. **Make a pledge mechanically real only if it's backed by something the player actually pays for.** A lab or government agreeing to outside testers or the inspection line should require the player to spend a resource (staff, funding, a turn of setup) to make that commitment enforced; anything agreed to but never funded stays cheap talk that can be walked back without a penalty. Borrows from *Twilight Imperium*'s binding-vs-non-binding deal rule and its promissory-note system, *Cosmic Encounter*'s rule that only a tangible transfer (never a spoken promise) counts as part of a deal, and *Sheriff of Nottingham*'s split between a bribe paid now and an unenforceable "future favor."

4. **Make refusing a commitment cheap, but make signing and later breaking it expensive.** A lab that never agrees to the training-run cap should take little reputational damage for saying no; a lab that signs and is later caught exceeding it should take a bigger hit than either the honest refuser or an undetected cheater who was never caught. Borrows from *Frostpunk 2*'s Trust rule, where a broken promise costs more than a refusal, and *Stellaris*'s instant universal opinion penalty the moment a signed law is breached.

5. **Give the leading lab the strongest temptation to break the deal first, and the biggest reward if it gets away with it.** Whichever of OpenBrain, Lodestar, DeepThink, or Qilin currently has the capability lead should have the most to gain from a training run past the cap and be the most likely candidate to defect, mirroring what actually happens at the table. Borrows from *Intelligence Rising*'s documented pattern that defectors are "usually a team with significant AI R&D resources."

6. **Make monitoring symmetric — it should have a real chance of flagging the player's own lab too, and a false accusation should cost something.** The same weekly check that can catch a rival lab exceeding the training-run cap should be able to flag the player's own studio if the player pushes a run past the line, and if monitoring wrongly accuses a compliant party, that costs the accuser standing rather than being a free look. Borrows from *Sheriff of Nottingham*'s rule that a wrongful inspection costs the inspector a real, fixed penalty, and from the inspection-game-theory framing that treats the inspector and the inspectee as symmetric, interchangeable roles under the same verification scheme.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframe from this research is that "summit," "signature," and "getting caught" are three separate design problems that different genres have already solved separately, and the era-5 mechanic just needs to chain them together rather than invent all three at once: Frostpunk 2 and Civilization solve legibility (show the room, show the stances), Twilight Imperium and Cosmic Encounter solve commitment (only a tangible pledge counts), and Intelligence Rising plus the inspection-game literature solve detection (verification effort sets odds, not certainty). No surveyed game — 4X, narrative, tabletop, or AI-governance-specific — already wires all three together into "sign a commitment, then have its funding level set your odds of catching a cheater weeks later," which means the era-5 pacing summit, if built along the six lines above, would be doing something genuinely novel rather than reskinning an existing system; it should be treated as original design informed by precedent, not a port of one.
